## 17. Special Diets and Restrictions: Vegetarian/Vegan, Gluten-Free, and Food Allergies

**Bottom line:** Any restriction is only as healthy as the planning behind it — a planned vegan diet is excellent (B12 is non-negotiable), celiac/allergy exclusions are absolute, but never self-impose a restriction without a diagnosis, because every food group you cut is nutrition you may not replace.


Some diets are chosen; others are imposed by biology. A vegetarian eats by conviction; a person with celiac disease or a peanut allergy has no choice at all. The two get lumped together as "special diets," but they sit at opposite ends of a spectrum, and the most important rule cuts across both: **restriction is only as healthy as the planning behind it.** A well-built plant-based diet is one of the healthiest patterns we know of; a poorly built one — or a needlessly restrictive one adopted on a hunch — narrows nutrition for no benefit.

The single biggest mistake in this whole territory is **self-imposing a restriction without a diagnosis.** Cutting out gluten, dairy, or whole food groups because they feel "inflammatory" usually does nothing but shrink the variety that protects you, raise your grocery bill, and (in the gluten case) sabotage the test that could have told you the truth. Diagnose first, restrict second.

The other recurring theme is that almost any restriction *can* be made healthful with attention. Vegan, gluten-free for celiac disease, allergen-avoidant, halal, kosher, Ramadan-fasting — none of these is inherently deficient. What matters is whether the gaps each one creates get filled.

### Key evidence

**Vegetarian and vegan diets are nutritionally adequate for all life stages — when planned (strong, with caveats).** The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics' position is unambiguous: "appropriately planned vegetarian, including vegan, diets are healthful, nutritionally adequate, and may provide health benefits for the prevention and treatment of certain diseases," and are appropriate "for all stages of the life cycle, including pregnancy, lactation, infancy, childhood, adolescence, older adulthood, and for athletes" [Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics 2016](https://www.jandonline.org/article/S2212-2672(16)31192-3/abstract). The benefits are real and measured. A meta-analysis of 30 randomized trials found vegetarian and vegan diets lowered total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and apoB versus omnivorous diets [Koch et al., Eur Heart J 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37226630/), and Stanford's identical-twin trial saw LDL fall further on a vegan diet (from ~110.7 to 95.5 mg/dL) alongside lower insulin and body weight [Stanford Medicine 2023](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2023/11/twin-diet-vegan-cardiovascular.html). Vegetarians and vegans also tend to have lower BMI, blood pressure, and rates of type 2 diabetes and ischemic heart disease.

But "appropriately planned" is load-bearing. The non-negotiable gap is **vitamin B12**: there is no reliable plant source, deficiency causes irreversible nerve damage and anemia, and every vegan (and most vegetarians) must use a supplement or reliably fortified foods — this is not optional [NIH ODS, B12](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/) (see §12).

**Adjust it (B12 dose).** The RDA is only **2.4 mcg/day**, but you cannot dose a supplement at the RDA, because intrinsic-factor–mediated absorption saturates at **~1.5–2 mcg per dose** and the passive route that handles the rest absorbs only **~1%** — at a single 500 mcg tablet about **2%** is absorbed, and at 1,000 mcg about **1.3%** [NIH ODS, B12](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/). Two equivalent regimens cover the gap: (a) a **daily** cyanocobalamin dose of **50–100 mcg** (frequent small doses use the saturable, efficient route), or (b) an **infrequent large** dose of **1,000 mcg taken 2–3×/week** (relies on the ~1% passive route — 1,000 mcg × ~1.3% ≈ 13 mcg absorbed per dose, ample). Scale it to your situation: **fortified foods reliably eaten** (e.g., 2–3 servings/day of fortified plant milk or nutritional yeast at ~1–2.5 mcg/serving) can substitute for a low-dose supplement but rarely replace it entirely; **age >50 or low stomach acid / metformin / PPIs** → favor the supplement over food-bound B12 and stay at the high end; **established deficiency** → clinician-directed **1,000 mcg/day** (or intramuscular) until repleted, then maintenance; **pregnancy/lactation** → don't drop below daily 50 mcg, since fetal/infant stores depend on maternal intake. (See §12.) The other nutrients to actively manage:

- **Iron** — plant (non-heme) iron is far less bioavailable; the NIH sets the iron requirement **1.8× higher** for vegetarians, and pairing iron-rich foods with vitamin C meaningfully boosts absorption while tea/coffee tannins blunt it [NIH ODS, Iron](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/).
  - **Adjust it (iron target).** Baseline RDA × **1.8** for any vegetarian/vegan: **men 19+ and women 51+** 8 mg → **~14 mg/day**; **menstruating women 19–50** 18 mg → **~32 mg/day**; **pregnancy** 27 mg → **~49 mg/day**. Modifiers with numbers: pair each iron-rich meal with **~50–100 mg vitamin C** (e.g., one orange or 1/2 cup bell pepper) to multiply non-heme absorption roughly **2–3×**; separate **tea/coffee by ≥1 hour** (tannins cut absorption up to ~60%); heavy menstrual loss or diagnosed deficiency → clinician may add **~30–65 mg elemental iron** as a supplement (don't self-prescribe high doses — excess iron is harmful). Confirm status with **ferritin** rather than guessing.
- **Zinc** — also less bioavailable from plants (phytates bind it); favor legumes, seeds, and whole grains, and soak/sprout/ferment them to cut phytate [Hunt, AJCN 2003](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0002916522033652).
  - **Adjust it (zinc target).** The IOM judges that zinc requirements may be **up to 50% greater** for vegetarians whose staples are grains and legumes (dietary phytate:zinc molar ratio >15:1) [IOM DRI, Zinc](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK222317/). So scale the RDA by up to **1.5×**: **men** 11 mg → **~16.5 mg/day**; **women** 8 mg → **~12 mg/day**; **pregnancy** 11 mg → **~16.5 mg/day**; **lactation** 12 mg → **~18 mg/day**. The multiplier shrinks toward 1.0× as you lower the phytate:zinc ratio — soaking/sprouting legumes and grains and leavening bread (yeast fermentation) can roughly halve phytate, so a mixed plant diet with such prep sits nearer **1.2–1.3×**.
- **Calcium, vitamin D, iodine** — at risk on vegan diets; use fortified plant milks, consider supplements, and don't assume non-iodized salt covers iodine [NIH ODS, Iodine](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-HealthProfessional/).
  - **Adjust it (calcium).** Baseline RDA **1,000 mg/day** for adults 19–50 and men 51–70; **1,200 mg/day** for women 51+ and everyone 71+ [NIH ODS, Calcium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/). Practical fill: most calcium-fortified plant milks supply **~300 mg per cup** (check the label — ranges ~120–450 mg), so 2–3 cups/day plus calcium-set tofu (~200–400 mg/100 g), fortified juice, and low-oxalate greens (kale, bok choy) usually reaches target without pills. Modifiers: **pregnancy/lactation** stays at the age-band RDA (no increase 19–50); **postmenopausal or low bone density** → hold the full 1,200 mg and pair with adequate D; keep total intake **<2,000–2,500 mg/day** (the UL).
  - **Adjust it (vitamin D).** RDA **15 mcg (600 IU)/day** ages 1–70, **20 mcg (800 IU)/day** if >70, targeting serum 25(OH)D **≥50 nmol/L (≥20 ng/mL)** [NIH ODS, Vitamin D](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/). This is not vegan-specific — it scales with **sun exposure and skin tone, not diet**: little midday sun, higher latitude (>~37°), darker skin, obesity, or winter → supplement at the RDA or higher and verify by blood test; reliable regular sun on bare skin may cover it. Vegans should choose **vitamin D3 from lichen** (most D3 is animal-derived) or D2. Don't exceed the **UL of 100 mcg (4,000 IU)/day** without a measured deficiency and clinician guidance.
  - **Adjust it (iodine).** RDA **150 mcg/day** for adults, **220 mcg/day** in pregnancy, **290 mcg/day** in lactation [NIH ODS, Iodine](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-HealthProfessional/). Vegan diets are a notable gap because dairy and fish are major iodine sources. Fill from **iodized salt** (~45 mcg iodine per gram of salt, so a 1/4 tsp ≈ ~70 mcg — about half a day's need) or a supplement; do **not** rely on sea salt, pink/Himalayan salt, or unspecified plant milks, which are unreliable. Caution the other way: **kelp/seaweed** can deliver thousands of mcg and push you past the **UL of 1,100 mcg/day**, so treat it as a supplement, not a free seasoning. Pregnancy/lactation: a supplement providing **~150 mcg** is widely advised given the higher targets.
- **Omega-3 (EPA/DHA)** — plant ALA converts poorly (only a few percent of ALA becomes EPA and <1% becomes DHA); an **algal DHA/EPA** supplement is the clean fix, and is specifically advised in pregnancy/lactation for those who don't eat fish (see §12).
  - **Adjust it (algal omega-3).** Baseline **~250 mg/day combined EPA+DHA** for general adults (the common cardiovascular intake target), best met with **~200–300 mg of preformed algal DHA** since plant ALA won't reliably top it up. Modifiers with numbers: **pregnancy/lactation** → add **~100–200 mg DHA/day on top**, i.e., aim **~300 mg DHA** total (ISSFAL); **high triglycerides or clinician-directed cardiac use** → much higher pharmacologic doses (1–4 g) under supervision. Keep total EPA+DHA **≤3 g/day**, with **≤2 g/day from supplements** (FDA). Background ALA still matters: **~1.1 g/day (women) to 1.6 g/day (men)** from flax, chia, walnuts, or canola supports the small endogenous conversion.
- **Protein** — adequate but requires variety across legumes, soy, grains, nuts, and seeds to cover all essential amino acids over the day.
  - **Adjust it (plant protein).** Use the canonical targets from §02/§20 — RDA **0.8 g/kg/day**, with **~1.2–1.6 g/kg** for active people and older adults — but because plant protein is somewhat less digestible (lower DIAAS) and lower in lysine, a practical rule is to aim toward the **upper end (+~10%)** of whatever band §02 gives you, and to **anchor lysine** with **legumes/soy at most meals** (soy and lentils are lysine-rich; grains and nuts are not). Numeric handles: **~0.4 g protein/kg per meal** across 3–4 meals to maximize muscle protein synthesis; **age >65 → ~1.2–1.5 g/kg/day** to counter anabolic resistance; **endurance/strength training → 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day**; **CKD without dialysis → lower, ~0.6–0.8 g/kg** per §09, not higher. "Complementary proteins" need only balance over the **whole day**, not the same meal.

Extra care applies to **infants, children, and pregnancy**, where the margin for error is thinner — these groups should be planned with a clinician or dietitian rather than improvised.

**Gluten-free is a medical treatment, not a wellness diet (strong).** A strict gluten-free diet is the only treatment for **celiac disease** (~1% of the population), an autoimmune condition where gluten damages the small intestine; it is also required for **wheat allergy** and helps a subset with **non-celiac gluten sensitivity** [AAFP/ACG 2024](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2024/0100/practice-guidelines-celiac-disease.html). For everyone else, the evidence does not support a benefit: a review of gluten avoidance in non-celiac patients concluded that "no nutritional advantage was found for gluten-free foods" and that most people on a gluten-free diet "do not have a physiologic requirement for the diet and likely do not derive substantial benefit" [Niland & Cash, Gastroenterol Hepatol 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5866307/). Commercial gluten-free products are frequently **lower in fiber and protein and higher in fat, salt, and (in some categories) sugar** than their wheat counterparts [Fry et al., J Hum Nutr Diet 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28851025/) — and cost markedly more (averaging ~159% more in the UK, and ranging from 46% to 443% more across categories on the Norwegian market) [Myhrstad et al., Food Nutr Res 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8009084/). The critical procedural point: **get tested while still eating gluten.** Celiac serology (tissue transglutaminase IgA) requires active gluten consumption to be accurate; remove gluten first and you may force an unnecessary, uncomfortable "gluten challenge" later to confirm the diagnosis [ACG 2024](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2024/0100/practice-guidelines-celiac-disease.html). (For gut symptoms without celiac disease, see the low-FODMAP approach in §09.)

**Adjust it (how strict, and for whom).** Strictness scales with the diagnosis, not preference:
- **Confirmed celiac disease →** lifelong, near-zero gluten. The dose-response evidence puts the daily threshold for mucosal harm between **10 and 50 mg/day**: **50 mg/day** for 3 months damages the intestine in most patients, while **10 mg/day** does not [Catassi et al., AJCN 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17616789/). Practically that means **"gluten-free"–labeled foods (<20 ppm gluten, the FDA/Codex limit)** [FDA gluten-free rule](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/questions-and-answers-gluten-free-food-labeling-final-rule), plus managing cross-contact (separate toaster, shared-fryer awareness) — a few slices of regular bread (~2–4 g gluten) is orders of magnitude over the limit.
- **Wheat allergy →** avoid wheat protein specifically (IgE-mediated), but other gluten grains (barley, rye) may be fine — this is a different restriction than celiac.
- **Non-celiac gluten sensitivity →** no validated threshold; many tolerate small amounts, and the trigger is often FODMAPs rather than gluten (see "Unsettled," below). Trial a **structured reduction** rather than zero-tolerance.
- **Pre-test (any of the above suspected) →** keep eating gluten until testing is done. If you've **already gone gluten-free**, a formal gluten challenge means resuming **≥3 g gluten/day (≈1.5–2 slices of wheat bread) for at least 6–8 weeks** (guidelines span 6–12) before serology/biopsy so the test isn't falsely negative — which is exactly why removing gluten *before* testing is the costly mistake [ACG 2024](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2024/0100/practice-guidelines-celiac-disease.html).
- **No diagnosis →** there is no threshold to manage, because there is no benefit to manage it for.

**Food allergies: immune, potentially life-threatening, and distinct from intolerance (strong).** A true food allergy is an IgE-mediated immune response — reacting within minutes, capable of anaphylaxis — and affects roughly **1 in 10 U.S. adults and ~1 in 12 children** by population survey [Gupta et al., epidemiology review, PMC 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7883751/). This is fundamentally different from an **intolerance** (e.g., lactose intolerance, an enzyme shortfall affecting a large share of adults), which causes discomfort but is not immune-mediated and is not dangerous. The U.S. recognizes **nine major allergens** requiring labeling: milk, egg, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and — since the FASTER Act took effect January 2023 — **sesame** [FDA 2023](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-allergies/faster-act-sesame-ninth-major-food-allergen). Management rests on label-reading, avoiding cross-contamination, and carrying **epinephrine** as the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis (antihistamines do not treat it). Two developments are reshaping the field: **early introduction prevents allergy** — the LEAP trial cut peanut allergy by **81%** (overall intention-to-treat: 17.2% with avoidance vs 3.2% with consumption; 13.7% → 1.9% in the larger skin-prick-negative stratum) by feeding peanut to high-risk infants enrolled at **4–11 months** rather than avoiding it [Du Toit et al., NEJM 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25705822/) (funded by NIAID/NIH, with partial support from the National Peanut Board) (see §16) — and **oral immunotherapy** now exists: Palforzia, FDA-approved in 2020 for peanut-allergic children, *desensitizes* but does not cure, so epinephrine is still required [PALISADE, NEJM 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30449234/) (industry-funded: Aimmune Therapeutics, the manufacturer).

**Adjust it (epinephrine and avoidance).** Avoidance of a true IgE-mediated allergen is **absolute** — there is no safe dose to titrate, so this is the one "recommendation" with no scaling: zero exposure. What you *do* scale is the rescue plan. Carry **two** epinephrine auto-injectors, not one, because **~10–20% of anaphylaxis episodes need a second dose**; give the second **5–15 minutes** after the first if symptoms don't improve or worsen. Dose follows weight (the prescribed device, not you, fixes this): an **0.1 mg** infant device exists for **7.5–15 kg**, **0.15 mg** ("Jr") for roughly **7.5/15–25 kg**, **0.3 mg** for **≥25–30 kg**, and **0.5 mg** for larger adults — your allergist sets the right one. After any epinephrine use, **seek emergency care** (biphasic reactions can recur hours later). Antihistamines and inhalers are adjuncts only — they do **not** substitute for epinephrine.

**Religious and ethical patterns can all be healthy (moderate).** Halal, kosher, Buddhist/Hindu and Jain vegetarianism, and the fasting of Ramadan (and other traditions) impose structure rather than deficiency. Each can be built to meet needs — plant-based religious vegetarianism needs the same B12/iron/omega-3 attention as any vegan diet, and Ramadan benefits from hydration and balanced pre-dawn/post-sunset meals (intermittent fasting is covered in §11). Structure is not the same as inadequacy.

**Adjust it (Ramadan).** Hit your full day's calorie and protein targets (§20/§02) inside the eating window — don't under-eat. Spread fluid intake to **~2–3 L** across iftar to suhoor (more in hot climates or with heavy sweating; see the hydration math in §07); make **suhoor** a low-glycemic, protein-containing meal (e.g., **~25–40 g protein** plus slow carbs and fat) to blunt daytime hunger, and break the fast at **iftar** with water and a moderate first course rather than a single large load. Modifiers: **diabetes on insulin/sulfonylureas, pregnancy, or chronic illness** → individualized medical clearance and dose adjustment before fasting, as hypoglycemia and dehydration risk rise.

### What it depends on

- **Diagnosis vs. preference.** A medically required restriction (celiac, IgE allergy) is non-negotiable and precise. A chosen restriction (vegan, religious) is a design problem — solvable with planning.
- **How strict.** Vegan demands more supplementation (B12 always, often D/iodine/omega-3) than lacto-ovo vegetarian, which may still get B12 and calcium from dairy/eggs. Celiac demands near-zero gluten — staying under the **<20 ppm** "gluten-free" label limit and below the **~10–50 mg/day** harm threshold, trace cross-contamination included — whereas non-celiac gluten sensitivity often tolerates small amounts and lacks any validated threshold.
- **Life stage.** Infants, children, and pregnant/lactating people have the least margin; plant-based or allergy-restricted diets in these groups warrant professional oversight (§16).
- **Whether the gaps get filled.** The diet's healthiness is decided less by what's removed than by what replaces it — whole foods and targeted supplements, not ultra-processed "free-from" products.

### Practical takeaways

- **Going vegan?** A numeric daily checklist (scale per the "Adjust it" notes above): **B12** 50–100 mcg/day or 1,000 mcg 2–3×/week (no exceptions); **iron** ~1.8× RDA (≈14 mg men/older women, ≈32 mg menstruating women) paired with vitamin C; **zinc** up to 1.5× RDA (≈12–16.5 mg) with soaked/sprouted legumes; **iodine** 150 mcg (iodized salt or supplement, not sea salt); **calcium** 1,000–1,200 mg (≈3 cups fortified plant milk + tofu); **vitamin D** 600–800 IU as needed by sun/latitude; **algal DHA/EPA** ~200–300 mg; and **varied protein** at the upper end of your §02 band. Plan pregnancy and childhood diets with a dietitian.
- **Suspect celiac or gluten issues? Test first — while still eating gluten.** Don't go gluten-free as a diet trend; for non-celiac eaters it adds cost and often subtracts fiber.
- **Choose whole foods over "free-from" packaged products,** which tend to be lower-fiber and higher in sugar/fat/salt across both gluten-free and vegan aisles.
- **Food allergy:** know your nine major allergens, read labels every time, carry **two epinephrine auto-injectors**, and treat anaphylaxis with epinephrine first. Ask an allergist about oral immunotherapy.
- **For infants, introduce allergenic foods early** (including peanut and egg) rather than delaying — around **6 months**, when the infant is developmentally ready for solids. **Adjust it by risk tier** (NIAID 2017): **low risk / mild–moderate eczema →** introduce peanut and other allergens **around 6 months** at home, no testing needed; **high risk (severe eczema and/or egg allergy) →** see a clinician for **peanut-specific IgE or skin-prick testing first**, then introduce peanut as early as **4–6 months** if testing allows. Once started, the LEAP-style maintenance "dose" is **~2 g peanut protein per feeding ≈ ~6 g/week spread over ≥3 meals/week** (about **2 tsp of smooth peanut butter** thinned for safety — never whole nuts or globs, a choking hazard), continued regularly through age **~5 years**. This prevents allergy (§16).
- **Don't self-impose restrictions without a diagnosis.** Every food group you cut is nutrition (and pleasure) you may not get back. See §03 (micronutrients), §08 (personalization), and §12 (supplements).

### Unsettled or debated

**Non-celiac gluten sensitivity (NCGS)** is the genuine gray zone. It is a real clinical entity for some — symptoms improve off gluten with celiac disease and wheat allergy excluded — but blinded re-challenge studies suggest that in many people the trigger is **fermentable carbohydrate (FODMAPs), not gluten itself**, and a nocebo effect is common. There is no validated biomarker, so diagnosis is one of exclusion (§09, §15). The pragmatic stance: rule out celiac disease properly first, then trial a structured low-FODMAP or gluten reduction with professional guidance rather than indefinite self-restriction.
