## 12. Supplements: What Helps, What to Skip

**Bottom line:** In well-nourished people most supplements don't prevent disease — but a short list genuinely matters for specific gaps (B12 if plant-based or older, vitamin D if low-sun, folic acid pre-pregnancy, iron only if deficient, algal/fish omega-3, iodine, creatine); test, don't guess, and don't megadose.


The supplement aisle is where the gap between marketing and evidence is widest. The headline finding from decades of large trials is sobering: in well-nourished people, most supplements — multivitamins, antioxidants, and the rest — do **not** lower the risk of death, heart disease, or cancer. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force concluded in 2022 that there is *insufficient evidence* to recommend most vitamin or mineral supplements for preventing cardiovascular disease or cancer, and it recommended *against* beta-carotene and vitamin E for that purpose [USPSTF, JAMA 2022](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2793446). The governing principle is **"food first, then supplement the gaps"**: supplements are a tool for correcting a genuine shortfall, not a shortcut to a healthy diet [Harvard T.H. Chan, The Nutrition Source](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/multivitamin/).

That said, supplements are genuinely valuable — sometimes essential — for specific people with specific gaps. The trick is knowing which gaps are real for *you*: the same pill that is pointless for one person can be important for another. (A short list of who actually benefits from what is in *Practical takeaways*, below.)

### Key evidence

**The "supplement the gap" winners (strong evidence, in the right person):**

- **Vitamin B12** is non-negotiable for anyone eating little or no animal food. There is no reliable plant source; deficiency causes irreversible neurological damage and macrocytic anemia. Vegans, many vegetarians, older adults (who absorb food-bound B12 poorly), and people on metformin or long-term proton-pump inhibitors should supplement or use fortified foods [NIH ODS, B12](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/).

  **Adjust it —** baseline RDA is **2.4 mcg/day** (2.6 mcg in pregnancy, 2.8 mcg in lactation), but the *supplemental* dose is deliberately far higher because passive absorption (the route that doesn't need intrinsic factor) captures only ≈1% of an oral dose. Scaling and dependencies: a **plant-based eater with normal absorption** needs roughly **50–100 mcg/day, or 1,000–2,000 mcg twice weekly** to clear the RDA via passive uptake [NIH ODS, B12](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/). **Age >50, metformin, or chronic PPI/H2-blocker use** → impaired food-bound B12 release, so default to the same **50–500 mcg/day** supplemental range rather than relying on food. **Documented deficiency or malabsorption** (pernicious anemia, ileal resection, bariatric surgery) → **1,000–2,000 mcg/day oral** matches intramuscular injections in efficacy. There is no UL — excess is renally cleared — so erring high is cheap; the cost of underdosing is permanent nerve damage.
- **Folic acid before and in early pregnancy** is one of the best-proven supplements in medicine: 400–800 mcg/day starting *before* conception cuts neural-tube defects substantially, which is why grain fortification was mandated in many countries [USPSTF, JAMA 2023](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2807739); U.S. mandatory fortification has measurably lowered neural-tube-defect prevalence [CDC MMWR 2015](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm6401a2.htm).

  **Adjust it —** baseline for anyone who could become pregnant is **400–800 mcg/day**, ideally begun **≥1 month before conception** and continued through the first trimester [USPSTF, JAMA 2023](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2807739). Dependencies that raise the dose: **prior NTD-affected pregnancy** → **4,000 mcg/day (4 mg)** starting ≥1–3 months pre-conception, per CDC (note: trials show no added benefit above ~1 mg, so this is a margin-of-safety dose, not a linear one); **certain anti-seizure drugs (valproate, carbamazepine) or known MTHFR concerns / pre-gestational diabetes / obesity** → clinicians commonly use **1,000–4,000 mcg/day**. The UL for folic acid from supplements/fortified food is **1,000 mcg/day** in non-pregnant adults, so the 4 mg doses are intentional, supervised exceptions — not a default.
- **Vitamin D** reliably corrects deficiency (and prevents rickets/osteomalacia). It is reasonable for people with low sun exposure, darker skin at high latitude, older or housebound adults, and documented low blood levels. But in *replete* adults the large VITAL trial (n≈25,871) found no reduction in cancer or cardiovascular events from 2,000 IU/day [Manson et al., NEJM 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6425757/) — so it treats a deficiency, it is not a longevity drug.

  **Adjust it —** anchor on the blood level, not a fixed pill. The IOM/NIH RDA is **600 IU/day (15 mcg) for ages 1–70 and 800 IU/day (20 mcg) for >70**, with a UL of **4,000 IU/day (100 mcg)** [NIH ODS, Vitamin D](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/). Target serum 25(OH)D is **≥20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L)** for bone health; **<12 ng/mL is deficient**, and **>50 ng/mL (>60 ng/mL especially) is potentially harmful** — there is no payoff to chasing high numbers. Scaling and dependencies: **maintenance/mild shortfall** → **1,000–2,000 IU/day** (a typical over-the-counter repletion dose, as used in VITAL — though the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline recommends *against* routine supplementation above the 600 IU RDA for disease prevention in healthy adults aged 19–74, reserving higher empiric doses for specific higher-risk groups such as those >75, pregnancy, prediabetes, or high falls/fracture risk); **documented deficiency** → a clinician-supervised repletion course (e.g., **6,000 IU/day**, or **50,000 IU once weekly for 8 weeks**, then drop to 1,500–2,000 IU/day) [Endocrine Society guideline](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/96/7/1911/2833671). Modifiers that push toward the top of the maintenance range: **darker skin / consistent sun avoidance / latitude >37° in winter / BMI >30** (vitamin D sequesters in fat, so obesity can need ~1.5–2× the dose to reach the same serum level). Retest in **8–12 weeks** and titrate — don't keep escalating blind.
- **Iron** corrects iron-deficiency anemia — common in menstruating and pregnant women, frequent blood donors, and some plant-based eaters — but should be taken only for a *documented* deficiency, because iron overload is harmful. Routine iron can do more harm than good in men and postmenopausal women, and is actively dangerous in undiagnosed hereditary hemochromatosis (a common inherited iron-overload disorder, especially in people of Northern European descent). Iron-containing products are also a leading cause of fatal poisoning in young children, so keep them out of reach [NIH ODS, Iron](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/).

  **Adjust it —** the dietary RDA is **8 mg/day for adult men and postmenopausal women, 18 mg/day for menstruating women (19–50), and 27 mg/day in pregnancy**; the UL is **45 mg/day** [NIH ODS, Iron](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/). The trigger for *supplementing*, though, is a lab value, not a demographic: **ferritin <30 mcg/L** indicates iron deficiency and **<10–15 mcg/L** indicates iron-deficiency anemia. Scaling the *repletion* dose (elemental iron, clinician-guided): mild deficiency or maintenance → **~18–37.5 mg/day elemental** (e.g., 37.5 mg from ferrous gluconate restored stores in blood donors over 24 weeks); frank anemia → typically **45–65 mg/day** (often 65 mg ferrous sulfate, sometimes dosed **every other day**, which newer data suggest absorbs more efficiently than splitting across the day). Dependencies: **plant-based diet** → non-heme iron is ~2–3× less bioavailable, so pair intake with vitamin C and assume needs run ~1.8× the RDA; **hemochromatosis or ferritin already >150–200 mcg/L** → **0 mg, supplementation contraindicated**; **regular blood donation** → recheck ferritin and replace only if low. Iron is the rare case where "more, just in case" is genuinely dangerous.
- **Omega-3 (EPA/DHA)** from an algal or fish-oil supplement is sensible for people who eat little oily fish, and **algal DHA** is specifically recommended in pregnancy/lactation for those who don't eat fish. For broad cardiovascular *prevention*, fish-oil supplements were neutral in VITAL [Manson et al., NEJM 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6392053/); high-dose prescription EPA (icosapent ethyl) helped only a narrow high-risk, high-triglyceride, statin-treated group, and raised atrial-fibrillation hospitalization (3.1% vs 2.1%) in the REDUCE-IT trial (industry-funded: Amarin Pharma, maker of the drug) [Bhatt et al., NEJM 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/).

  **Adjust it —** baseline replacement dose for someone who eats little oily fish is **~250–500 mg/day combined EPA+DHA**, the amount you'd get from the widely cited "1–2 fish servings per week" (≈250 mg/day) that anchors most national guidance [NIH ODS, Omega-3](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/). Scaling and dependencies: **pregnancy/lactation without fish** → add **~200–300 mg/day DHA** specifically (EFSA: +100–200 mg DHA on top of the 250 mg adult baseline; low-DHA pregnancies sometimes 600–1,000 mg/day DHA+EPA from the 2nd trimester), preferably **algal** to avoid contaminant and methylmercury concerns; **vegan** → algal oil is the only practical EPA/DHA source since ALA→EPA/DHA conversion is only ~5–10% (EPA) and <0.5% (DHA), so don't count flax/chia toward this target; **established CHD** → the AHA suggests ~**1 g/day EPA+DHA**; **prescription-level triglyceride lowering** → **4 g/day** prescription omega-3 — but that is a drug indication, not general prevention, and (per REDUCE-IT) carries a real atrial-fibrillation signal, so it is doctor-directed, not a supplement-aisle decision.
- **Iodine** matters where salt isn't iodized; the RDA is **150 mcg/day for adults, rising to 220 mcg in pregnancy and 290 mcg in lactation**, with a UL of **1,100 mcg/day** [NIH ODS, Iodine](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-HealthProfessional/). **Adjust it —** if you use **iodized salt** or eat **dairy/seafood/seaweed** regularly, you likely need **0 supplemental**; the people who actually need a supplement are those eating mostly **non-iodized salt (most "sea salt," kosher salt, and the salt in packaged food)** plus little dairy or seafood — and **vegans** especially, who should target the 150 mcg RDA via a measured supplement (note seaweed is wildly variable, sometimes delivering many times the UL, so it's a poor primary source). In pregnancy/lactation, many prenatal vitamins supply ~150 mcg, closing most of the gap to 220–290 mcg.
- **Calcium** is best from food, with supplements reserved for those who can't meet needs. **Adjust it —** the RDA is **1,000 mg/day for adults 19–50 and men 51–70**, rising to **1,200 mg/day for women >50 and everyone >70**; the UL falls from **2,500 mg/day (≤50) to 2,000 mg/day (>50)** [NIH ODS, Calcium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/). Supplement only the *gap*: tally dietary calcium (≈300 mg per cup of milk/fortified soy milk or per ~1.5 oz hard cheese; ~100–200 mg per serving of leafy greens, tofu, or fortified foods) and add a supplement only to reach the RDA — for many people that's **0–500 mg/day**, not the 1,000+ mg often sold. Keep single doses **≤500 mg** (absorption fraction drops above that), take with food, and pair with adequate vitamin D. Because high-dose *supplemental* calcium is the form tied to possible cardiovascular and kidney-stone risk, food-first is the conservative default.
- **Creatine monohydrate** (~3–5 g/day) is among the best-evidenced ergogenic aids for strength and lean mass, is cheap and safe in healthy people, and shows promising (not yet definitive) signals for muscle preservation in older adults [ISSN position stand, JISSN 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5469049/).

  **Adjust it —** baseline maintenance is **3–5 g/day**, every day, taken at any time (timing barely matters) [ISSN position stand, JISSN 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5469049/). Scaling and dependencies: **want full muscle saturation in ~5–7 days instead of ~4 weeks** → optional loading of **~0.3 g/kg/day (≈20 g split into 4 doses)** for 5–7 days, then drop to maintenance — loading is purely about speed and is skippable; **larger/more muscular athletes (>90–100 kg)** → may need the upper end, **5–10 g/day**, since the dose scales roughly with muscle mass (~0.03 g/kg/day); **vegans/vegetarians** → start with lower baseline stores, so they tend to see the *largest* response. Skip or get medical sign-off if you have **significant kidney disease** (creatine is safe for healthy kidneys but raises serum creatinine cosmetically, which can confuse renal tests). Buy a product that is just **creatine monohydrate** and ideally third-party certified; the fancier "forms" cost more without added benefit.

**The "skip it" list (strong evidence of no benefit, or net harm):**

- **Multivitamins** do not extend life or prevent chronic disease in well-nourished adults; the large Physicians' Health Study II and COSMOS analyses found, at best, marginal effects [USPSTF, JAMA 2022](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2793446); the COSMOS trial (n=21,442) found no reduction in cancer or cardiovascular disease from a daily multivitamin (industry-supported: investigator-initiated grant from Mars Edge; multivitamin supplied by Pfizer Consumer Healthcare) [Sesso et al., COSMOS, AJCN 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9170475/). **Adjust it —** "skip" applies to the well-nourished. A **standard once-daily multivitamin (roughly 100% of the RDA, not megadoses)** is a reasonable low-cost insurance policy for genuinely at-risk eaters: very low total intake (**<1,200 kcal/day** dieting), poor diet variety, post-bariatric surgery, or food insecurity — but it does not substitute for correcting a *specific* measured deficiency, which needs a targeted dose (see the winners list above).
- **Beta-carotene** *increased* lung cancer and mortality in smokers in two large trials — a landmark cautionary tale that "antioxidant = good" is false: the ATBC study found 18% higher lung-cancer incidence and 8% higher total mortality in male smokers [ATBC Study Group, NEJM 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8127329/), and CARET found a 28% rise in lung cancer and 17% rise in death in smokers and asbestos-exposed workers [Omenn et al., CARET, NEJM 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8602180/).
- **High-dose vitamin E** showed no benefit and *increased* prostate cancer in the SELECT trial — **400 IU/day raised prostate-cancer risk by 17%** in healthy men [Klein et al., JAMA 2011](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/1104493) — and high doses are associated with higher all-cause mortality [Bjelakovic et al., Cochrane 2012](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD007176.pub2/abstract). For context the RDA is just **15 mg/day (≈22 IU)** and the UL is **1,000 mg/day (≈1,500 IU)**; the harm signal is a dosing problem, not an argument against food sources like nuts and seeds.
- **High-dose antioxidant cocktails** (A, E, beta-carotene) trend toward *higher* mortality in Cochrane meta-analyses. In the Bjelakovic Cochrane review (78 trials, ~296,000 participants), beta-carotene and vitamin E were associated with increased all-cause mortality (with a similar but weaker, dose-dependent signal for higher-dose vitamin A); the increase was significant in the fixed-effect model and in low-risk-of-bias trials, though not in the primary random-effects analysis [Bjelakovic et al., Cochrane 2012](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD007176.pub2/abstract).
- **Calcium supplements** (as opposed to dietary calcium) are debated: some analyses link them to a modest rise in cardiovascular and kidney-stone risk, so food sources are preferred and supplements individualized [NIH ODS, Calcium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/).

**Regulation is weak.** In the U.S., supplements are not pre-approved for safety or efficacy; independent testing repeatedly finds mislabeling, under- or over-dosing, and contamination. Third-party certification (USP, NSF, Informed Sport) is a reasonable filter.

### What it depends on

- **Diet pattern.** Vegans need B12 (and usually vitamin D, omega-3/algal DHA, iodine, and attention to iron/zinc/calcium); omnivores eating fish and varied plants need far less.
- **Life stage and sex.** Pregnancy/lactation (folate, iron, iodine, choline, DHA), menstruation (iron), and older age (B12, D, protein, possibly creatine) each change the list.
- **Sun, latitude, and skin tone.** These drive vitamin D need far more than diet does.
- **Medications and conditions.** Metformin and PPIs lower B12; malabsorptive conditions (celiac, bariatric surgery, IBD) create multiple real deficiencies that *should* be supplemented and monitored.
- **Blood levels, not guesses.** The strongest case for any supplement is a measured deficiency, not a symptom or a marketing claim.

### Practical takeaways

- **Default to food.** A varied, mostly-plant, minimally-processed diet plus adequate sun makes most supplements unnecessary for a healthy adult.
- **The short "actually worth it" list for most people who need anything** — with the dose tied to the gap, not a one-size pill:
  - **B12** (plant-based or age >50, on metformin/PPI): **50–100 mcg/day, or 1,000–2,000 mcg twice weekly** routine; **1,000–2,000 mcg/day** if deficient/malabsorbing.
  - **Vitamin D** (low sun, dark skin, BMI >30, or 25(OH)D <20 ng/mL): **1,000–2,000 IU/day** maintenance, target serum **20–30 ng/mL**, UL 4,000 IU.
  - **Folic acid** (could become pregnant): **400–800 mcg/day**; **4,000 mcg** only with a prior NTD pregnancy under medical guidance.
  - **Iron** (only if ferritin <30 mcg/L): **~18–37.5 mg/day elemental** for mild deficiency, **45–65 mg** for anemia; not at all if hemochromatosis or ferritin already high.
  - **Algal/fish omega-3** (little oily fish): **~250–500 mg/day EPA+DHA**; pregnancy without fish add **~200–300 mg DHA** (algal).
  - **Iodine** (no iodized salt, low dairy/seafood, vegan): **~150 mcg/day**; pregnancy/lactation 220/290 mcg.
  - **Creatine** (strength/lean-mass or older adults): **3–5 g/day**, no testing needed.
- **Don't megadose.** More is not better — fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E) and minerals (iron, selenium, zinc) have real toxicity ceilings. **Adjust it —** keep total intake (food + supplements) under the adult Tolerable Upper Intake Levels: **vitamin A 3,000 mcg RAE (≈10,000 IU); vitamin D 4,000 IU (100 mcg); vitamin E 1,000 mg (≈1,500 IU); iron 45 mg; zinc 40 mg; selenium 400 mcg; iodine 1,100 mcg; calcium 2,000–2,500 mg; folic acid 1,000 mcg** [NIH ODS factsheets](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/list-all/). Water-soluble vitamins (B12, vitamin C) are far more forgiving and largely renally cleared, but "no UL" is not "more is better." Pregnancy lowers the safe ceiling for some (notably preformed vitamin A, where >3,000 mcg RAE is teratogenic).
- **Avoid beta-carotene supplements entirely if you smoke.**
- **Test, then treat.** Ask your clinician for B12, vitamin D, iron/ferritin levels rather than self-prescribing a cabinet of pills.
- **Buy third-party-certified products** (USP/NSF/Informed Sport) given weak regulation.
- **Treat "immune boosters," "detoxes," antioxidant blends, and most herbal megadoses as unproven** until shown otherwise — and tell your doctor what you take, because some interact with medications (e.g., vitamin K with warfarin [NIH ODS, Vitamin K](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminK-HealthProfessional/), St. John's Wort with many drugs).

### Unsettled or debated

- **Vitamin D dosing and the "optimal" blood level** remain contested: as the VITAL evidence above shows, supplementation corrects deficiency but hasn't been shown to prevent disease in replete people, and the right target threshold is still debated.
- **Omega-3 for cardiovascular prevention** is unsettled, for the reasons covered above: neutral in broad trials, beneficial only in a narrow high-triglyceride subgroup, and carrying a real atrial-fibrillation signal at high doses.
- **Calcium supplements and cardiovascular risk** are genuinely debated (see above); the safest current read remains "get calcium from food when you can."
- **Creatine's non-muscle benefits** (cognition, bone, healthy aging) are an active and promising research area, not yet settled.
