## 02. Macronutrients: Carbohydrate Quality, Protein, and Fats

**Bottom line:** Within safe macro ranges, *which foods* supply each macro matters far more than the ratio — favor intact carbs and fiber, replace saturated with unsaturated fat, and treat 0.8 g/kg protein as a floor (general adults ~1.2-1.6 g/kg), not a target.


For most of the last century, macronutrient advice was a numbers game: how many grams of carbs, how much fat, what percentage protein. The strongest modern evidence has moved decisively past that framing. Within the broad ranges that nutrition authorities consider safe — what the US and Canadian Dietary Reference Intakes call the Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Ranges (AMDR): carbohydrate 45-65%, fat 20-35%, and protein 10-35% of total energy [IOM DRI Macronutrients 2002/2005](https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/10490) (these ranges are now under active re-examination [NASEM 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK610333/)) — your health outcomes are driven far more by *which foods* supply each macro than by hitting a particular ratio.

That single shift reorganizes everything that follows. Carbohydrate is not "good" or "bad"; lentils and a doughnut are both carbohydrate, and they behave nothing alike. Fat is not a monolith; the question is which fatty acid you eat instead of which other one. Protein is not just a number to maximize; the source and your life stage change what "enough" means.

**Adjust it (where to sit inside the AMDR) —** baseline: a generalist lands mid-range — carbohydrate ~50%, fat ~30%, protein ~15-20% of energy. Shift *within* the ranges, not outside them, based on demand. **Endurance/high training volume → push carbohydrate toward the 60-65% top** (the joint Academy/Dietitians of Canada/ACSM position translates this to 6-10 g carb/kg/day for 1-3 h/day of training, and 8-12 g/kg/day for ≥4-5 h/day) [Thomas et al., ACSM/AND/DC Position Stand 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26891166/). **Insulin resistance or type 2 diabetes → shift carbohydrate toward the 45% floor or below** (a low-carb pattern is ~26-45% of energy / ~130-230 g/day, and each 10% reduction in carbohydrate energy is associated with ~0.39% lower HbA1c and ~1.5-point lower HOMA-IR over 3-6 months, though the glycemic edge often fades past 12-24 months without sustained adherence) [Jing et al., systematic review/meta-analysis 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12126305/); see §09 for disease-specific targets. **Higher protein goals (older adults, athletes, fat-loss) → take protein toward the 25-35% top** and let carbohydrate fall accordingly. The carbohydrate floor for brain glucose is only the 130 g/day RDA, so even a 1,600-kcal low-carb day stays safely above it.

Three themes recur throughout this section. First, **fiber and whole, intact foods carry the heaviest evidence** — much heavier than glycemic index or macro percentages. Second, for fat, the right mental model is **replacement**: the health effect of saturated fat depends on what you swap it for — replacing it with polyunsaturated fat lowers cardiovascular risk, while swapping in refined carbohydrate does not [AHA Presidential Advisory, Sacks et al., Circulation 2017](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000510). Third, the protein RDA (0.8 g/kg/day) is a **floor, not a target** — adequate to prevent deficiency, but probably not optimal for muscle, satiety, or aging. US guidance itself has now moved off that floor: the 2025-2030 Dietary Guidelines (released January 2026) recommend roughly 1.2-1.6 g/kg/day for general adults, while the 0.8 g/kg RDA remains unchanged as the technical deficiency-prevention reference [DGA 2025-2030](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/).

A note on certainty: some of this is settled (fiber benefits, eliminating industrial trans fat), some is strong but observational (protein source and mortality), and some is genuinely debated (the absolute magnitude of saturated fat's harm, how high to push protein). I flag the strength of evidence as we go.

### Key evidence

**Carbohydrate quality beats quantity (strong).** Within the 45-65% AMDR, outcomes hinge on source: refined grains and added sugars (high glycemic load, low fiber) track with worse cardiometabolic health, while whole grains and fiber track with better. WHO and the landmark 2019 *Lancet* review concluded the disease associations were robust for fiber and whole grains but weaker for glycemic index alone [Reynolds et al., Lancet 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30638909/). The carbohydrate RDA — the amount needed to supply the brain with glucose — is only 130 g/day, far below typical intake.

**Fiber, 25-29 g/day, is genuinely protective (strong).** The 2019 *Lancet* meta-analysis pooled 185 prospective studies (~135 million person-years) plus 58 randomized trials and found risk reduction greatest at 25-29 g/day, with the dose-response curve suggesting *more* still helps [Reynolds et al., Lancet 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30638909/). Each additional 8 g/day was tied to 5-27% lower rates of total death, coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and colorectal cancer. The catch: most adults eat only ~15 g/day against US targets of ~25 g (women) and ~38 g (men), roughly 14 g per 1,000 kcal; the US Dietary Guidelines name fiber a "nutrient of public health concern" because intake is so widely below recommendations [DGA 2020-2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/sites/default/files/2020-12/Dietary_Guidelines_for_Americans_2020-2025.pdf). Soluble/viscous fiber (oats, psyllium, legumes) lowers LDL cholesterol and post-meal glucose; insoluble fiber (wheat bran, whole grains) aids regularity and tracks with lower diabetes risk. Both matter [Harvard Nutrition Source 2024](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/carbohydrates/fiber/).

**Adjust it (fiber) —** baseline: **14 g per 1,000 kcal** — primary source is the IOM/NASEM Dietary Reference Intakes [IOM DRI Macronutrients 2002](https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/10490), restated in the US guidelines [DGA 2020-2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/sites/default/files/2020-12/Dietary_Guidelines_for_Americans_2020-2025.pdf), so scale to your actual intake:
- 1,500 kcal → **~21 g/day**; 2,000 kcal → **~28 g/day**; 2,500 kcal → **~35 g/day**; 3,000 kcal → **~42 g/day**. (These bracket the sex-based US targets — ~25 g for women, ~38 g for men.)
- **Ramp, don't jump.** Coming from a typical ~15 g/day, raise by **~5 g/week** and drink more water; the dose-response curve keeps falling past 25-29 g/day, so **up to ~50 g/day** is reasonable for most healthy adults, but going from 15 to 40 g overnight reliably causes gas and bloating.
- **Depends on:** prior intake (the lower you start, the slower you ramp); GI conditions (active IBD flare, recent bowel surgery, or known stricture → individualize with a clinician, sometimes *low*-residue temporarily); supplement type (a viscous fiber like psyllium ~5-10 g/day specifically lowers LDL and post-meal glucose, whereas wheat bran mainly aids regularity). Worked example: a 2,000-kcal eater currently at 16 g aims for ~28 g — that's +12 g, so ramp over ~2-3 weeks.

**Whole grains beat refined grains (strong).** Eating ≥2.5 servings/day of whole grains (versus <2 servings/week) is associated with about 21% lower cardiovascular disease risk, and ~50 g/day of whole grain corresponds to a relative risk near 0.76 for type 2 diabetes in dose-response meta-analyses [Harvard Nutrition Source 2023](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/what-should-you-eat/whole-grains/); [Nutrition Journal 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11044462/). Refined grains are stripped of bran and germ, sacrificing fiber, nutrients, and phytochemicals. The simplest rule — make at least half your grains whole — is high-confidence.

**Added/free sugars should be sharply limited (strong).** WHO strongly recommends keeping free sugars below 10% of energy (~50 g/day on a 2,000-kcal diet), with a conditional further reduction to under 5% (~25 g, or 6 teaspoons) for added benefit against weight gain and dental caries [WHO 2015](https://www.who.int/news/item/04-03-2015-who-calls-on-countries-to-reduce-sugars-intake-among-adults-and-children). "Free sugars" means sugars added by manufacturers, cooks, or you, plus those in honey, syrups, and fruit juice. Crucially, the *intrinsic* sugars locked in the cellular matrix of whole fruit and vegetables, and the lactose in plain dairy, are explicitly excluded — whole fruit is not the problem. Sugar-sweetened beverages are the single largest source and the most strongly implicated in weight gain and diabetes [Harvard Nutrition Source 2024](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/healthy-drinks/sugary-drinks/). The US 2020-2025 guidelines cap added sugars at under 10% of calories [DGA 2020-2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/sites/default/files/2020-12/Dietary_Guidelines_for_Americans_2020-2025.pdf).

**Adjust it (free sugars) —** baseline: the WHO <10%-of-energy cap is a *strong* recommendation; the <5% further reduction is *conditional* (weaker evidence — graded "moderate" for the 10% line and "very low" for the 5% line) [WHO 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK285525/). At 4 kcal/g, convert energy to grams:
- **<10% cap:** 1,600 kcal → **<40 g/day**; 2,000 kcal → **<50 g/day**; 2,500 kcal → **<63 g/day**.
- **<5% stretch goal:** 1,600 kcal → **<20 g/day**; 2,000 kcal → **<25 g/day** (~6 tsp); 2,500 kcal → **<31 g/day**.
- **Depends on:** dental-caries risk or weight-loss goal → aim for the 5% line; children → tighter still. The current US position is stricter than the adult cap: the 2025-2030 Dietary Guidelines (released January 2026) advise **avoiding added sugars until about age 10** — up from the 2020-2025 guidance to avoid them before age 2 [DGA 2025-2030](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/). For school-age children a useful older expert ceiling is the AHA's 2016/2017 scientific-statement benchmark of **<25 g/day (~6 tsp) of added sugar for ages 2-18** [AHA, Circulation 2016](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/cir.0000000000000439) (a single 350 mL soda ≈ 35-40 g already blows that whole-day budget). **Whole fruit and the lactose in plain dairy do not count** — only sugars added by a manufacturer/cook/you, plus those free in honey, syrup, and fruit juice. The single highest-leverage move is cutting sugar-sweetened beverages, since they are the largest single source.

**Glycemic index/load: useful, but weaker (moderate).** High-GI and high-GL diets are associated with roughly 10-30% higher type 2 diabetes risk across meta-analyses, and a large PURE cohort analysis linked high-GI diets to greater cardiovascular events and mortality, especially in people with existing disease [Nutrients 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6627334/); [NEJM 2021](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2007123). But GI/GL is heavily confounded with fiber and food matrix and varies between people and meals, so WHO judged the evidence insufficient to drive standalone guidance. The practical upshot: choosing whole, minimally processed carbohydrates captures most of the benefit without anyone tracking index values.

**Protein RDA is a floor, not an optimum (strong).** The RDA of 0.8 g/kg/day — about 56 g for a 70-kg adult — has been essentially unchanged for 70+ years and was set, via nitrogen-balance studies, to prevent deficiency in 97.5% of healthy adults [Adv Nutr 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5347101/). Tellingly, even the *lowest* end of the protein AMDR (10% of energy) exceeds the RDA for most people, leaving ample room for higher intake within a healthy pattern.

**Adjust it (daily protein — the canonical math) —** baseline target scales with body weight, *not* a flat gram number. Use kg (lb ÷ 2.205). For people well above a healthy weight, calculate on a reasonable-body-weight or adjusted basis rather than total weight, or protein will be overestimated.
- **General healthy adult (under 75):** **1.2-1.6 g/kg/day** (current 2025-2030 DGA, which raised the figure specifically for healthy adults under 75 years of age) [DGA 2025-2030](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/) — e.g., a 70-kg adult → **~84-112 g/day**. The 0.8 g/kg RDA (~56 g at 70 kg) remains only the deficiency-prevention *floor*, not the goal; the size of this jump off the RDA is itself debated (see "Unsettled" below). Adults **75 and older** are not covered by this higher figure and should follow older-adult guidance (~1.0-1.2+ g/kg/day; see the next line).
- **Healthy older adult (≥65):** **1.0-1.2 g/kg/day** baseline; **+0.2 g/kg** (to ~1.2-1.5) with acute or chronic illness, and **up to 2.0 g/kg** with severe illness/injury or marked malnutrition [PROT-AGE 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23867520/); [ESPEN 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4208946/).
- **Endurance or strength athlete:** **1.4-2.0 g/kg/day**; gains plateau above **~1.6 g/kg** in resistance-training meta-analysis, so going beyond ~1.6-2.0 buys little for muscle [Morton et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/).
- **Active fat-loss (energy deficit):** push toward **~1.6-2.4 g/kg/day** to spare lean mass while dieting; the deeper the deficit and the leaner you already are, the higher within that band.
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** RDA rises to **1.1 g/kg/day** in pregnancy and **1.3 g/kg/day** in lactation [IOM DRI 2005](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK562630/).
- **Reduced kidney function (CKD, not on dialysis):** this is the one direction that goes *down* — see the dedicated CKD note below. Do not push protein high without checking kidney function first.

**Older adults and athletes benefit from more (moderate).** Expert groups (PROT-AGE, ESPEN) recommend 1.0-1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older adults, 1.2-1.5 g/kg with illness, and up to 2.0 g/kg during recovery [PROT-AGE 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23867520/); [ESPEN 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4208946/). Aging muscle shows "anabolic resistance" — it needs a larger per-meal bolus, roughly 25-30 g of protein (enough to clear a leucine threshold of ~2.5 g in younger adults and ~3 g in older adults, leucine being the trigger amino acid for muscle protein synthesis), versus less in younger adults [PROT-AGE 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23867520/); [Breen & Phillips, Nutr Metab 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3201893/). Strength and endurance athletes are commonly advised ~1.4-2.0 g/kg/day; a meta-analysis of resistance-training trials found that protein intakes beyond roughly 1.6 g/kg/day yielded no further gains in muscle mass [Morton et al., Br J Sports Med 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/) (the study itself was publicly funded, but its senior author discloses grants, travel, and honoraria from the US National Dairy Council, which had supported some trials included in the analysis).

**Adjust it (per-meal protein dose) —** baseline: hit roughly **0.4 g/kg per meal** spread across 3-4 meals, because muscle protein synthesis is maximized per-bolus, not per-day [Schoenfeld & Aragon, ISSN review 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5828430/).
- For a 70-kg adult, 0.4 g/kg → **~28 g per meal**; older adults push to **~0.4-0.55 g/kg (~30-40 g)** to overcome anabolic resistance and clear the **~3 g leucine** threshold (vs **~2.5 g** in younger adults).
- **Depends on:** age (≥65 → upper end, ~3 g leucine / ~0.4-0.55 g/kg); protein source (plant-only → add ~25% more total and combine complementary sources, since a single plant protein clears the leucine threshold at a larger dose); training timing (one meal in the ~0-2 h window around resistance exercise). Practical anchors for ~3 g leucine: ~30 g whey, ~3-4 eggs plus a side, ~110-140 g cooked chicken/fish, or ~1.5 cups cooked lentils.

**Protein source matters for chronic disease (moderate, observational).** Across large cohorts (Nurses' Health Study, EPIC, Adventist Health Study-2), higher red and especially processed meat intake tracks with greater all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality, while plant protein tracks with lower mortality [JAMA Intern Med 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7358979/). Processed meat is classified as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) and red meat as probably carcinogenic (Group 2A) by the WHO's International Agency for Research on Cancer, based principally on colorectal cancer evidence [IARC/WHO 2015](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/cancer-carcinogenicity-of-the-consumption-of-red-meat-and-processed-meat). Substitution models suggest replacing ~3% of energy from animal protein with plant protein is associated with about 18% lower CVD and 24% lower coronary risk [AJCN 2024](https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(24)00737-8/fulltext). These are observational and confounded by overall lifestyle, but the consistency across cohorts is notable. Animal proteins still supply high-quality complete amino acids and bioavailable nutrients (B12, heme iron, choline), and score highest on quality metrics like DIAAS (~1.0+, versus ~0.4-0.7 for many individual plant proteins) [FAO 2013](https://www.fao.org/4/i3124e/i3124e.pdf).

### What it depends on

- **Life stage.** Older adults need more protein per kilogram *and* per meal than the RDA implies; children's added-sugar tolerance is lower still.
- **Activity and goals.** Athletes and those building or preserving muscle benefit from the upper end (1.4-2.0 g/kg); sedentary adults need less.
- **Diet pattern.** Plant-only eaters should combine complementary sources (grains + legumes), favor higher-quality plants (soy, quinoa), and eat modestly more total protein to offset lower digestibility — not a barrier, just a design choice.
- **Health status.** Existing cardiovascular disease or diabetes raises the payoff from cutting refined carbs and high-GI foods. Reduced kidney function is a real exception where protein may need limiting under medical guidance — and because chronic kidney disease is common and frequently undiagnosed in older adults — more than a third of US adults 65+ have it (~34%), and roughly 9 in 10 of those affected do not know it (exactly the group urged toward the upper protein range) — aggressive protein increases should not be made without checking kidney function first [CDC CKD in the United States 2023](https://www.cdc.gov/kidney-disease/php/data-research/index.html).

  **Adjust it (CKD protein — this one goes DOWN, and only under clinical supervision) —** the general 1.2-1.6 g/kg target does *not* apply once kidney function is reduced. The current primary recommendation is **~0.8 g/kg/day (and avoid >1.3 g/kg/day)** for adults with non-dialysis CKD, per the KDIGO 2024 CKD guideline [KDIGO 2024](https://www.kidney-international.org/article/S0085-2538(23)00766-4/fulltext). The stricter KDOQI 2020 nutrition guideline framing, for metabolically stable, non-dialysis CKD stages G3-G5, is: **without diabetes → 0.55-0.60 g/kg/day** (or a supervised very-low-protein 0.28-0.43 g/kg/day plus keto-acid analogs); **with diabetes → 0.6-0.8 g/kg/day**; **on maintenance dialysis → 1.0-1.2 g/kg/day** (dialysis raises protein needs because the treatment itself removes amino acids) [KDOQI 2020](https://www.ajkd.org/article/S0272-6386(20)30726-5/fulltext). These targets are keyed to GFR stage and diabetes status, must be clinician-set and supervised by a renal dietitian, and presuppose adequate energy intake to avoid catabolizing the restricted protein. See §09 for the disease-specific protocol.
- **Total calories.** Macro *percentages* shift with intake; the gram-per-kilogram framing for protein and the absolute fiber/sugar targets are more reliable anchors. **Adjust it —** anchor protein in g/kg and fiber/sugar/saturated-fat in grams (per the tables above), then let *carbohydrate fill the remainder* of your energy. Estimate that energy budget from your own maintenance calories (Mifflin-St Jeor; see §20), not a generic 2,000-kcal slate. For fat loss, a **−500 kcal/day** deficit predicts roughly **−0.45 kg (1 lb)/week** as a *first-pass* estimate — but the old 3,500-kcal-per-pound rule overestimates real loss over months because metabolic adaptation lowers expenditure as you shrink, so expect closer to **−0.25 to −0.45 kg/week** and recalculate as weight drops [NIDDK Body Weight Planner](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/research-funding/at-niddk/labs-branches/laboratory-biological-modeling/integrative-physiology-section/research/body-weight-planner). In pregnancy, add **+340 kcal/day** in the 2nd trimester and **+452 kcal/day** in the 3rd (1st trimester unchanged), and **+330 kcal/day** in the first 6 months of lactation [NASEM DRI for Energy 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK591034/).

### Practical takeaways

- **Default to intact carbs.** Whole grains, legumes, vegetables, and whole fruit. Aim for **~14 g fiber per 1,000 kcal** (≈28 g at 2,000 kcal; scale per the fiber table, ramp +5 g/week toward up to ~50 g) — that one habit captures most of the "carb quality" benefit.
- **Make at least half your grains whole**; better, aim for 2-3 whole-grain servings daily (≥2.5 servings/day tracks with ~21% lower CVD risk; ~50 g/day with ~24% lower diabetes risk) and treat refined grains as occasional.
- **Cap free sugars at <10% of energy, ideally toward <5%.** In grams that's **<50 g/day at 2,000 kcal** (the <10% line) and **~25 g/day** (the <5% stretch); scale to your calories per the sugar table. The highest-leverage single move is cutting sugar-sweetened beverages. Whole fruit does not count against this.
- **Treat 0.8 g/kg as the deficiency-prevention minimum, not the goal.** Current US guidance (2025-2030 DGA) recommends ~1.2-1.6 g/kg for general adults (~84-112 g/day at 70 kg); older adults and athletes land toward the upper end (up to ~2.0 g/kg, with no extra muscle benefit past ~1.6). Spread it across meals at **~0.4 g/kg each (~25-30 g)**, more for older adults (~0.4-0.55 g/kg, ~30-40 g) to clear the ~3 g leucine threshold. Use the adjustable protein math above; see the kidney/source caveats before pushing to the top of the range.
- **Shift the protein mix toward plants, fish, legumes, and nuts**, with red and processed meat as the smaller share — without eliminating nutrient-dense animal foods if you eat them. The case for higher protein assumes high-quality, predominantly plant, fish, and lean sources; meeting an elevated target mostly through processed and red meat carries the cancer signal noted above and forfeits the benefit.
- **Check kidney function before pushing protein high.** If you have reduced kidney function — common and often undiagnosed in older adults — the upper protein range can be harmful; raise intake only with medical guidance.
- **Don't chase glycemic index numbers.** Eating whole, minimally processed food gives you the GI benefit for free.

### Unsettled or debated

- **The absolute magnitude of saturated fat's harm.** There is broad agreement that *replacing* saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat or whole grains reduces cardiovascular events — a Cochrane review of randomized trials found that cutting saturated fat reduced combined cardiovascular events by about 17%, with benefit driven by replacement with unsaturated fat [Hooper et al., Cochrane 2020](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD011737.pub3/full) — but experts genuinely disagree on how harmful saturated fat is in absolute terms and on the role of specific dairy and meat foods.

  **Adjust it (saturated fat) —** baseline cap is **<10% of energy** (US DGA, general population); tighten to **<6% of energy** if you have or are lowering elevated LDL cholesterol (American Heart Association) [AHA Saturated Fats](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/eat-smart/fats/saturated-fats). At 9 kcal/g of fat, convert to grams of saturated fat:
  - **<10% cap:** 1,600 kcal → **<18 g/day**; 2,000 kcal → **<22 g/day**; 2,500 kcal → **<28 g/day**.
  - **<6% (high-LDL) target:** 1,600 kcal → **<11 g/day**; 2,000 kcal → **<13 g/day**; 2,500 kcal → **<17 g/day**.
  - **Depends on:** what you swap *in*, not just out — replacing the cut saturated fat with **polyunsaturated fat** (e.g., a 5%-energy swap is tied to ~25% lower coronary risk) or whole grains lowers events, whereas replacing it with refined carbohydrate or sugar does not [Sacks et al., Circulation 2017](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000510). For scale, ~13 g saturated fat is about 2 tablespoons of butter or ~140 g of 80%-lean ground beef.
- **How high to push protein.** The 2025-2030 US Dietary Guidelines, released in January 2026, raised the general-adult recommendation to ~1.2-1.6 g/kg/day — a 50-100% jump over the old 0.8 g/kg floor — though the 0.8 g/kg RDA itself is unchanged as the technical deficiency reference. This is now finalized guidance, but it remains contested: the figure came out of an unusual supplemental analysis rather than the standard Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee process, and Harvard nutrition researchers (Frank Hu, Deirdre Tobias, among others) criticized it for not distinguishing protein *source* — one could meet the higher target entirely from red and processed meat — and warned that raising overall protein without that distinction may carry long-term harms [Harvard Nutrition Source 2026](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/2026/01/09/dietary-guidelines-for-americans-2025-2030/). Whether higher intake is actually better for the general population, and the optimum, remain genuinely debated.
- **The standalone value of glycemic index/load.** Useful as a lens, but whether it deserves to anchor guidance — independent of fiber and whole-food status — is still debated.
