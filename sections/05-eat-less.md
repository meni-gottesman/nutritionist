## 05. What to Limit: Ultra-Processed Foods, Sugar, Sodium, Red/Processed Meat, and Alcohol

**Bottom line:** Cut the proven harms — industrial trans fat (to zero), excess sodium, added sugar (especially sugary drinks), processed meat, and alcohol — but the certainty runs from rock-solid (trans fat) to genuinely contested (unprocessed red meat); the same few swaps move most of these dials at once.


If the rest of this guide is about what to add, this section is about the short list of things to subtract. And it really is short. The strongest dietary *harm* signals in the entire literature converge on a handful of factors: industrial trans fats, excess sodium, added/free sugars (especially sugar-sweetened beverages), processed meat, alcohol, and — newer and more contested — ultra-processed foods as a category. The Global Burden of Disease study attributed roughly 11 million deaths in 2017 to dietary risks, led not by exotic toxins but by too much sodium and too few whole grains, fruits, and vegetables [Afshin et al., Lancet 2019](https://www.thelancet.com/article/S0140-6736(19)30041-8/fulltext) ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6899507/)).

The evidence behind these "limit" recommendations is not uniform, and honesty about that is the whole point. At one end sits trans fat: mechanism, randomized lipid trials, and real-world policy experiments all agree, and the right intake is essentially zero. At the other end sits unprocessed red meat, where reasonable scientists genuinely disagree about how much guidance the data support. I flag the strength as we go, because "limit" does not mean the same thing for a known carcinogen as it does for a food whose risk estimate is small and partly confounded.

One organizing idea ties most of this together. The harms here are not about single villainous molecules so much as about *patterns* — liquid sugar that bypasses fullness, a sodium-to-potassium balance tilted the wrong way, foods engineered to be easy to overeat. The good news is that the same handful of swaps (fewer sugary drinks, less processed meat, fewer refined-grain snacks, more whole plant foods) moves nearly all of these dials at once.

### Key evidence

**Industrial trans fats — eliminate them (strong; the clearest causal harm in nutrition).** Artificial trans fat, formed by partially hydrogenating oils, simultaneously raises LDL and lowers HDL — a uniquely atherogenic combination [Mozaffarian et al., NEJM 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035) ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16611951/)); [Harvard Chan / The Nutrition Source](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/what-should-you-eat/fats-and-cholesterol/types-of-fat/transfats/). WHO sets the target at under 1% of total energy and recommends a regulatory cap of 2 g per 100 g of total fat, plus an outright ban on partially hydrogenated oils [WHO 2023](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat). The organization estimates industrial trans fat causes up to roughly 500,000 premature coronary deaths a year, nearly all preventable [WHO 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/23-01-2023-five-billion-people-unprotected-from-trans-fat-leading-to-heart-disease). This is the rare area where mechanism, trial-level lipid data, and policy "natural experiments" line up; by 2023, best-practice elimination policies covered several billion people across 40-plus countries [WHO 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/23-01-2023-five-billion-people-unprotected-from-trans-fat-leading-to-heart-disease). For an individual, this mostly means avoiding products listing "partially hydrogenated oil" — increasingly rare where bans apply.

**Sodium — most people eat about double the target (strong).** WHO recommends under 2,000 mg of sodium (under 5 g of salt) per day [WHO 2023](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/sodium-cvd-adults); the US guidelines say under 2,300 mg [Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8713704/). Global mean intake is roughly twice that — about 4,278 mg/day (≈11 g salt) as of 2021 [WHO 2023](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/sodium-reduction). The sodium–blood-pressure dose-response is established by randomized trials such as DASH-Sodium, where cutting sodium lowered blood pressure in a stepwise fashion [Sacks et al., NEJM 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11136953/), and high sodium was among the leading dietary risk factors for death in GBD 2017 (~3 million deaths, roughly tied with low whole-grain intake) [Afshin et al., Lancet 2019](https://www.thelancet.com/article/S0140-6736(19)30041-8/fulltext) ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6899507/)). The strongest outcome-level proof comes from the SSaSS trial (~21,000 people in rural China, all with a prior stroke or high blood pressure and elevated cardiovascular risk): replacing regular salt with a substitute that was 75% sodium chloride and 25% potassium chloride by mass cut stroke by 14%, major cardiovascular events by 13%, and all-cause death by 12% over about five years, without excess clinical hyperkalemia [SSaSS, NEJM 2021](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2105675). One important limit on generalizing that safety reassurance: the trial excluded people with renal impairment or those taking potassium-sparing diuretics or potassium supplements, so it does not speak to the very groups flagged in the potassium caveat below.

**Adjust it — sodium.** Baseline: **under 2,300 mg/day** (US) or **under 2,000 mg/day** (WHO). The dose-response is roughly linear over the usual intake range: in a 2021 analysis, each ~2,300 mg/day cut lowered systolic blood pressure by ~5.6 mmHg and diastolic by ~2.3 mmHg (≈2.4/1.0 mmHg per 1,000 mg), and the effect is larger in people who are already hypertensive [AHA](https://www.heart.org/en/news/2021/02/15/lower-your-sodium-and-blood-pressure-will-follow). The lower your starting point, the smaller each further cut buys you. Dial it by who you are:
  - **Hypertensive, salt-sensitive, Black, diabetic, or age >50–51 → target <1,500 mg/day** (the AHA "ideal" cap; salt-sensitivity and blood-pressure response to sodium are all higher in these groups) [AHA / Circulation](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/cir.0b013e318279acbf).
  - **Chronic kidney disease → individualized, but typically <2,000 mg/day**; do not add potassium-based salt substitutes without labs and clinician sign-off (see potassium caveat).
  - **Endurance athletes / heavy or "salty" sweaters → you may need MORE, not less, during and around heavy exercise.** Sweat sodium runs ~0.5–1.5 g/L (≈20–65 mmol/L; salty sweaters exceed 2 g/L), and sweat rates of 1–2 L/h are common — so 2–4 h of hard exercise can shed 2,000–6,000 mg sodium. Replace ~300–700 mg/h for sessions over an hour, up to ~700–1,500 mg/h for salty sweaters, on top of (not instead of) the everyday limit [STYRKR review](https://styrkr.com/en-us/blogs/training-and-nutrition-hub/how-much-sodium-do-you-lose-in-sweat). Worked example: a 70-kg runner losing 1.0 g/L over 2 h at 1.2 L/h sweat loses ≈2,400 mg sodium — replacing ~50–75% means ~1,200–1,800 mg of added intake that day, which sits outside the resting cap.

**Potassium — the counterweight (strong).** Sodium's harm is partly a story of *balance*. WHO recommends at least 3,510 mg/day (90 mmol/day) of potassium from food, and higher intake lowers blood pressure and offsets sodium's pressor effect [WHO](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/potassium-cvd-adults); most populations fall well short of that target [Global mean potassium intake, PMC 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10349712/). The lever is more fruits, vegetables, and legumes — and, for many, potassium-enriched salt substitutes (the SSaSS mechanism). One serious caveat: potassium supplements and substitutes can be dangerous in chronic kidney disease or with certain blood-pressure medications, so this is a "from food first, check with a clinician otherwise" recommendation.

**Adjust it — potassium.** Baseline: **≥3,510 mg/day from food** (WHO). The US adequate-intake reference is in the same range but sex-split: ~3,400 mg/day for men and ~2,600 mg/day for women. The dependency that flips the sign is kidney function and certain drugs:
  - **CKD stage G3b–G5, or eGFR <45, or anyone on ACE inhibitors / ARBs / potassium-sparing diuretics → do NOT chase a high target or use KCl salt substitutes;** potassium is often *restricted* (commonly ~2,000–3,000 mg/day, individualized by serum potassium and labs). The SSaSS safety data explicitly exclude these people.
  - **Otherwise (normal kidney function) → prefer whole-food sources:** a medium banana ≈ 420 mg, a cup of cooked spinach ≈ 800–1,180 mg, a medium baked potato with skin ≈ 925–1,060 mg, a cup of cooked white beans ≈ 1,000–1,190 mg. Two to three such servings closes most of the gap to 3,510 mg.

**Added/free sugars — under 10% of energy, ideally under 5% (strong recommendation, moderate evidence).** WHO strongly advises keeping free sugars below 10% of total energy, with a conditional further reduction below 5% [WHO 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK285525/). It's worth being precise about the evidence: the strong rating rests largely on moderate-quality data for *dental caries*, and the under-5% target on very-low-quality data from ecological/"natural experiment" studies — the body-weight link runs through simple energy balance, since free sugars add calories without satiety. At 2,000 kcal, 10% is about 50 g/day and 5% about 25 g. US guidelines similarly cap added sugars at under 10% of daily calories [Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8713704/). "Free sugars" excludes the intrinsic sugar in whole fruit and the lactose in plain dairy — whole fruit is not the target.

**Adjust it — free/added sugars.** Baseline: **<10% of energy (better target <5%)**. Because the cap is a *percentage*, the gram limit scales with your calorie intake (sugar = 4 kcal/g, so grams ≈ kcal × 0.10 ÷ 4 = **kcal ÷ 40** for the 10% line, and **kcal ÷ 80** for the 5% line):
  - **1,500 kcal → <37 g/day (10%), ideally <19 g (5%).**
  - **2,000 kcal → <50 g/day, ideally <25 g.**
  - **2,500 kcal → <62 g/day, ideally <31 g.**
  - **3,000 kcal (large/active person) → <75 g/day, ideally <37 g.**
  For scale, one 12-oz cola ≈ 39 g free sugar — by itself ~78% of the 10% cap and over 1.5× the 5% target at 2,000 kcal, which is why a single daily soda is the highest-leverage cut. Tighten toward the 5% line if you have, or are at risk of, dental caries, type 2 diabetes, or non-alcoholic fatty liver disease; the 10% line is the floor, not the goal.

**Sugar-sweetened beverages — limit these first (moderate, observational).** Liquid sugar is the most robustly harmful sugar source, plausibly because it bypasses fullness signaling and delivers a fast glycemic load. In dose-response meta-analyses, each additional daily serving is associated with roughly +27% type 2 diabetes risk and about +9% cardiovascular risk; among people who already have type 2 diabetes, each extra daily SSB tracks with about +8% all-cause mortality [Ma et al., BMJ 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37076174/) (15,486 adults with type 2 diabetes in the NHS and HPFS cohorts; highest-vs-lowest SSB intake HR 1.20, 95% CI 1.04–1.37) [Meng et al., Nutrients 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8402166/); [Harvard Chan 2023](https://hsph.harvard.edu/news/sugar-sweetened-beverages-linked-with-increased-risk-of-premature-death-for-people-with-type-2-diabetes/). The diabetes and weight signals are consistent enough that SSBs are a near-universal "cut first" target and the main rationale behind sugar taxes.

**Adjust it — sugar-sweetened beverages.** Baseline: **0 servings/day is the goal; the harm scales per serving.** One "serving" ≈ 12 oz / 355 mL of regular soda (~39 g sugar). Each additional daily serving tracks with roughly **+27% type 2 diabetes** and **+9% cardiovascular** risk in dose-response meta-analyses, so the dial is essentially "how many daily servings do you currently drink, and how many can you displace." Two or three sodas a day is not "double" the one-soda risk in raw terms but it compounds, and the marginal benefit of cutting is largest for the heaviest users.
  - **Already have type 2 diabetes → each extra daily SSB ≈ +8% all-cause mortality**, so cutting to zero is higher-leverage here than in the general population [Ma et al., BMJ 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37076174/).
  - **100% fruit juice is a partial substitute, not a free pass:** treat ≤120–150 mL/day (about half a cup) as the ceiling; its sugar still counts toward the free-sugar gram limit above.
  - **Diet/zero-sugar swaps** remove the sugar/calorie load entirely and are a reasonable transition tool; plain water, unsweetened coffee/tea are the endpoint.

**Processed meat — an IARC Group 1 carcinogen (strong).** Bacon, ham, hot dogs, sausages, and deli meats — preserved by smoking, curing, salting, or nitrite/nitrate addition — are classified as "carcinogenic to humans" for colorectal cancer, based on an IARC review of over 800 studies [IARC 2015](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf). One crucial nuance: Group 1 reflects the *strength* of the evidence, not the *size* of the risk; processed meat is nowhere near tobacco in magnitude. IARC's dose-response estimate is roughly +18% colorectal cancer risk per 50 g/day [IARC 2015](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf), and a 2025 meta-analysis separately found a 21% higher colorectal cancer risk (HR ≈ 1.21, 95% CI 1.14–1.28) comparing higher vs. lower processed-meat intake [GeroScience 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12181564/). Mechanisms include N-nitroso compounds, heme iron, and amines formed during cooking.

**Adjust it — processed meat.** Baseline: **0 g/day is the ideal; there is no established "safe" threshold, only "less is better."** The dose-response is roughly **+18% colorectal-cancer relative risk per 50 g/day** consumed daily (≈ one hot dog, two bacon rashers, or three slices of deli ham) [IARC 2015](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf). Keep the framing honest: that 18% is a *relative* increase on a colorectal-cancer baseline that is modest in absolute terms, so the harm is real but far smaller than tobacco. Scale it by frequency, not just amount:
  - **Daily processed meat (≥50 g/day) → the priority cut;** moving to ~once/week roughly removes most of the modeled excess risk.
  - **Higher baseline risk (family history of colorectal cancer, Lynch syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, age >50) → push toward 0**, since the same relative risk applies to a larger absolute baseline.
  - **WCRF/AICR target: "little, if any" processed meat** — effectively occasional, not a weekly quota [WCRF/AICR](https://www.wcrf.org/research-policy/evidence-for-our-recommendations/limit-red-processed-meat/).

**Unprocessed red meat — smaller risk, genuinely contested guidance (contested).** Red meat is IARC Group 2A, "probably carcinogenic," with a smaller signal — a 2025 meta-analysis found about +15% colorectal cancer risk (HR ≈ 1.15, 95% CI 1.10–1.21) comparing higher vs. lower intake [GeroScience 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12181564/). But the *dietary advice* is honestly debated. The 2019 NutriRECS reviews graded the evidence low-certainty and issued a weak recommendation to continue current intake [NutriRECS, Annals 2019](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M19-1621) (the lead author's prior industry funding from the food-industry-backed International Life Sciences Institute and undisclosed ties to meat-industry-funded Texas A&M AgriLife later prompted a published *Annals* disclosure correction [Annals correction 2019](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/L19-0822)), prompting strong objections from Harvard, the AHA, and others over methods and conflicts of interest [Harvard Chan / The Nutrition Source 2019](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/2019/09/30/flawed-guidelines-red-processed-meat/). Most guidelines still advise limiting red meat, for combined health and environmental reasons — the WCRF/AICR recommends no more than about three portions of red meat per week and little, if any, processed meat [WCRF/AICR](https://www.wcrf.org/research-policy/evidence-for-our-recommendations/limit-red-processed-meat/), and the EAT-Lancet planetary health diet likewise frames low red-meat intake as both a health and planetary goal [Harvard Chan / EAT-Lancet](https://hsph.harvard.edu/news/this-dietary-pattern-could-save-lives-and-the-planet/).

**Adjust it — unprocessed red meat.** Baseline: **≤350–500 g cooked per week** (the WCRF/AICR cap of ~3 portions; one portion ≈ 90 g cooked / ~130 g raw — a small chop or a deck-of-cards serving). That's roughly **≤500 g cooked/week → about 700–750 g raw/week**, or ~1–2 modest servings spread across 3 days, not daily. The dependency is mostly how much certainty you demand and your other risk factors:
  - **Colorectal-cancer risk signal ≈ +15% relative risk** at higher vs. lower intake — smaller than processed meat and contested — so for *cancer alone* the data don't compel going below the WCRF cap [GeroScience 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12181564/).
  - **Planetary/CVD-pattern reasons → lower still:** EAT-Lancet's reference figure is ~98 g/week of red meat (≈ one ~100 g serving), an order of magnitude under the cancer-only cap, driven by environmental plus cardiometabolic goals.
  - **Iron-deficiency / pregnancy / menstruating with low ferritin → the floor isn't zero:** red meat is a dense source of bioavailable heme iron, so a few portions/week within the 350–500 g cap can be a feature, not a bug.

**Alcohol — zero is safest; any "limit" is a ceiling, not a goal (strong for harm, contested at low doses).** WHO's position is that *no level of alcohol consumption is safe* for health, since risk for several cancers begins at the first drink [WHO 2023](https://www.who.int/europe/news/item/04-01-2023-no-level-of-alcohol-consumption-is-safe-for-our-health), echoing the GBD finding that the level minimizing total health loss is zero [Lancet 2018](https://www.thelancet.com/article/S0140-6736(18)31571-X/fulltext). The earlier "J-curve" (light drinking apparently protective for heart disease) is now widely attributed to confounding and "sick-quitter" bias in the abstainer reference group. Where guidelines do give numbers, they are explicitly *upper limits for people who already drink*, not encouragements: the 2020–2025 US Dietary Guidelines defined moderate drinking as **≤1 standard drink/day for women and ≤2/day for men** (the 2025–2030 update dropped the specific numbers in favor of "consume less alcohol for better health") [Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/alcohol/info). A US "standard drink" is 14 g of pure ethanol ≈ 12 oz beer (5%), 5 oz wine (12%), or 1.5 oz spirits (40%).

**Adjust it — alcohol.** Baseline: **0 drinks/day is the health-optimal target.** If you do drink, the *ceiling* (not goal) is **≤1 standard drink/day for women, ≤2 for men** (≤14 g and ≤28 g ethanol/day). Adjust the ceiling down toward zero for:
  - **Pregnancy, trying to conceive, or breastfeeding → 0** (no known safe amount).
  - **History of or risk for breast/colorectal/liver/esophageal cancer, liver disease, or on interacting medication → 0**; breast-cancer risk rises measurably even within the "moderate" range (~+7–10% per ~10 g/day).
  - **Personal or family history of alcohol use disorder → 0.**
  - **Note country variation:** some national guidelines are stricter — e.g., Canada (2023) frames 1–2 drinks/week as "low-risk" and ≥7/week as "increasingly high-risk," well below the US ceiling. Treat the US ≤1/≤2 numbers as a permissive cap, not a recommendation to drink up to it.

**Ultra-processed foods — consistent associations, causation still emerging (moderate, mostly observational).** NOVA Group 4 foods — industrial formulations of extracted substances plus additives, like packaged snacks, mass-produced bread, reconstituted meats, and instant meals — are linked across dozens of cohorts to higher mortality and cardiometabolic disease. A 2024 BMJ umbrella review and a 2024–2025 dose-response meta-analysis (18 cohorts, ~1.15 million people) found roughly +10% all-cause mortality per 10% increment of UPF energy, about +15% comparing highest to lowest intake, and elevated cardiovascular and coronary risk [Lane et al., BMJ 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38418082/); [Syst Rev 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11874696/). The honest caveat: most of this is observational, and residual confounding by overall diet quality is a live concern. The strongest causal hint is the Hall NIH metabolic-ward trial, where people on a UPF diet — matched for calories, sugar, fat, sodium, and fiber — spontaneously ate about 500 kcal/day more and gained weight [Hall et al., Cell Metabolism 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31269427/).

**Adjust it — ultra-processed foods.** There's no validated milligram threshold, so the dial is *share of energy from NOVA Group 4*. The dose-response is roughly **+10% all-cause mortality per +10-percentage-point of UPF energy** [Lane et al., BMJ 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38418082/). Baseline aim: **keep UPF below ~10–20% of calories** (vs. ~50%+ in a typical US/UK diet, where the worst risk sits). Worked example: dropping from 50% to 30% of a 2,000-kcal diet is a 20-point cut ≈ a ~20% lower modeled mortality association — and, per Hall, can passively remove ~500 kcal/day of overeating, ≈ 0.45 kg/week of fat loss if sustained (calorie math: −500 kcal/day × 7 = −3,500 kcal/week ≈ 0.45 kg; see §20). Prioritize by sub-type, since not all UPF is equal: cut sugary drinks, reconstituted/processed meats, and refined-grain snacks first; whole-grain packaged bread or plain yogurt are far weaker signals.

### What it depends on

- **Which factor.** "Limit" is a directive for trans fat and processed meat; for unprocessed red meat it is closer to "modest restraint within reasonable doubt."
- **Health status.** Existing hypertension, cardiovascular disease, or diabetes sharply raises the payoff from cutting sodium and SSBs, and lowers the sodium target to <1,500 mg/day. Chronic kidney disease flips the potassium advice — substitutes and supplements can be harmful — and individualizes the sodium target. Heavy-sweating athletes are the rare group who may need *more* sodium around exercise (~0.3–1.5 g/h).
- **Baseline intake.** The biggest gains come from the heaviest users; trimming an occasional treat matters far less than displacing a daily soda or daily deli sandwich.
- **Definitions matter.** Free sugars exclude whole fruit and plain-dairy lactose; "processed meat" (cured/preserved) is a different and stronger category than fresh red meat.
- **Diet as a whole.** UPF and red-meat risk estimates are entangled with overall pattern — someone eating UPFs amid an otherwise whole-food diet is not the cohort average.

### Practical takeaways

- **Treat sugar-sweetened beverages as the single highest-leverage cut.** Water, unsweetened drinks, or coffee/tea displace the clearest sugar harm.
- **Aim under 2,300 mg sodium (under 1,500 mg if hypertensive, salt-sensitive, Black, diabetic, or over ~50) and shift the balance** by cooking more, reading labels, and considering a potassium-based salt substitute — unless you have kidney disease or take affected medications. Heavy-sweating athletes replace sodium around exercise on top of this.
- **Make processed meat occasional, not daily.** This is the firmest "less is better" call in the section; bacon and deli meats are the priority, not fresh meat.
- **Keep free sugars near 50 g/day, ideally toward 25 g** (at 2,000 kcal; scale as kcal ÷ 40 for the 10% line, kcal ÷ 80 for the 5% line) — whole fruit doesn't count.
- **If you drink alcohol, treat ≤1 drink/day (women) or ≤2 (men) as a ceiling, not a target;** zero is health-optimal, and 0 is the right number in pregnancy or with elevated breast/liver/colorectal-cancer risk.
- **Use the UPF label as a shortcut, not dogma:** fewer packaged snacks, sodas, and reconstituted-meat products, more intact whole foods, captures most of the benefit even if the construct is still being litigated.
- **Check ingredient lists for "partially hydrogenated oil"** and avoid it; where bans apply, you'll rarely find it.

### Unsettled or debated

- **Unprocessed red meat guidance.** Whether the evidence justifies anything stronger than "current intake is probably fine for cancer risk" is a real, public scientific disagreement — distinct from processed meat, where it is not.
- **Is "ultra-processed" itself causal, or a proxy?** The associations are strong and the Hall trial is suggestive, but whether the *processing* drives harm — versus accompanying sugar, salt, fat, energy density, and lower diet quality — is not yet settled. Long-term RCTs are still lacking.
- **How low to push sodium for everyone.** The benefit of reduction is well established at high intakes; whether the very lowest intakes add further benefit for already-healthy, normotensive people is still argued at the margins.
- **The alcohol "J-curve."** Whether light drinking is net-neutral or net-harmful for all-cause outcomes is contested: cancer risk clearly starts at zero, but some age-stratified GBD analyses still suggest small cardiovascular offsets in older adults. The methodological critique (sick-quitter and abstainer-confounding bias) has pushed the consensus toward "less is better, zero is safest," but no RCT settles it.
