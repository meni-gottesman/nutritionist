## 19. Food Safety and Cooking Methods

**Bottom line:** The cooking method and dose — not the food — drive risk: favor gentle moist heat, aim for golden-not-charred, and own a thermometer for the acute risk; keep mercury, pesticides, and arsenic in proportion and don't let contaminant fear cut your fish or produce.


How you cook matters almost as much as what you cook. The same fish can be a near-ideal meal or a source of avoidable carcinogens depending on whether it's gently poached or charred black over a flame. This section covers two kinds of risk: the slow, chronic ones from cooking chemistry (heat-formed compounds, oxidized oils) and the acute one from foodborne pathogens. It also puts a few much-hyped "contaminants" — mercury, pesticides, arsenic, plastics — into honest proportion.

A theme runs through all of it: the dose and the method, not the food itself, usually matter. Grilling isn't off-limits; repeated charring is the problem. Fish is one of the healthiest foods available; a few high-mercury species during pregnancy are the exception. The goal is to lower real risks with a handful of habits — not to add anxiety to every meal.

The acute risk deserves top billing because it's the one that sends people to the hospital. The CDC estimates roughly 48 million foodborne illnesses, 128,000 hospitalizations, and 3,000 deaths in the US each year [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/about/index.html), nearly all preventable with four simple practices and a thermometer.

### Key evidence

**High-heat cooking of meat forms HCAs and PAHs (moderate; mechanism strong, human-cancer link suggestive).** When muscle meat — beef, pork, poultry, fish — is cooked at high temperature, two families of mutagens form. Heterocyclic amines (HCAs) arise when amino acids, sugars, and creatine react above ~300°F (150°C); polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) form when fat drips onto flames and the smoke deposits on the surface [NCI](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/diet/cooked-meats-fact-sheet). Both are mutagenic and carcinogenic in animals; human epidemiology links frequent well-done/charred meat to colorectal, pancreatic, and prostate cancer, though it can't fully separate the compounds from the meat itself (cross-ref §05, §14). The NCI's own mitigation advice is concrete: avoid direct flame, flip frequently, microwave-precook to cut grilling time, and discard charred portions [NCI](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/diet/cooked-meats-fact-sheet).

**Marinating measurably reduces HCAs (moderate).** Marinating for at least 30 minutes — especially in marinades rich in polyphenolic antioxidants from herbs and spices — has cut total HCA formation by 50–90% in controlled grilling studies [Smith et al., J Food Sci 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19241593/). The antioxidants suppress the free-radical chemistry that builds HCAs.

> **Adjust it — grilling-mitigation stack.** Each habit is independent and roughly additive against HCA/PAH load: **marinate ≥30 min** in an herb/spice (rosemary, thyme, garlic, turmeric) or acidic base (−50% to −90% total HCAs, with rosemary extract alone >−90%); **flip every ~1 min** instead of once (frequent flipping substantially lowers HCAs by holding surface temperature down, per NCI); **microwave-precook ~2 min and pour off the juices** before grilling (≈−90% HCAs per NCI, by shedding grill time and the creatine/precursor pool); **avoid flare-ups and trim dripping fat** to limit PAHs; **discard charred/blackened bits**. *Scaling:* benefit tracks total high-char meat frequency — at **≤1×/week** the absolute risk is small and one or two habits suffice; at **daily** char (e.g., habitual barbecue or pan-blackening) stack all of them. *Depends on:* higher-risk colorectal profile (family history, age >50, prior adenoma — cross-ref §09) → treat well-done/charred red meat as a several-×/month ceiling, not a daily food.

**Starchy foods cooked dry above 120°C form acrylamide (moderate; IARC Group 2A "probable").** Frying, roasting, and baking starchy foods (potatoes, bread, crackers, coffee) above 120°C drives the Maillard reaction between asparagine and sugars, producing acrylamide — the browning chemistry that also makes food tasty. IARC rates it a probable human carcinogen, and EFSA concluded that dietary acrylamide potentially raises cancer risk for consumers across all age groups, though the human data are limited and animal-based [EFSA 2015](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/150604). Practical control: cook to golden, not brown; soak raw potatoes before frying; store potatoes outside the fridge (cold storage raises sugars and thus acrylamide).

> **Adjust it — acrylamide control.** *Baseline:* the reaction effectively switches on above **120°C (250°F)** and climbs steeply with browning, so "golden, not brown" is the single dial — visibly browner = exponentially more acrylamide. *How it scales:* soaking cut raw potato in water **15–30 min** before frying/roasting (then drying, and frying only to light gold) cuts acrylamide ~**40%** (≈48% with a 2-hour soak); toasting bread to light-gold rather than dark cuts it severalfold; pantry (not fridge) potato storage avoids the cold-induced sugar rise. *Depends on:* **children** carry the highest exposure **per kg body weight** (EFSA) — go lighter on fries/crisps/over-toasted starches for kids; **heavy daily fried-starch or dark-toast eaters** get the most absolute benefit from dialing browning back; occasional golden fries are a non-issue.

**Dry high heat creates dietary AGEs (limited–moderate).** Advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) form when proteins and sugars react under dry heat; grilling, roasting, and frying can raise food AGE content 10- to 100-fold over the raw (uncooked) state, and several-fold over moist-cooked (boiled/steamed) food [Uribarri et al., J Acad Nutr Diet 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3704564/). Dietary AGEs are associated with oxidative stress and inflammation, and small trials show moist, lower-temperature cooking lowers circulating AGEs — the same lesson as the HCA and acrylamide story.

> **Adjust it — dietary AGEs.** *Baseline:* the cooking *method* dominates over the *food* — broiling/grilling/frying generates roughly **10–100×** the AGEs of boiling/steaming/poaching the same item, and animal foods high in fat and protein (fatty meats, full-fat cheese, butter) are the highest-AGE foods. *How it scales:* switching a dish from dry high-heat to moist low-heat, plus acidic marinades (lemon, vinegar), can cut its AGE load by roughly half or more. *Depends on:* this lever matters most for those with **diabetes, CKD, or established cardiovascular disease** (cross-ref §09), where lower dietary-AGE patterns have shown the clearest benefit; for metabolically healthy people the evidence is weaker (see *Unsettled*), so treat it as a free side-benefit of cooking gentler rather than a standalone mandate.

**Gentle, moist methods reduce all three — and don't char.** Steaming, boiling, stewing, poaching, sous-vide, and slow-cooking stay near or below water's boiling point, so they form far fewer HCAs, PAHs, acrylamide, and AGEs. This is the single highest-leverage cooking habit: favor moist, lower-temperature methods for everyday cooking and save searing/charring for occasional use.

**Nutrient retention cuts both ways (moderate).** Cooking isn't universally destructive. Heat ruptures plant cell walls and makes some nutrients *more* available — cooked tomato products deliver more absorbable lycopene than raw, and a little oil sharply boosts carotenoid (lycopene, beta-carotene, lutein) absorption [Lycopene review, PMC 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7996133/). The trade-off is water-soluble vitamins: in a head-to-head study, boiling was the harshest on vitamin C and microwaving the gentlest, with steaming in between — less water and shorter time preserve more [Lee et al., PMC 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6049644/). Microwaving, despite its reputation, is among the more nutrient-protective methods.

> **Adjust it — method should follow the food.** *Default lever:* shorter time + less water + lower temperature retains the most heat-/water-labile nutrients; boiling can leach **~50%+ of vitamin C** from greens, while microwaving/steaming the same vegetable typically retains far more. *When to flip the rule:* for **carotenoid-rich foods** (tomato, carrot, red pepper, leafy greens), light cooking *plus* **~3–5 g added fat** *raises* usable lycopene/beta-carotene several-fold — heat and oil are a feature, not a cost, here. *Depends on the target nutrient:* maximizing **vitamin C / folate / glucosinolates** → steam or microwave, minimal water, eat soon; maximizing **lycopene / beta-carotene / lutein** → cook with a little oil; **boiling water-soluble vitamins away** is only "lost" if you discard the cooking water — keep it for soups/stocks to recover much of it.

**Oils: respect smoke points and don't reuse oil repeatedly (moderate).** Heating oil past its smoke point degrades it and produces irritants and PAHs; repeatedly reheated frying oil shifts its fatty-acid profile unfavorably — trans-fat content rises and atherogenicity/thrombogenicity indices worsen, a more cardiovascular-adverse fat — across successive frying cycles [Effects of Repeated Heating, PMC 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8774349/). Match oil smoke point to cooking heat, and avoid reusing the same oil more than a couple of times.

> **Adjust it — oil choice and reuse.** *Baseline:* keep cooking temperature **below the oil's smoke point**, and reuse frying oil **no more than 2–3 cycles**, discarding sooner if it darkens, smells acrid, or foams. *How it scales (match heat to smoke point):* refined avocado **~270°C (520°F)** and refined/light olive **~240°C (465°F)** suit high-heat searing/deep-frying; extra-virgin olive **~190–210°C (375–410°F)** and butter **~150°C (300°F)** are for low-medium sauté/baking only; degradation markers (trans fats, polar compounds, atherogenicity index) climb with each reheat cycle. *Depends on:* **deep-frying** (oil reused across batches) → filter and cap at ~2–3 uses and never top-up old with new; **a single sauté in fresh oil** → reuse is a non-issue; **dyslipidemia / established CVD** (cross-ref §09) → minimize repeatedly-fried foods entirely, since the fat shifts toward a more atherogenic, trans-richer profile.

**Foodborne illness: four practices plus safe temperatures (strong).** The CDC/USDA framework is **clean, separate, cook, chill** [FoodSafety.gov](https://www.foodsafety.gov/keep-food-safe/4-steps-to-food-safety). A verified internal temperature is what actually kills pathogens: poultry **165°F (74°C)**; ground meats **160°F (71°C)**; whole beef, pork, veal, and lamb (steaks, roasts, chops) **145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest**; fish and seafood **145°F (63°C)** (or until opaque and flaking); egg dishes **160°F (71°C)** [FoodSafety.gov](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/safe-minimum-internal-temperatures). Keep the fridge **≤40°F (4°C)** and never leave perishable food in the 40–140°F "danger zone" over **2 hours** (1 hour above 90°F) [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/prevention/index.html). Refrigerate leftovers within that window, use them within **3–4 days**, and reheat to **165°F (74°C)** (bring soups, sauces, and gravies to a rolling boil) [FSIS](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/leftovers-and-food-safety). Higher-risk foods include raw/undercooked poultry, eggs, and seafood, unpasteurized milk/juice, raw sprouts, and deli meats; higher-risk people are pregnant women, young children, adults over 65, and the immunocompromised.

> **Adjust it — safe temperatures and timing.**
> - **Baseline (healthy adults):** thermometer targets above; danger-zone clock **2 h** in the 40–140°F zone, **1 h** when ambient is **>90°F (32°C)**; leftovers **3–4 days** at **≤40°F (4°C)**.
> - **How it scales:** pathogen kill is a time–temperature trade-off, so a target a few degrees low just needs hold time, not panic — a 7-log *Salmonella* reduction is reached **instantly at 165°F (74°C)** but also at lower temperatures held longer (FSIS publishes validated lower-temperature/longer-hold combinations down to ~130°F for whole-muscle product) [FSIS Appendix A](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media_file/2021-12/Appendix-A.pdf). Treat **165°F** as the no-timing-required shortcut for home cooks. Cold storage buys time the other way: bacterial growth slows sharply as you move below 40°F, which is why the fridge target is a ceiling, not a goal.
> - **What it depends on (numeric):** **pregnant / age >65 / immunocompromised** → treat the danger-zone clock as **half** (≤1 h at room temp), eat leftovers within **1–2 days**, and reheat deli meat and hot dogs to **steaming 165°F** to kill *Listeria*; **>90°F/32°C ambient** (summer picnic, hot car) → **1 h** not 2 h; **freezer storage** extends quality (frozen food stays safe indefinitely at 0°F, but quality degrades) — use most cooked leftovers within **3–4 months** of freezing; **whole-cut beef/pork rare-preference** is acceptable only at **145°F + 3-min rest** (the rest delivers further log-reduction), but **ground meat has no safe "rare"** — surface pathogens are mixed throughout, so **160°F** is non-negotiable.

**Contaminants, in proportion:**
- **Mercury in fish.** Most fish are net-beneficial; a few predators concentrate methylmercury. FDA/EPA advise pregnant and breastfeeding people to eat **8–12 oz/week (2–3 servings of 4 oz) from the "Best Choices" list** (salmon, sardines, shrimp, pollock, canned light tuna, tilapia, trout, catfish, Atlantic mackerel, anchovies, herring) — *or* **1 serving/week of a "Good Choices" fish** (e.g., albacore/yellowfin tuna, halibut, mahi-mahi) and nothing else that week — while **avoiding the 7 highest-mercury species**: shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish (Gulf of Mexico), marlin, orange roughy, and bigeye tuna [FDA/EPA](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish) (cross-ref §04, §16).
  > **Adjust it — fish servings by group.**
  > - **General healthy adults:** **2–3 servings/week** (≈8–12 oz total), emphasizing low-mercury, omega-3-rich choices (salmon, sardines, herring); no upper "avoid" list applies but keep high-mercury predators to ≈1 serving/month at most.
  > - **Pregnant / breastfeeding / planning pregnancy:** hold to **8–12 oz/week** of Best Choices, **strictly zero** of the 7 avoid-species (methylmercury crosses the placenta and concentrates in the fetus), and cap albacore ("white") tuna at **≤6 oz/week**.
  > - **Children, by age (serving size scales with body size):** **1–3 yr → 1 oz** per serving; **4–7 yr → 2 oz**; **8–10 yr → 3 oz**; **11+ → 4 oz (adult)**; **2 servings/week** from Best Choices.
  > - **Local/recreational catch:** if no advisory exists, limit to **1 serving (4 oz)/week** and eat no other fish that week (waterbody mercury varies 10–100×).
- **Pesticide residues.** Regulators consistently find the large majority of sampled produce below safety tolerances, and EFSA and FDA both conclude the benefits of eating fruits and vegetables far outweigh residue risk [EFSA](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/news/pesticide-residues-food-latest-data-released). Washing under running water reduces residues; don't let residue fear cut your produce intake.
  > **Adjust it — pesticide residues.** *Baseline:* rinse all produce **20–30 sec under running water** (rubbing firm produce) before eating — this removes a meaningful fraction of surface residue and dirt/microbes; soap and commercial "produce washes" are unnecessary and not FDA-recommended. *How it scales / depends on:* the **net health benefit of eating more fruits and vegetables far outweighs residue risk** at any realistic intake, so the priority order is *quantity first, sourcing second*. If budget is limited and you want to spend it, prioritize organic only for the few thin-skinned high-residue items (e.g., berries, leafy greens, peppers — the EWG "Dirty Dozen" pattern) and skip it for thick-skinned/low-residue produce (avocado, onion, citrus); for **pregnancy/young children** the same rule holds — washing plus variety beats fear-driven avoidance.
- **Arsenic in rice.** Rice takes up inorganic arsenic from soil. Cooking it in excess water (**6–10 parts water to 1 part rice**) then draining removes **40–60%** of the inorganic arsenic (≈40% for polished long-grain, ≈50% for brown, ≈60% for parboiled), whereas rinsing alone has minimal effect on arsenic, per the FDA; the trade-off is that draining also strips **50–70%** of the enriched iron, folate, niacin, and thiamin from polished/parboiled rice [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/environmental-contaminants-food/what-you-can-do-limit-exposure-arsenic). Varying grains (oats, barley, quinoa) lowers exposure, which matters most for infants — the FDA's action level for inorganic arsenic in infant rice cereal is **100 ppb** [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/environmental-contaminants-food/what-you-can-do-limit-exposure-arsenic).
  > **Adjust it — rice and arsenic.**
  > - **Baseline (average adult, varied diet):** rice once or twice a week needs no special handling; the excess-water-and-drain method is optional.
  > - **How it scales:** exposure is roughly linear in rice frequency × per-serving arsenic; brown rice runs **~80% higher** in inorganic arsenic than white (arsenic concentrates in the bran), and parboiled/excess-water cooking cuts it most (≈60%).
  > - **What it depends on (numeric):** **infants/toddlers on rice cereal** → choose products at/under the **100 ppb** action level and rotate to oat/barley cereals (brain-development window, low body weight); **frequent rice eaters (≥1×/day) or rice-heavy diets (some South/East Asian patterns)** → use the **6–10:1 drain method** and favor lower-arsenic varieties (California white basmati and US sushi rice run ~50% lower; non-basmati brown and rice grown in AR/LA/TX run highest); **pregnancy** → cap brown-rice frequency and diversify grains. If you drain-cook to lower arsenic, take folate/iron from other foods or a supplement to offset the 50–70% nutrient loss.
- **Plastics/BPA and nitrates (brief, non-alarmist).** Avoid microwaving food in plastic and prefer glass/stainless for hot or fatty foods; regulators consider typical exposures low-risk, but the precaution is cheap. Dietary nitrate from vegetables is not the same risk as nitrites in processed meat (cross-ref §05) — vegetable nitrate appears largely benign.

### What it depends on

- **Who's eating.** Pregnancy, infancy, older age, and immunosuppression raise the stakes on both pathogens and mercury/arsenic; healthy adults have much more margin (cross-ref §16).
- **Frequency.** Occasional grilling or fried food is trivial; daily charred meat or daily reused-oil frying is where chronic risk accrues.
- **The food.** Cooking helps tomatoes and carrots but costs vitamin C in greens — method should follow the food.
- **Baseline diet quality.** These are refinements on top of the big levers (§04, §05), not substitutes for them.

### Practical takeaways

- **Cook gentler by default:** steam, boil, stew, poach, or slow-cook for everyday meals; reserve high char for occasional use, and scrape off blackened bits.
- **When you grill or fry meat:** marinate first, flip often, avoid open flame and flare-ups, and consider microwave pre-cooking to shorten grill time.
- **Aim for golden, not brown** on potatoes, toast, and baked starches; store potatoes in the pantry, not the fridge.
- **Buy a $10 food thermometer** and hit 165°F poultry / 160°F ground meat (and egg dishes) / 145°F + 3-min rest for whole cuts and fish; keep the fridge ≤40°F, follow the 2-hour rule (1 hour if >90°F), eat leftovers within 3–4 days, and reheat them to 165°F. High-risk eaters: halve the danger-zone clock and use leftovers within 1–2 days.
- **Don't reuse frying oil more than 2–3 times;** match oil smoke point to cooking heat (high heat → refined avocado/light olive; low heat → EVOO/butter).
- **Eat fish 2–3×/week** (≈8–12 oz), favoring low-mercury, omega-3-rich choices; in pregnancy/breastfeeding hold to 8–12 oz/week of Best Choices and strictly avoid the 7 high-mercury species; children's servings scale by age (1 oz at 1–3 yr up to 4 oz at 11+).
- **Wash produce, skip the fear** — pesticide residues don't justify eating fewer fruits and vegetables. Cook rice in excess water and vary your grains.

### Unsettled or debated

- **How much heat-formed compounds matter at real-world doses.** The mechanisms (HCAs, PAHs, acrylamide, AGEs) are solid, but quantifying how much typical home cooking raises an individual's cancer risk is genuinely uncertain — the human epidemiology is observational and confounded by overall meat and diet patterns (cross-ref §14).
- **Dietary AGEs as an independent risk factor.** Whether *food* AGEs (versus those the body forms internally from high blood sugar) meaningfully drive disease in humans is still actively debated, with mostly short-term trial evidence.
