## 06. Dietary Patterns: What the Evidence Says (Mediterranean, DASH, Plant-Based, and More)

**Bottom line:** Only Mediterranean (cardiovascular events) and DASH (blood pressure) rest on strong trial evidence; everything else leans on observational data — but beneath the branding they share one core, so pick a Tier-1 pattern you'll keep and match it to your goal.


Nutrition science has largely abandoned the old habit of judging diets one nutrient at a time. We don't eat grams of saturated fat or milligrams of potassium; we eat meals, and the foods in those meals interact in ways isolated nutrients can't capture. So the modern question is about *patterns* — the whole shape of what you eat over years. That reframing is also where the branding wars live: Mediterranean, DASH, MIND, paleo, keto, vegan, Nordic. It's worth separating the marketing from what the evidence supports.

Here's the punchline up front. Two patterns rest on genuinely strong trial evidence: the **Mediterranean diet** (one large hard-outcome cardiovascular RCT) and **DASH** (a stack of controlled-feeding trials for blood pressure). Almost everything else — MIND, plant-based, Nordic, low-carb — leans mostly on observational cohorts and trials of risk factors (cholesterol, weight, glucose) rather than trials of actual disease and death. That distinction matters enormously, because observational signals in nutrition have a track record of *not* surviving rigorous randomized testing.

The reassuring part: beneath the branding, the best-scoring patterns converge on the same core — mostly minimally-processed plants (vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts, seeds), plus healthy unsaturated fats (olive oil, plant oils, fish), modest or no red and processed meat, and little added sugar, sodium, or refined starch. The major bodies agree on that core: the US Dietary Guidelines for Americans emphasize vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and seafood while limiting added sugars, sodium, and saturated fat [Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8713704/), and the WHO's healthy-diet guidance converges on the same emphasis [WHO healthy diet fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet). Patterns that respect it rate highest; those that exclude whole food groups rate lowest. I'll flag throughout where evidence is strong RCT-grade, observational only, or genuinely debated.

### Key evidence

**Mediterranean diet — strongest hard-outcome RCT evidence.** In PREDIMED, ~7,447 adults at high cardiovascular risk were randomized to a Mediterranean diet supplemented with free extra-virgin olive oil or nuts, versus a reduced-fat control. Over ~4.8 years, the MedDiet groups had roughly **30% fewer major cardiovascular events** (heart attack, stroke, cardiovascular death): combined HR 0.69 (95% CI 0.53–0.92), with the olive-oil arm at HR 0.69 (95% CI 0.53–0.91) and the nut arm at HR 0.72 (95% CI 0.54–0.95) [Estruch et al., NEJM 2018](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389) (publicly funded by Spain's Instituto de Salud Carlos III, but the supplemental olive oil and nuts were donated by industry — Patrimonio Comunal Olivarero/Hojiblanca, the California Walnut Commission, Borges, and Morella Nuts — with no sponsor role in design or analysis). Two honest caveats. First, the 2013 paper was *retracted in 2018* over randomization irregularities — some participants were assigned by household or clinic rather than individually — but reanalysis left the ~30% effect essentially unchanged, so the conclusion survived correction, and it was republished [Harvard Nutrition Source 2018](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/2018/06/22/predimed-retraction-republication/). Second, the control group got weak "eat less fat" advice and adhered poorly — so PREDIMED shows the MedDiet beating thin dietary advice, not beating another optimized diet. The result also comes from a high-risk Spanish population; extending it to low-risk or non-Mediterranean groups is inference, not proof. A Cochrane systematic review likewise rated the overall certainty of Mediterranean-diet cardiovascular evidence as low-to-moderate, reflecting how heavily the field leans on PREDIMED [Rees et al., Cochrane 2019](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009825.pub3/information).

**Adjust it (the ~30% payoff scales with your baseline risk).** The "~30% fewer events" is a *relative* risk reduction; the *absolute* benefit depends entirely on how high your risk was to begin with. PREDIMED's high-risk Spanish cohort ran ~3.8 major CV events per 100 people over ~4.8 years in controls; a 30% relative cut removes ~1.1 of them (an absolute risk reduction of ~1.1 points, NNT ≈ 90 over ~5 years) [Estruch et al., NEJM 2018](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389). Translate that to *your* arithmetic: a healthy 40-year-old with a 10-year CV risk of ~2.5% stands to drop perhaps ~0.7 points (NNT in the hundreds), while someone with established disease or a 10-year risk ≥20% can see an absolute drop of several points (NNT often <30). Rule of thumb: **expected absolute benefit ≈ 0.30 × your baseline event rate** — so the same diet is roughly an order of magnitude more valuable for a secondary-prevention patient than for a low-risk 30-year-old. See §09 for converting your 10-year risk to a numeric target.

**Score your adherence (don't guess at it).** Mediterranean eating is measurable, and dose matters. The classic **0–9 Trichopoulou score** awards 1 point each for above-median intake of vegetables, legumes, fruit/nuts, cereals, and fish; 1 point each for *below*-median meat and dairy; 1 for a high monounsaturated:saturated fat ratio; and 1 for moderate alcohol — observationally, each **2-point rise tracked with ~25% lower total mortality (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.64–0.87)** [Trichopoulou et al., NEJM 2003](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa025039). **Adjust it — baseline: aim ≥6 of 9** (the "high adherence" tier in most cohorts). Scaling: each +2 points ≈ a further ~25% relative drop in the observational mortality signal, so moving 3→7 is roughly two increments, not one. Depends on which tool you use: the **PREDIMED 14-item MEDAS** screener (the one tied to the ~30% RCT effect) sets its bar at **≥10 of 14** for "high adherence," with ≤5 low and 6–9 moderate [PREDIMED MEDAS validation, Nutrients 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5852714/). Caveat — these are *observational* score-mortality associations (the RCT proves the diet works, not that each scale point is causal), and the alcohol point is contested: if you don't drink, treat the practical ceiling as **8/9 or ~9/14** rather than chasing the alcohol point.

**DASH — the best-proven diet for lowering blood pressure (strong).** DASH was built and tested in controlled-feeding RCTs, where meals are provided and intake is verified. The original trial lowered blood pressure with no weight loss and no change in sodium: −5.5/−3.0 mmHg overall, and −11.4/−5.5 mmHg in people who already had hypertension [Appel et al., NEJM 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9099655/). Pairing DASH with sodium reduction (to ~1,500 mg/day) produces the **largest non-drug blood-pressure effect known** — up to −11.5 mmHg systolic in hypertensives in DASH-Sodium [Sacks et al., NEJM 2001](https://www.nejm.org/doi/pdf/10.1056/nejm200101043440101); [StatPearls 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482514/). The pattern is plant-forward and potassium-, magnesium-, and fiber-rich. At a 2,000-kcal reference, the NHLBI plan specifies **6–8 servings/day grains (mostly whole), 4–5/day vegetables, 4–5/day fruits, 2–3/day low-fat dairy, ≤6/day lean meat/poultry/fish, 2–3/day fats and oils, and 4–5/week nuts/seeds/legumes** [NHLBI DASH eating plan](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/education/dash-eating-plan); effects show up within about two weeks. The benefit is dose-responsive: largest in those starting with the highest blood pressure, in salt-sensitive individuals, and in Black adults [JACC 2017](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2017.10.011). Because it's a feeding-study construct, free-living adherence yields smaller real-world effects.

**Adjust it (BP payoff scales with starting pressure; servings scale with calories).** Baseline expectation: combining DASH with ~1,500 mg/day sodium produces **up to −11.5/−5.5 mmHg** — but only in hypertensives. The systolic drop scales roughly with where you start: ~**−11 mmHg if hypertensive** (≥140 systolic), but only ~**−5 to −7 mmHg if normotensive** [Sacks et al., NEJM 2001](https://www.nejm.org/doi/pdf/10.1056/nejm200101043440101); add another **~−1 mmHg systolic for roughly every −1,000 mg/day cut in sodium** within the trial's 3,500→1,500 mg range, with steeper response in salt-sensitive and Black adults. Serving counts scale ~linearly with energy intake (NHLBI also publishes 1,600- and 2,600-kcal versions): roughly **vegetables + fruits ≈ 4–5 servings each per 2,000 kcal**, i.e. ~2–2.5 servings per 1,000 kcal, so a 1,600-kcal plan runs ~3–4 of each and a 2,600-kcal plan ~5–6. See §20 for setting your calorie target via Mifflin–St Jeor and §09 for your numeric BP goal. Caveat: real-world (self-catered) effects are typically **half** the feeding-trial size — budget for ~−4 to −6 mmHg, not −11, unless intake is verified.

**MIND — the field's cautionary tale (genuinely contested).** The MIND diet (a Mediterranean–DASH hybrid aimed at the brain) had striking observational results: in the Rush cohort (n=960), high adherence tracked with slower cognitive decline equivalent to being ~7.5 years younger [Morris et al., Alzheimer's & Dementia 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4581900/), and a companion analysis (n=923) found ~53% lower Alzheimer's incidence in the top adherence tier (HR 0.47) [Morris et al., Alzheimer's & Dementia 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4532650/). Then a rigorous 3-year RCT (n=604) found **no significant difference** in cognition or brain MRI versus a control diet — both arms had mild calorie restriction and both improved slightly (mean between-group difference 0.035 SD units, P=0.23) [Barnes et al., NEJM 2023](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2302368); [PMC mirror](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10513737/). This is the clearest example of an observational diet signal failing to replicate. The trial doesn't prove MIND is useless — three years may be too short for cognitive prevention, and the comparator was itself a healthy diet — but the specific brain-protection claim is not RCT-supported. MIND foods (berries, leafy greens, nuts, olive oil, fish, beans) remain sound on cardiometabolic grounds.

**Plant-based (vegetarian/vegan) — solid on risk factors, observational on outcomes (moderate).** Across 30 RCTs, vegetarian and vegan diets reliably lowered LDL cholesterol by about 10% (~12 mg/dL, −0.30 mmol/L) and apolipoprotein B by about 14% versus omnivorous diets [Koch et al., European Heart Journal 2023](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/44/28/2609/7177660); a controlled identical-twin RCT saw LDL drop ~15 mg/dL (110.7→95.5) on a vegan diet in 8 weeks [Landry et al., JAMA Network Open 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10690456/). Cohorts suggest ~25–30% lower ischemic heart disease, but **mortality findings are inconsistent**: Adventist Health Study-2 found lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality [Orlich et al., JAMA Internal Medicine 2013](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/1710093), while EPIC-Oxford found lower ischemic heart disease but slightly higher stroke risk in vegetarians [Tong et al., BMJ 2019](https://www.bmj.com/content/366/bmj.l4897) — and the Adventist signal is confounded by lower smoking, less alcohol, and stronger social support. The decisive variable is *quality*: a whole-food plant-based diet lowers risk, while one built on refined grains, fries, and sugary drinks raises it. Vegans must supplement **vitamin B12** — non-negotiable, since natural B12 is limited to animal foods and deficiency risk is high without fortified foods or supplements [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/) — and should watch iron, zinc, iodine, omega-3 (EPA/DHA), calcium, and vitamin D.

**Adjust it (LDL drop and B12 dose are both quantifiable).** The LDL benefit scales with how much animal fat you displace and where you start: across RCTs, the average is ~**−10% LDL (~−12 mg/dL / −0.30 mmol/L)** [Koch et al., European Heart Journal 2023](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/44/28/2609/7177660), but the higher your baseline LDL the larger the absolute fall (the twin RCT started at ~111 mg/dL and dropped ~15) — and most of the swing comes from cutting saturated fat, so expect roughly **~−2 mg/dL LDL per 1% of energy shifted out of saturated fat**. For **B12, the RDA is 2.4 µg/day**, but because passive absorption is only ~1% at high single doses, the practical vegan regimen is **~50–100 µg/day, or ~2,000 µg once weekly** [NIH ODS B12](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/); scale up if depleted, pregnant/lactating (target ≥2.6–2.8 µg/day intake), age >50 or on metformin/PPIs (malabsorption → favor the daily 50–100 µg route over food alone). Other floors to hit daily: **iodine ~150 µg** (≈¼ tsp iodized salt or a kelp-free supplement), **EPA+DHA ~250 mg from algal oil**, **calcium ~1,000 mg** (1,200 mg if age >50/female), **vitamin D ~600–800 IU**, plus attention to iron and zinc.

**Low-carb and ketogenic — effective short-term, source-dependent long-term (moderate).** For weight loss and type 2 diabetes, low-carb works in the near term: a meta-analysis of randomized trials found low-carbohydrate diets raised 6-month diabetes remission rates and improved triglycerides and weight, though benefits diminished by 12 months [Goldenberg et al., BMJ 2021](https://www.bmj.com/content/372/bmj.m4743). The DiRECT trial (a low-calorie program, not strictly keto) achieved **46% diabetes remission at one year** versus 4% in controls, scaling with weight lost — but remission durability fades, from 46% at one year to 36% at two years, with ~13% (11/85) of the supported intervention group remaining in remission at 5 years versus 5% in controls [Lean et al., Lancet 2018](https://www.thelancet.com/article/S0140-6736(17)33102-1/fulltext); [Lean et al., Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38423026/). On hard outcomes, carbohydrate intake shows a **U-shape**: in a large cohort and meta-analysis, mortality was lowest around 50–55% of energy from carbohydrate, with both low (<40% of energy, pooled HR 1.20) and high (>70%, HR 1.23) intakes worse [Seidelmann et al., Lancet Public Health 2018](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpub/article/PIIS2468-2667(18)30135-X/fulltext). Crucially, "low-carb" isn't one diet: replacing carbs with animal protein and fat tracked with *higher* mortality, while replacing them with plant protein and fat tracked with *lower* mortality. Keto also raises LDL in some people. Its strongest cases are short-to-medium-term weight loss, metabolic syndrome, and the established medical use in epilepsy.

**Adjust it (define "low-carb" by grams, and expect the glycemic edge to fade).** "Low-carb" is a number, not a vibe: **moderate ~26–45% of energy (~130–225 g/day at 2,000 kcal), low <26% (<130 g), very-low/ketogenic <10% (~20–50 g/day)** — ketosis generally needs ≤50 g. Baseline payoff: at **6 months**, low-carb roughly doubled diabetes remission (a ~32% absolute increase, ~28 more remissions per 100) and improved HbA1c, weight, and triglycerides — but **by 12 months most of that gap closes** [Goldenberg et al., BMJ 2021](https://www.bmj.com/content/372/bmj.m4743), so treat any HbA1c or weight win as a **6-month effect that needs an exit plan**, not a plateau you can coast on. The remission lever is weight, and it scales steeply: in DiRECT, remission ran **~7% at 0–5 kg lost, ~34% at 5–10 kg, ~57% at 10–15 kg, and ~86% at ≥15 kg** [Lean et al., Lancet 2018](https://www.thelancet.com/article/S0140-6736(17)33102-1/fulltext) — i.e. **aim for ≥10 kg (or ≥10–15% of body weight) if remission is the goal**, and note durability decays (~46%→36% at 2 years→~13% at 5 years). Depends on substitution and biology: keep protein/fat **plant-skewed** (in the U-shape data above, animal-substitution tracked with HR 1.18 mortality vs 0.82 for plant), recheck **LDL at 6–12 weeks** and reconsider keto if it climbs >~30 mg/dL, and if you're not chasing diabetes remission there's no mortality reason to drop below the ~50–55%-carb sweet spot. Coordinate medication tapering with a clinician (see §09).

**Expert alignment scoring — patterns stratify clearly (strong as expert judgment).** The AHA scored 10 popular patterns against its 2021 heart-health guidance. **Tier 1** (best): Mediterranean, DASH-style, pescatarian, and ovo-lacto vegetarian. **Tier 2**: vegan and low-fat. **Tier 3**: very-low-fat and low-carb. **Tier 4** (worst): **paleo and ketogenic**, penalized for excluding whole grains, legumes, and fruit and for high saturated fat [AHA, Circulation 2023](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001146). This is expert judgment of cardiovascular *alignment*, not a head-to-head outcome trial — and a low-scoring pattern can still suit a specific clinical goal.

### What it depends on

- **Your starting risk and goal.** PREDIMED's benefit was shown in high-cardiovascular-risk adults; DASH's effect is largest in people with hypertension. The healthier and lower-risk you already are, the smaller the marginal gain.
- **What you're optimizing for.** DASH for blood pressure, Mediterranean for cardiovascular events, short-term low-carb for weight or diabetes, keto for epilepsy. No single pattern wins every endpoint.
- **Diet *quality* within the pattern.** "Plant-based" and "low-carb" each span a healthy and an unhealthy version. Whole foods vs. refined, and plant vs. animal substitutions, drive the outcome more than the label.
- **Population and biology.** Salt sensitivity, ancestry (Black adults respond more to DASH), and genetics (LDL hyper-response to keto) shift the effect size.
- **Adherence and real life.** Feeding-study and supplemented-RCT effects shrink when people self-cater. The best pattern is one you'll actually sustain — and Tier-1 patterns adapt readily to culture, budget, and preference.

### Practical takeaways

- **Pick a Tier-1 pattern as your default, then match it to your goal.** Mediterranean or DASH-style eating has the strongest evidence and the widest endorsement, and both flex to your cuisine and wallet. **Adjust the pick by what you're treating:** **high CV risk / post-event → Mediterranean** (~30% relative event reduction in high-risk adults; absolute benefit ≈ 0.30 × your baseline event rate); **hypertension → DASH + low sodium** (up to ~−11/−5.5 mmHg if hypertensive, ~half that if normotensive); **type 2 diabetes / weight, short-term → low-carb** (6-month HbA1c and remission edge that fades by 12 months); **drug-resistant epilepsy → ketogenic** (established medical use). No pattern wins every endpoint, and a low-AHA-tier pattern can still be the right tool for a specific goal.
- **For blood pressure, run DASH plus sodium reduction.** **Adjust the sodium target by who you are:** general-population ceiling **<2,300 mg/day**; **<1,500 mg/day** if hypertensive, salt-sensitive, Black, or age >50 (the groups with the steepest dose-response); expect results within ~2 weeks, and budget roughly **−1 mmHg systolic per −1,000 mg/day** cut in the 3,500→1,500 mg range. But the floor is individual and the lower bound is genuinely contested — don't drive below ~1,500 mg without a reason: **heart failure or loop-diuretic use → individualize, often ~2,000–3,000 mg** (over-restriction can worsen outcomes); **salt-wasting CKD → may need *more*, not less**; **hyponatremia risk (older adults, endurance athletes losing ~0.5–1.5 g sodium/L of sweat, pregnancy) → don't aggressively cut.** Individualize with a clinician [AHA dietary guidance, Circulation 2021](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/cir.0000000000001031); [AHA, Circulation: Heart Failure 2023](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIRCHEARTFAILURE.122.009879). See §09 for your numeric BP goal.
- **Build around the shared core**, not the brand: vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts, olive oil, fish; minimal red/processed meat, added sugar, sodium, and refined starch.
- **If you go plant-based, supplement B12 without exception** — **~50–100 µg/day or ~2,000 µg/week** (RDA is only 2.4 µg/day, but absorption is the bottleneck) — choose whole-food versions, and hit daily floors for iodine (~150 µg), algal EPA+DHA (~250 mg), calcium (~1,000–1,200 mg), and vitamin D (~600–800 IU), plus iron and zinc.
- **Use low-carb/keto as a targeted tool**, not a forever default. **Define it (<130 g/day low, <50 g keto), treat the glycemic win as a 6-month effect** that fades by 12, aim for **≥10–15% weight loss if remission is the goal**, favor plant fats and proteins, and **recheck LDL at 6–12 weeks** — reconsider if it climbs >~30 mg/dL.
- **Don't expect a specific diet to protect your brain.** Eat the cardiometabolically sound foods anyway; the cognitive payoff, if any, is a bonus, not a proven guarantee.

### Unsettled or debated

- **Does plant-based eating extend lifespan, or just improve risk factors?** RCTs consistently improve LDL, weight, and glucose, but all-cause mortality evidence is observational and conflicting (Adventist benefit vs. EPIC-Oxford null), heavily confounded by overall lifestyle.
- **How harmful is low-carb in the long run?** The U-shaped mortality curve is observational, and the animal-vs-plant split means "low-carb" is too coarse a category to bless or condemn outright.
- **Is the MIND/cognitive-protection story dead or just unproven?** A null 3-year RCT against a healthy comparator doesn't rule out a longer-term or higher-contrast benefit — but it does mean the headline cognitive claim is currently unsupported by trial evidence.
- **How much should the "Blue Zones" carry?** The Blue Zones (Sardinia, Okinawa, Ikaria, Nicoya, Loma Linda) are popularly cited as proof that a plant-leaning, traditional diet drives exceptional longevity — but they are *hypothesis-generating*, not strong causal evidence. They're uncontrolled observational regions where diet is hopelessly confounded with movement, social ties, religion, and genetics, so no single dietary cause can be isolated. More fundamentally, the underlying age data have been seriously challenged: demographer Saul Justin Newman found that validated supercentenarian and "remarkable age" records cluster in regions with *missing or late birth registration, poverty, low literacy, and short average life expectancy* — patterns indicative of age exaggeration, clerical error, and pension fraud rather than real longevity (only ~18% of exhaustively validated supercentenarians worldwide have a birth certificate; near 0% in the US), work that won the 2024 Ig Nobel Prize in Demography [Newman, bioRxiv 2019](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/704080v3); [UCL 2024](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/ioe/news/2024/sep/ucl-demographers-work-debunking-blue-zone-regions-exceptional-lifespans-wins-ig-nobel-prize). Treat Blue Zones as a suggestive prompt that converges with the trial evidence above — not as proof of any single dietary cause.
