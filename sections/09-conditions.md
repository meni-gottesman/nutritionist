## 09. Diet for Common Health Conditions

**Bottom line:** The same plant-forward base helps the heart, metabolism, blood pressure, and liver at once — where conditions diverge, one lever usually dominates (weight loss for diabetes/fatty liver, sodium-potassium for BP, fat swaps for LDL), and CKD is the exception where protein goes *down* under clinician supervision.


For the conditions most shaped by what we eat — cardiovascular disease, high cholesterol, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, fatty liver disease, IBS, and chronic kidney disease — diet is often the first-line therapy, and in a few cases (notably type 2 diabetes) it can do something drugs rarely do: push the disease into remission. But the evidence ranges from large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) down to mechanistic reasoning, and the right move for one condition can be the wrong move for another.

A single message converges across most of these conditions. A plant-forward pattern — vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and unsaturated fats, low in refined carbohydrate, sodium, processed and red meat, and alcohol — plus weight management where excess body fat is present, is good for the heart, the metabolism, the blood pressure, and the liver at once. You rarely have to choose between conditions when building the base of the diet.

Where conditions diverge, it is usually because **one lever dominates**: weight loss for diabetes remission and liver fibrosis, sodium-potassium balance for blood pressure, specific food swaps for LDL cholesterol. And a few areas are genuinely contested — protein targets in kidney disease, potassium handling — where the same change helps one patient and harms another. None of this replaces medical management: diet modifies risk and can reduce or eliminate medication, but condition-specific monitoring (labs, blood pressure, A1c, eGFR) is non-negotiable.

### Key evidence

**Cardiovascular disease — the Mediterranean diet (strong RCT evidence).** PREDIMED randomized 7,447 high-CV-risk adults to a Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil (~4 tbsp/day) or mixed nuts (~30 g/day) versus a low-fat control (industry-donated foods; see §06), and cut major cardiovascular events (heart attack, stroke, CV death) by roughly 28-31%, with hazard ratios of 0.69 (95% CI 0.53-0.91) for the olive-oil arm and 0.72 (95% CI 0.54-0.95) for the nuts arm versus control [PREDIMED, NEJM 2018](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389). One honest caveat: the original 2013 paper was retracted and republished in 2018 after randomization irregularities in a minority of participants — but the corrected analysis preserved the benefit, and it remains the largest dietary RCT for CVD prevention [Harvard Nutrition Source 2018](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/2018/06/22/predimed-retraction-republication/).

> **Adjust it — the PREDIMED "dose."** Baseline: ~4 tbsp/day (~50 mL) extra-virgin olive oil *or* ~30 g/day mixed nuts (≈23 almonds, or a small handful), on top of a Mediterranean base. Scaling: the trial gave one or the other, not both — the marginal benefit beyond ~30 g nuts/day or ~4 tbsp oil/day is unproven, so more is not better and the calories add up fast (1 tbsp oil ≈ 120 kcal; 30 g nuts ≈ 180–200 kcal). Depends on: weight goal — if running a deficit (§20), count these in your calorie budget and trim elsewhere, not above it; on warfarin → vitamin-K-rich foods need *consistency*, not avoidance (clinician-only, coordinate with INR); tree-nut allergy → substitute the olive-oil arm. This is a CV-event lever for high-risk adults, not a weight-loss tool.

**High cholesterol — quantifiable, stackable food swaps (strong).** Several changes lower LDL by known amounts that add up. Plant sterols/stanols at 2 g/day lower LDL roughly 8-10% (with diminishing returns at higher doses) [Ras et al., BJN 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4071994/); soluble (viscous) fiber at 5-10 g/day from oats, barley, psyllium and beans lowers it about 5-11 mg/dL [Ghavami et al., Adv Nutr 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10201678/); soy protein and nuts add more. The Portfolio diet, which stacks all four, achieved about a 13-14% LDL reduction over 6-12 months in real-world trials and up to ~30% under controlled feeding [Jenkins et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4223745/); [Harvard Health 2023](https://www.health.harvard.edu/heart-health/the-portfolio-diet-a-smart-investment-for-your-heart). On saturated fat, the AHA/ACC advise under 6% of calories for elevated LDL (about 13 g/day on a 2,000-kcal diet), while federal and WHO guidance set the general cap at under 10%; trans fat should be as low as possible (under 1% of energy) [AHA 2024](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/eat-smart/fats/saturated-fats); [ACC 2025](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Articles/2025/07/01/01/Prioritizing-Health-Dietary-Approaches-For-Elevated-LDL-C). The biggest gains come from replacing saturated fat with *unsaturated* fat, whole grains, or fiber — not with refined carbohydrate, which does not improve risk.

> **Adjust it — the LDL "stack."** Baseline (general): saturated fat <10% of calories. For elevated LDL: <6% of calories — about 13 g/day on 2,000 kcal, ~10 g on 1,500 kcal, ~16 g on 2,500 kcal (scale ≈0.67 g sat-fat cap per 100 kcal). Add soluble fiber ~5–10 g/day and plant sterols/stanols ~2 g/day. Scaling, with numbers you can stack: soluble fiber lowers LDL ≈2 mg/dL per gram/day (median ~7 g/day → ~14 mg/dL; diminishing returns above ~10 g/day) [Ghavami 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10201678/); sterols/stanols 2 g/day → ~8–10% LDL drop (little extra benefit above 2–3 g/day) [Ras 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4071994/); each 1% of energy shifted from saturated to polyunsaturated fat lowers LDL by roughly 1–2 mg/dL. Depends on: baseline LDL (the higher you start, the bigger the absolute drop); statin use → diet stacks *on top of* drug effect, never replaces a prescribed statin (clinician-only to change meds); familial hypercholesterolemia → diet alone is insufficient, this is a pharmacologic condition; trans fat → push as low as possible (<1% of energy, ~2 g/day on 2,000 kcal).

**Type 2 diabetes — remission through weight loss (strong RCT evidence).** DiRECT, a cluster-RCT of about 298 people, used a total diet replacement (an 825-853 kcal/day formula for 12-20 weeks), stepped food reintroduction, then maintenance. Remission was 46% at one year and 36% at two years, and it tracked tightly with weight kept off: among those who maintained ≥10 kg of loss at two years, 64% (29 of 45) were in remission [DiRECT 2-year, Lancet D&E 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30852132/), and at one year ≥15 kg of loss produced 86% remission (31 of 36) [DiRECT 1-year, Lancet 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29221645/). But durability tracks weight maintenance, not the initial loss: by year 5 most participants had regained weight (mean loss ~6 kg in the intervention group) and only ~13% remained in remission, underscoring that remission persists only as long as the weight loss does [DiRECT 5-year, Lancet D&E 2024](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landia/article/PIIS2213-8587(23)00385-6/fulltext). A 2024 meta-regression of 22 RCTs (>12,000 participants) confirmed a strong dose-response: each 1% of body-weight lost raised the chance of remission, reaching about 50% remission at 20-29% loss and ~79% at ≥30% loss [Kanbour et al., Lancet D&E 2024](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landia/article/PIIS2213-8587(24)00346-2/abstract). This works best in those with under six years' diabetes duration, BMI 27-45, and not on insulin. For day-to-day glycemic control rather than remission, the ADA endorses Mediterranean, DASH, plant-based, *and* low-carbohydrate patterns, stating there is no single optimal carbohydrate amount; low-carb (<130 g/day) gives about 0.5% greater HbA1c reduction at six months, though the edge attenuates by twelve as adherence wanes (moderate) [ADA Standards of Care 2024, Section 5](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/47/Supplement_1/S77/153949/5-Facilitating-Positive-Health-Behaviors-and-Well). Carbohydrate *quality* and weight loss matter more than the exact macro split.

> **Adjust it — two levers: weight (for remission) and carb quality (for control).**
> - **Weight loss for remission.** Baseline target: get to and keep off ≥10–15 kg (or ≥10% of body weight) — within that band, DiRECT saw ~57% remission at 10–15 kg loss but ~86% only at ≥15 kg, so the higher end of the range matters [DiRECT 1-year, Lancet 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29221645/). Scaling is near-linear: each ~1% of body weight lost raises remission odds, reaching ~50% remission at 20–29% loss and ~79% at ≥30% [Kanbour 2024](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landia/article/PIIS2213-8587(24)00346-2/abstract); durability tracks *maintained* loss, not the dip. Depends on: diabetes duration <6 years, BMI ~27–45, and not on insulin → best candidates; longer duration or insulin-dependent → remission less likely, aim for control. Run the deficit via Mifflin–St Jeor (§20): −500 to −750 kcal/day ≈ −0.45 to −0.7 kg/week; total-diet-replacement formulas (~800–850 kcal/day for 12–20 weeks) are a faster but clinician-supervised option, especially if on glucose-lowering drugs that need dose reduction.
> - **Carbohydrate for daily control.** There is *no single best number* — the ADA endorses Mediterranean, DASH, plant-based, and low-carb patterns alike. The lever: lower-carb (<130 g/day, or <26% of energy) buys ~0.5% extra HbA1c drop at 6 months, fading by 12 as adherence wanes; pair any carb level with ≥14 g fiber per 1,000 kcal and minimal refined/ultra-processed carbs and sugary drinks. Depends on: on insulin or a sulfonylurea → cutting carbs sharply risks hypoglycemia, so titrate carbs and drug doses together (clinician-only); on an SGLT2 inhibitor → very-low-carb raises euglycemic ketoacidosis risk, avoid ketogenic carb levels.

**Hypertension — DASH plus sodium reduction (strong).** In the DASH-Sodium feeding RCT, the DASH pattern plus low sodium produced the largest blood-pressure drops — up to ~10-11 mmHg systolic in people starting at ≥150 mmHg — with a clear dose-response. Cutting sodium alone (from ~3,450 to ~1,150 mg/day) lowered systolic BP by ~3-9 mmHg depending on baseline, with bigger effects in hypertensives, older adults, and Black participants [DASH-Sodium, NEJM 2001](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM200101043440101); [JACC 2017](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2017.10.011). Potassium-enriched salt substitutes add a second lever: the SSaSS trial (20,995 adults, 75% NaCl / 25% KCl) cut stroke by 14%, major CV events by 13%, and death by 12% over five years, with no significant excess of clinical hyperkalemia [SSaSS, NEJM 2021](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2105675). Important: SSaSS excluded people with advanced CKD or on potassium-raising drugs, who should not use KCl substitutes without medical advice.

> **Adjust it — sodium down, potassium up, by severity.** Baseline sodium: <2,300 mg/day, moving toward <1,500 mg/day when appropriate [AHA/ACC 2025](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001356). Scaling: in DASH-Sodium, dropping intake from ~3,450 to ~1,150 mg/day lowered systolic BP ~3–9 mmHg overall — and ~10–11 mmHg in those starting at ≥150 mmHg — i.e., roughly 1 mmHg per ~250–300 mg of sodium cut at the high-baseline end, with most of the gain by ~1,500 mg/day [DASH-Sodium, JACC 2017](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2017.10.011). Depends on: hypertensive, salt-sensitive, Black, diabetic, or age >50–51 → effect is largest, push toward <1,500 mg; baseline already <120 mmHg → benefit is small. Potassium: aim 3,500–5,000 mg/day from food. A 75% NaCl/25% KCl salt substitute is a second lever (SSaSS: −14% stroke, −13% major CV events) — **but do NOT use it (clinician-only) if you have eGFR <45, advanced CKD, or take an ACE inhibitor, ARB, or potassium-sparing diuretic**, which raise hyperkalemia risk. On a loop/thiazide diuretic → sodium and potassium both shift; coordinate with your prescriber.

**Fatty liver (NAFLD/MASLD) — tiered, dose-dependent weight loss (strong).** Liver fat responds to weight loss in steps: ≥5% reduces steatosis, ≥7% resolves steatohepatitis, and ≥10% regresses fibrosis. In a 52-week biopsy study (n=293), ≥10% loss resolved steatohepatitis in about 90% and regressed fibrosis in about 45% [Vilar-Gomez et al., Gastroenterology 2015](https://www.gastrojournal.org/article/S0016-5085(15)00496-5/fulltext); [AGA 2023](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/lifestyle-modification-using-diet-and-exercise-to-achieve-weight-loss-in-the-management-of-nonalcoholic-fatty-liver-disease-nafld/); [Frontiers in Nutrition 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1421386/full). The practical levers are a hypocaloric Mediterranean diet, reduced added sugar (especially fructose), and minimized alcohol, often via a 500-1,000 kcal/day deficit. Lifestyle change remains the cornerstone even as new drugs arrive.

> **Adjust it — pick the weight-loss target to the histologic goal.** Baseline: aim for ≥7–10% body-weight loss. Scaling is stepwise (so set the target to what you need to fix): ≥5% reduces liver fat/steatosis, ≥7% resolves steatohepatitis, ≥10% regresses fibrosis (~45% fibrosis regression and ~90% steatohepatitis resolution at ≥10% in the 52-week biopsy cohort) [Vilar-Gomez 2015](https://www.gastrojournal.org/article/S0016-5085(15)00496-5/fulltext). Get there with a 500–1,000 kcal/day deficit ≈ 0.45–0.9 kg/week (Mifflin–St Jeor baseline in §20). Worked example: an 85 kg adult needs ~6 kg off to clear the ≥7% bar, ~8.5 kg to reach ≥10%. Depends on: lean/normal-BMI MASLD → even ~3–5% loss plus cutting fructose/added sugar can help, since absolute weight matters less than the deficit; heavy alcohol use → alcohol removal is the first lever and reclassifies the diagnosis; rapid loss >1.5 kg/week → can worsen inflammation, so cap the deficit (clinician oversight if also on weight-loss drugs).

**Irritable bowel syndrome — low-FODMAP, done as a protocol (strong).** Across more than ten RCTs, a low-FODMAP diet relieves IBS symptoms in roughly 50-80% of patients (about 70% in practice), cutting symptom-severity scores by more than half, with bloating and abdominal pain most improved [Halmos et al., Gastroenterology 2014](https://www.gastrojournal.org/article/S0016-5085(13)01407-8/fulltext); [Black et al., Gut 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34376515/); [van Lanen et al., Eur J Nutr 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8354978/). Critically, this is a three-phase, dietitian-guided protocol — a 2-6 week elimination, systematic reintroduction to find triggers, then long-term personalization — *not* permanent restriction, which risks nutritional inadequacy and adverse microbiome shifts.

> **Adjust it — it's a timed protocol, not a forever diet.** Baseline phases (Monash): Phase 1 strict restriction 2–6 weeks; Phase 2 reintroduction ~6–8 weeks (test one FODMAP subgroup at a time against your background low-FODMAP diet); Phase 3 lifelong personalization restricting only the subgroups you actually react to. Scaling: ~50–80% of patients respond (≈70% in practice); if there's *no* improvement by the end of week 6, stop — extending strict restriction adds risk without benefit. Depends on: symptom-free after reintroduction → re-expand the diet, don't stay restricted (microbiome and micronutrient risk rises with duration); pregnancy, disordered-eating history, or already-limited diet → low-FODMAP is relatively contraindicated without close dietitian supervision (clinician-only); poor responders → reassess the IBS diagnosis rather than restrict harder.

**Chronic kidney disease — individualized, and shifting away from blanket restriction (moderate).** KDIGO 2024 recommends about 0.8 g/kg/day of protein for CKD stages G3-G5 not on dialysis, avoiding high intake (>1.3 g/kg/day) in those at risk of progression; very-low-protein diets are reserved for select stable patients with adequate energy (30-35 kcal/kg/day) and dietitian oversight [KDIGO 2024](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/KDIGO-2024-CKD-Guideline-Executive-Summary.pdf); [KDOQI US Commentary, AJKD 2025](https://www.ajkd.org/article/S0272-6386(24)00977-6/fulltext). Two notable shifts: KDIGO now advises *against* routine potassium restriction without persistent hyperkalemia (correct acidosis, constipation, and medications first), and phosphorus restriction is individualized, favoring whole-food plant sources over additives. Sodium target is under 2.0-2.3 g/day. And the direction reverses on dialysis, where protein needs *rise* to ~1.0-1.2 g/kg/day.

> **Adjust it — protein by stage, then individualize K and phosphate (renal-dietitian territory).** Protein baseline scales by stage of disease:
> - **G1–G2 (eGFR ≥60):** ~0.8 g/kg/day — the same as the general healthy adult target (§02/§20); no special restriction, just avoid chronic high intake (>1.3 g/kg/day).
> - **G3–G5, not on dialysis:** ~0.8 g/kg/day, explicitly avoiding >1.3 g/kg/day if at risk of progression [KDIGO 2024](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/KDIGO-2024-CKD-Guideline-Executive-Summary.pdf). Worked example: a 70 kg adult → ~56 g/day. A very-low-protein diet (0.3–0.4 g/kg/day) plus keto-acid/essential-amino-acid analogues is an option only for select, stable, motivated patients with energy adequacy of 30–35 kcal/kg/day and close monitoring — **clinician-only; do not self-prescribe** (malnutrition risk).
> - **On dialysis:** the target *rises* to ~1.0–1.2 g/kg/day (≈70–84 g for a 70 kg adult) to offset dialytic protein losses and protein-energy wasting.
>
> Potassium and phosphate are *not* fixed numbers — individualize. KDIGO now advises against reflexive potassium restriction without persistent hyperkalemia (correct acidosis, constipation, and medications first); when restriction is needed, target is set to lab values, not a blanket cap. For phosphate, favor whole-food plant and unprocessed sources (~40–50% absorbed) over inorganic additives (~90% absorbed) rather than chasing a single milligram target. Sodium: <2.0–2.3 g/day. Depends on: persistent hyperkalemia or on a K-raising drug → individualized K limit and avoid salt substitutes (clinician-only); pregnancy with CKD → protein needs rise, do not restrict; all of this requires a renal dietitian and serial labs (eGFR, potassium, phosphate, albumin).

**Adjust it — the four table-only conditions, with numbers:**

- **Gout / hyperuricemia.** Baseline target: serum urate <6 mg/dL (<5 mg/dL if tophi or frequent flares) — but this is achieved mainly by urate-lowering *drugs*; diet is adjunctive. Scaling/levers (all conditional, modest urate effect): limit alcohol (beer and spirits worse than wine), purine-rich organ meat, red meat, and shellfish, and high-fructose corn syrup, regardless of disease activity; lose weight if BMI ≥25 (any program). Depends on: overweight/obese → weight loss is the highest-yield diet move and lowers urate; do *not* take vitamin C supplements for urate-lowering (ineffective); on allopurinol/febuxostat → keep diet steady and titrate the drug to the <6 mg/dL target (clinician-only). Dairy and coffee are weakly urate-lowering and need not be limited.
- **Osteoporosis.** Calcium baseline scales by age/sex: 1,000 mg/day for men 51–70 and adults 19–50; **1,200 mg/day for women ≥51 and men ≥71**. Vitamin D: 600 IU/day RDA for adults 51–70, 800 IU/day for ≥71 — most bone guidelines target 800–1,000 IU/day for adults ≥50 to maintain serum 25(OH)D ~30 ng/mL. Get calcium from food first; cap *total* (food + supplement) calcium at the 2,000–2,500 mg/day upper limit and supplemental calcium at ~500–600 mg per dose (absorption plateaus, and high supplemental doses may carry CV signal). Depends on: malabsorption, dark skin, or limited sun → vitamin D need rises, dose to blood level (clinician-only); diet already supplies ~1,000 mg → little or no calcium supplement needed.
- **GERD.** Weight loss is the strongest diet lever for the overweight: dose-dependent, a >3.5 kg/m² BMI drop raises the odds of *losing* reflux symptoms ~2.4-fold (≈10 kg for a 1.7 m adult). Behavioral levers with numbers: finish eating ≥2–3 hours before lying down; elevate the head of the bed ~10–20 cm. On trigger foods: the commonly blamed culprits — alcohol, coffee, chocolate, peppermint, fatty/fried foods, citrus, and tomato — have only weak group-level evidence, so don't blanket-avoid them; instead run a personal test, eliminating a suspected food for 2–4 weeks and then rechallenging to confirm it actually drives *your* symptoms before cutting it for good. Depends on: nocturnal symptoms → bed elevation and meal timing matter most; identifiable trigger foods (confirmed by your own eliminate-then-rechallenge test) → cut those individually rather than restricting the whole list.
- **Heart failure.** No strict number: for stage C HF, the 2022 guideline calls only for avoiding *excessive* sodium (Class 2a, weak), and explicitly does **not** endorse a strict <1,500 mg/day target after SODIUM-HF showed no reduction in death or hospitalization. A pragmatic ceiling is ~2,000–3,000 mg/day. Depends on: overt fluid overload/congestion → tighter sodium and clinician-set fluid limits help symptoms; on diuretics → over-restricting sodium can cause hypovolemia and worsen renal function (clinician-only). Coordinate any target with the cardiology team.

### What it depends on

- **Which condition — and which lever.** Weight loss drives diabetes remission and liver-fibrosis regression; sodium-potassium balance drives blood pressure; LDL responds to specific food swaps. Pick the lever the condition rewards.
- **Disease stage and direction.** CKD protein goes *down* before dialysis and *up* on dialysis. Diabetes remission is most achievable early (under six years, not on insulin).
- **Baseline severity.** Blood-pressure and cholesterol benefits are largest in those who start highest; the same diet does less in someone near-normal.
- **Potassium is condition-dependent.** Higher potassium helps blood pressure and the heart but is dangerous in advanced kidney disease or on potassium-raising drugs. Never assume one rule fits all.
- **Co-existing conditions and medications.** Diuretics, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, SGLT2 inhibitors, and insulin all change the calculus — coordinate diet changes with the prescriber.

### Practical takeaways

- **Build the same base for almost everything:** a Mediterranean/DASH-style plate — vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, olive oil, fish — low in sodium, refined carbs, processed meat, and alcohol.
- **For heart and cholesterol,** swap saturated fat for unsaturated fat and stack LDL-lowering foods: 2 g/day plant sterols, 5-10 g/day soluble fiber, soy, and nuts.
- **For type 2 diabetes,** target meaningful, sustained weight loss if overweight; ≥10-15 kg kept off gives the best shot at remission. Otherwise pick any quality pattern you can adhere to.
- **For blood pressure,** adopt DASH, cut sodium toward ~1,500 mg/day, and consider a potassium salt substitute — *unless* you have advanced CKD or take potassium-raising drugs (ask your doctor first).
- **For fatty liver,** aim for ≥7-10% body-weight loss; cut sugary drinks and alcohol first.
- **For IBS,** do low-FODMAP as a supervised 3-phase protocol, then reintroduce — don't restrict forever.
- **For CKD,** do not self-prescribe protein or potassium limits. Get a renal dietitian; the targets are individualized and stage-dependent.
- **Track the right number** for your condition — A1c, LDL, blood pressure, eGFR, or liver enzymes — so you can see whether the diet is working.

### Unsettled or debated

- **CKD protein targets** are genuinely contested: the benefit of low- and very-low-protein diets versus their malnutrition risk is debated, and intake must be individualized with energy adequacy and close monitoring.
- **Potassium handling in CKD** has shifted — KDIGO now discourages reflexive potassium restriction, a reversal of long-standing practice that not all clinicians have adopted.
- **The optimal carbohydrate amount in type 2 diabetes** remains unsettled; low-carb's early HbA1c edge fades by a year, and the ADA explicitly declines to name one best macro split.
- **Calcium and vitamin D supplementation** (for bone and other conditions) remains contested and is best decided individually rather than by blanket rule; the evidence that routine supplements prevent fractures in community-dwelling adults is mixed, so prioritize dietary calcium and adequate vitamin D status first [NIH ODS Calcium 2024](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/).
