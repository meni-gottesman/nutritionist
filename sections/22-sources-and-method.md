## 22. Sources & Method

### How this guide was built

This document was produced by a structured, multi-agent research process designed to be exhaustive and self-critical:

1. **Decomposition.** "The healthiest diet" was broken into 14 core domains (frameworks, macronutrients, micronutrients, foods to favor, foods to limit, dietary patterns, beverages, personalization, conditions, energy/weight, timing, supplements, sustainability, and controversies) — later expanded to 22 sections (see the version history below).
2. **Parallel research.** Each domain was researched independently against authoritative primary sources via live web search — WHO/FAO, the USDA/HHS *Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025–2030* (and its 2020–2025 predecessor where unchanged), the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, Harvard's *The Nutrition Source*, the AHA and ADA, Cochrane reviews, the EAT-Lancet Commission, and landmark randomized trials and cohort studies.
3. **Adversarial fact-checking.** The highest-stakes and most contested claims in each domain were handed to independent fact-checkers instructed to *refute* them — to surface where a claim was overstated, oversimplified, or more context-dependent than it first appeared. Those verdicts are reflected in the cautious, hedged language around debated topics (MIND diet, vitamin D, red meat, salt floors, sweeteners).
4. **Drafting and synthesis.** Each verified domain became a cited section; the executive summary, personalization matrix, and sample plans were synthesized across them.

**Transparency & accuracy.** The guide was built and then hardened over many passes (see the version history below): initial research and adversarial fact-checking; a source-enrichment pass that web-verified every citation and added more; a skeptical section-by-section critique; the addition of quantitative personalization, weight-management guidance, and five further deep-dive sections; a per-claim numeric audit in which independent auditors re-opened each cited source to confirm every figure; and a later round that turned the static targets into a live **interactive diet engine** with an on-device adaptive refiner and a guide-grounded **nutrition-coach chatbot (retrieval-augmented)**, added 20 per-section "Bottom line" summaries, and updated the guide to the 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans. The numeric audit re-checked **427 quantitative claims, corrected 30 figure or attribution errors, upgraded 23 citations to primary sources, and added conflict-of-interest / industry-funding disclosures** to pivotal trials (e.g., the GLP-1 drug trials, REDUCE-IT, PREDIMED, ZOE). The calculator was then hardened over several adversarial-critique passes — a coherent macro partition, plus pregnancy-vs-CKD and underweight/safe-floor guards and calibrated GLP-1 and dialysis protein targets — and finally verified by an automated harness that drove the live engine across **2,265 input profiles**, comparing every output against independent evidence-based expected values and resolving every divergence it found. A link sweep confirmed every source resolves (paywalled journal and government links bot-block automated checkers but open normally in a browser). Each section carries a *Last reviewed* date. One scope limitation worth flagging: the underlying evidence base is predominantly drawn from North American and European populations (with a handful of Mediterranean RCTs such as PREDIMED), so the quantitative estimates may transfer imperfectly to other regions and ancestries — another reason to treat the numbers as directional rather than exact. Still — this is a synthesis, not a primary source: **verify specifics against the cited originals before relying on them**, treat numbers as well-supported estimates rather than precise constants, and bring anything clinical to a qualified professional.

### How to read the evidence labels

Throughout, claims are graded honestly:

- **Strong** — supported by randomized controlled trials, Cochrane reviews, and/or convergent meta-analyses (e.g., trans-fat harm, fiber benefit, replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat, sodium and blood pressure).
- **Moderate** — consistent observational evidence and mechanistic support, but limited or no trial confirmation.
- **Limited** — suggestive but sparse, short-term, or conflicting.
- **Contested** — credentialed experts genuinely disagree (Section 14).

The deepest caveat in all of nutrition: most diet–disease evidence is **observational**, built on imperfect self-reported intake and confounded by the fact that healthy eaters tend to do many healthy things at once. This guide leans hardest where randomized trials and multiple independent lines of evidence agree, and hedges where they don't.

### Final note and disclaimer

This guide aims to capture *everything we can reliably say* about building the healthiest diet — and to be equally clear about what depends on you and where the science is still arguing. The recurring lesson across all cited sources is reassuring: the foundation is simple, robust, and forgiving. **Eat mostly minimally-processed plants, get enough protein, favor unsaturated fats, go easy on sugar/salt/ultra-processed food, drink water, don't overeat, and keep the pattern you can actually live with.** Almost everything else is fine-tuning.

> ⚠️ **This is general education, not medical or dietetic advice.** It does not account for your individual health, medications, allergies, or conditions, and it cannot replace a physician or registered dietitian. Nutrition science is probabilistic and evolving. **If you are pregnant, managing a medical condition, taking medications, underweight, or have any history of disordered eating, talk to a qualified professional before making significant changes.** Verify any specific number or citation against its primary source before relying on it.
