## 16. Diet Across the Lifespan: Infancy, Childhood, Pregnancy, and Healthy Aging

**Bottom line:** The same core holds at every age, but the edges of life shift the rules qualitatively — and the highest-stakes windows are the shortest (folic acid before you know you're pregnant, early allergen introduction at 4-11 months, bone built by the early 20s, more protein after ~65).


The shared core described throughout this guide — mostly minimally-processed plants, adequate protein, unsaturated fats, little added sugar or sodium — holds at every age. But life stage is the single largest *legitimate* modifier of that core (§08), and at the two ends of life it shifts requirements not just by dose but qualitatively: a six-month-old, a pregnant adult, and an eighty-year-old have genuinely different nutritional rules, not just different portion sizes. This section puts numbers on those rules.

Two themes recur. First, the highest-stakes windows are the shortest: the folic-acid window closes before most people know they're pregnant, the early-allergen window is roughly months 4–11, and peak bone mass is mostly banked by the early twenties. Second, "more" and "less" both matter at the edges — infants and elders are vulnerable to *deficiency* (iron, vitamin D, B12, protein) in a way healthy adults rarely are, while childhood is where lifelong patterns around sugar and around food-as-control are set.

This deepens §08's life-stage overview. It cross-references micronutrients (§03), supplements (§12), weight change (§10), and special diets (§17). Where I cite a single number, treat it as a guideline target, not a personalized prescription — clinical situations vary.

### Key evidence

**Infancy — about 6 months exclusive breastfeeding, then complementary foods, with early allergen introduction (strong).** WHO and the AAP recommend roughly 6 months of exclusive breastfeeding (or formula), then introducing complementary foods while continuing milk feeds [WHO, Breastfeeding](https://www.who.int/health-topics/breastfeeding). The timing of *allergenic* foods was reversed by trial evidence: the LEAP RCT found early introduction of peanut (age 4–11 months) cut peanut allergy by **81%** (intention-to-treat) by age 5 in high-risk infants — a 13.7% vs 1.9% absolute split (avoidance vs early-introduction) in the no-prior-sensitization stratum [Du Toit/LEAP, NEJM 2015](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1414850). The EAT trial introduced six allergens at 3 months in a general-population cohort; it missed its intention-to-treat endpoint but established that early introduction is *safe*, and per-protocol analyses favored early egg and peanut [Perkin/EAT, NEJM 2016](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1514210). NIAID's 2017 risk-stratified guidance is the practical playbook: introduce peanut early for low-risk babies at home, but for high-risk infants (severe eczema and/or egg allergy) test peanut-specific IgE or skin-prick first, sometimes with a supervised first feeding [NIAID 2017 Addendum Guidelines](https://www.niaid.nih.gov/sites/default/files/addendum-peanut-allergy-prevention-guidelines.pdf). Two hard rules: **no honey before 12 months** (infant botulism risk; spores survive cooking) and **no added sugar in early childhood** — the 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines (released January 2026) now advise avoiding added sugars until **about age 10**, a jump from the 2020–2025 "before age 2" line [USDA/HHS, Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025–2030](https://www.usda.gov/about-usda/news/press-releases/2026/01/07/kennedy-rollins-unveil-historic-reset-us-nutrition-policy-put-real-food-back-center-health); [CDC, Foods and Drinks to Avoid or Limit](https://www.cdc.gov/infant-toddler-nutrition/foods-and-drinks/foods-and-drinks-to-avoid-or-limit.html). Two micronutrients need active attention: breastfed infants should get **400 IU/day (10 mcg) vitamin D** from the first days of life, and fetal iron stores — laid down mostly in the third trimester — run low around **4–6 months**, so the AAP recommends exclusively/partially breastfed term infants get ~**1 mg/kg/day** iron starting at **4 months** until iron-rich complementary foods (fortified cereals, pureed meats, legumes) take over [AAP/HealthyChildren, Vitamin D & Iron Supplements](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/feeding-nutrition/Pages/Vitamin-Iron-Supplements.aspx).

**Adjust it — infant supplements scale with feeding mode, intake, and prematurity.**
- *Vitamin D — baseline 400 IU/day* for all breastfed/partially breastfed infants from day one. Scaling: a formula-fed infant taking **≥1 L (≈32 oz)/day** of standard fortified formula (≈400 IU/L) already hits 400 IU and needs no drops; below that volume, supplement to close the gap. Stop the separate drop once a weaned toddler drinks **≥1 qt/day** of fortified whole milk.
- *Iron — baseline ~1 mg/kg/day* from 4 months in exclusively breastfed term infants. Depends on: **formula-fed → 0** (iron-fortified formula at ~12 mg/L covers it); **preterm/low-birth-weight → ~2 mg/kg/day** from ~1 month (lower iron stores at birth); once eating ~2 servings/day of iron-rich/fortified foods, taper the supplement. Worked example: a 7-kg breastfed 5-month-old → ~7 mg/day until solids supply it.
- *Hard zeros (no scaling): no honey before 12 months; no added sugar in early childhood (2025–2030 DGA: avoid until ~age 10); no cow's milk as the main drink before 12 months.*

**Childhood/toddler — responsive feeding, variety, and a hard sugar ceiling (strong on sugar; moderate on feeding style).** Federal guidance tightened in January 2026: the 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines now advise **avoiding added sugars until about age 10** (up from the 2020–2025 "none before age 2"), with no single meal exceeding ~10 g of added sugar [USDA/HHS, Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025–2030](https://www.usda.gov/about-usda/news/press-releases/2026/01/07/kennedy-rollins-unveil-historic-reset-us-nutrition-policy-put-real-food-back-center-health); [The Nutrition Source, DGA 2025–2030 analysis](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/2026/01/09/dietary-guidelines-for-americans-2025-2030/). As a useful older expert ceiling for school-age children, the AHA's 2017 scientific statement recommends **≤25 g (≈6 teaspoons, ~100 kcal) of added sugar per day for ages 2–18**, *none* before age 2, and sugar-sweetened beverages limited to **≤1 eight-ounce serving per week** (the SSB cap is the statement's only Level-of-Evidence-A recommendation; the 25 g ceiling is the weaker Class IIa/Level C) [Vos et al., AHA Scientific Statement, Circulation 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5365373/).

**Adjust it — the sugar ceiling is an absolute cap, not a percentage, so it does *not* scale up with appetite.** Current federal target: **avoid added sugars entirely until ~age 10** (2025–2030 DGA). The AHA-2017 benchmark of **≤25 g/day for ages 2–18** (and **0 g** under age 2) remains a useful older expert ceiling once a school-age child does consume added sugar. For a smaller or younger child the cap is, if anything, tighter relative to total intake: 25 g is ~10% of a 1,000-kcal toddler day but only ~5% of a 2,000-kcal teen day, so use the lower-density end (aim well under 25 g) for the youngest in the 2–18 band. One 12-oz soda (~39 g) or one 8-oz juice box (~20–25 g) can blow the entire daily budget; a single sweetened drink also breaches the weekly SSB cap. This ceiling is separate from total carbohydrate — it applies only to *added* sugar (whole fruit and plain dairy don't count).

Responsive feeding — the caregiver decides *what* and *when*, the child decides *whether* and *how much* — is the consensus approach to building variety and self-regulation, and picky eating is usually a developmental phase managed by repeated low-pressure exposure, not coercion. The flip side is real: overt restriction, pressure, and "diet talk" around children are associated with later disordered eating and worse self-regulation, so a healthy childhood food environment is as much about *how* food is offered as *what* is offered. Whole cow's milk is generally introduced at 12 months; portion-appropriate dairy or fortified alternatives help meet calcium and vitamin D.

**Adolescence — growth, iron, and a narrow window to bank bone (strong).** Iron needs jump with the growth spurt and menstruation: the RDA is **15 mg/day for girls 14–18** versus 11 mg for boys, and iron deficiency is among the most common nutrient gaps in menstruating teens [NIH ODS, Iron](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/). Calcium peaks at **1,300 mg/day (ages 9–18)** with **600 IU vitamin D**, because adolescence is when most peak bone mass is laid down — a deficit here is hard to recover later [NIH ODS, Calcium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/); [NIH ODS, Vitamin D](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/). Athletes need more energy and iron, and require vigilance for low energy availability (RED-S). Adolescence is also the peak-onset window for eating disorders, so the same caution against restriction and weight-focused messaging applies with even higher stakes.

**Adjust it — teen iron, calcium, and D scale with sex, menstrual status, diet pattern, and training load.**
- *Iron — baseline 15 mg/day (girls 14–18), 11 mg/day (boys 14–18); 8 mg/day ages 9–13.* Depends on: **vegetarian/vegan → ×1.8 (~27 mg/day for a teen girl)**, because non-heme iron is ~2–3× less bioavailable; **heavy menstrual bleeding or diagnosed deficiency → treat per labs** (often 30–60+ mg elemental, clinician-directed), not just the RDA; **endurance/high-volume athletes → screen ferritin** (foot-strike hemolysis and hepcidin effects raise needs).
- *Calcium — baseline 1,300 mg/day (ages 9–18), the peak of any age group.* Depends on: **dairy-free/vegan → build from fortified soy milk (~300 mg/cup), tofu set with calcium, fortified juice, greens**; **amenorrheic athlete (low estrogen) → calcium + D become more urgent for bone, alongside fixing energy availability**. There is no upside to exceeding the **3,000 mg/day** UL (ages 9–18).
- *Vitamin D — baseline 600 IU/day (15 mcg).* Depends on: **little sun exposure, darker skin, or higher latitude → the 600 IU RDA is a floor; check 25(OH)D and supplement to keep it ≥20 ng/mL**; UL is 4,000 IU/day (ages 9+). Calcium is only useful for bone if D status is adequate.

**Pregnancy/lactation — modest extra energy but sharply higher micronutrient needs (strong).** Energy rises only **+0 kcal in the first trimester, ~+340 kcal/day in the second, and ~+452 in the third** (2005 DRI Estimated Energy Requirement) — pregnancy is emphatically not "eating for two" [NIH ODS, Pregnancy](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy-HealthProfessional/). Lactation's energy increment is **~+330 kcal/day for the first 6 months and ~+400 for months 7–12** (the true energy cost of exclusive milk production is ~500 kcal/day, with ~170 expected to come from mobilizing pregnancy fat stores). Micronutrient targets climb well above non-pregnant RDAs: **600 mcg DFE folate** (with **400–800 mcg supplemental folic acid started ≥1 month before conception** to prevent neural-tube defects), **27 mg iron**, **220 mcg iodine** (290 in lactation), **450 mg choline** (550 in lactation), and **≥200 mg DHA** on top of a general **≥250 mg/day EPA+DHA** baseline [NIH ODS, Pregnancy](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy-HealthProfessional/); [CDC, Folic Acid](https://www.cdc.gov/folic-acid/about/index.html). Gestational weight-gain targets follow the IOM 2009 ranges by pre-pregnancy BMI: **28–40 lb if underweight (BMI <18.5), 25–35 lb normal (18.5–24.9), 15–25 lb overweight (25–29.9), 11–20 lb obese (≥30)** [IOM/NASEM 2009, summary](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2013/01/weight-gain-during-pregnancy).

**Adjust it — pregnancy targets scale by trimester, pre-pregnancy BMI, parity, and diet pattern.**
- *Energy — baseline +0 / +340 / +452 kcal by trimester.* Depends on: **overweight/obese pre-pregnancy → use the low end or less** (IOM 2009 notes energy needs are lower; aim for the bottom of the gain range, not added calories); **twins → roughly +1 extra serving's worth again and a higher gain target** (37–54 lb normal-BMI twins). Don't front-load: there is *no* increase in the first trimester.
- *Weight gain — baseline by BMI band above, ~0.5–1 lb/week in trimesters 2–3.* Worked example: a normal-BMI person (target 25–35 lb) gains ~3–4 lb in T1, then ~1 lb/week. **Underweight → up to 40 lb; obese → as low as 11 lb.** This is the single clearest "what it depends on" lever in the section.
- *Iron — baseline 27 mg/day.* Depends on: **vegetarian/vegan → ×1.8 bioavailability adjustment (pair with vitamin C, separate from tea/coffee/calcium)**; **iron-deficiency anemia (Hb <11 g/dL T1/T3) → higher therapeutic dose per labs**; routine high-dose iron is not for everyone (constipation, and excess is not benign).
- *Folic acid — baseline 400 mcg supplemental from preconception.* Depends on: **prior NTD-affected pregnancy → 4,000 mcg (4 mg)/day under clinician supervision** (10× the routine dose); certain anti-seizure meds or malabsorption also raise the target.
- *DHA — baseline ≥200 mg/day added.* Depends on: **little/no fish intake or vegan → algal-oil supplement** (the limiting nutrient when the 8–12 oz/week fish window isn't used).

Food-safety avoidances are non-negotiable: listeria-risk foods (unpasteurized dairy, deli meats, refrigerated smoked seafood), high-mercury fish (the FDA "avoid" list: shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish from the Gulf of Mexico, plus bigeye tuna, marlin, orange roughy) — while still eating **8–12 oz/week of lower-mercury fish** (≈2–3 servings of ~4 oz; see §19) for the DHA — plus raw/undercooked animal foods and **alcohol (no known safe amount)** [FDA/EPA, Advice About Eating Fish](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish); [FDA, Listeria — Food Safety for Moms-to-Be](https://www.fda.gov/food/health-educators/listeria-food-safety-moms-be).

**Healthy aging — more protein, key micronutrient gaps, and pattern over pill (strong on protein/B12; moderate on longevity diets).** Older adults resist muscle-building stimuli ("anabolic resistance"), so expert groups recommend **1.0–1.2 g/kg/day protein** for healthy elders (more with illness), paired with resistance exercise, to counter sarcopenia [Bauer/PROT-AGE, JAMDA 2013](https://www.jamda.com/article/S1525-8610(13)00326-5/fulltext) (industry-funded: the EUGMS study group received a grant from Nestlé Nutrition, and most authors disclosed honoraria/grants from medical-nutrition companies — discount the upper end accordingly).

**Adjust it — older-adult protein scales with body weight, per-meal distribution, illness, and kidney function.**
- *Baseline 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day* (vs the 0.8 g/kg adult RDA; canonical protein math in §02/§20). Per-meal: aim **~0.4 g/kg/meal ≈ 25–30 g** across 3 meals to clear the higher leucine threshold (~2.5–3 g leucine) that anabolic resistance imposes — front-loading dinner while skimping at breakfast wastes the day's protein.
- *Depends on:* **acute or chronic illness / hospitalization → 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day**; **frailty or sarcopenia recovery → up to ~1.5 g/kg/day** with resistance training; **CKD G3–G5 not on dialysis → 0.55–0.60 g/kg/day (0.6–0.8 if diabetic), clinician-supervised** — a restriction that *overrides* the geriatric push, so this is the key conflict, decided on eGFR (KDOQI 2020; disease targets in §09); **on dialysis → back up to ~1.0–1.2 g/kg/day**. Worked example: a healthy 70-kg elder → ~70–84 g/day, ~25–30 g per meal; the same person hospitalized with pneumonia → ~84–105 g/day.

B12 absorption falls with age-related atrophic gastritis and PPI/metformin use — deficiency reached **~38% with atrophic gastritis** (vs 21% in PPI users, 15% in controls) in one cohort — so adults over ~50 should get B12 from fortified foods or supplements (the crystalline form doesn't need stomach acid) [Porter et al., AJCN 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8488868/). Vitamin D, hydration (thirst blunts with age), and attention to appetite and dentition round out the priorities.

**Adjust it — B12, vitamin D, and fluids scale with absorption, status, and body size.**
- *B12 — baseline 2.4 mcg/day RDA, but met from fortified food/supplements (not just meat) after ~50.* Depends on: **PPI or metformin use, or atrophic gastritis → check serum B12/methylmalonic acid; oral 100–250 mcg/day or 1,000 mcg if low** (crystalline B12 bypasses the acid requirement); **strict vegan → supplement is mandatory at any age** (§12).
- *Vitamin D — baseline 600 IU/day to age 70, then **800 IU/day (20 mcg) at 71+**.* Depends on: **housebound/institutionalized, darker skin, or measured 25(OH)D <20 ng/mL → 800–2,000 IU/day to correct** (UL 4,000 IU/day); pair with calcium 1,000–1,200 mg/day for bone.
- *Hydration — baseline ~1.6 L/day (women) / 2.0 L/day (men) total water from drinks, scaling to ~30 mL/kg/day.* Depends on: **fever, heat, or diuretics → add ~0.5 L per °C of fever / per hot day**; **heart failure or hyponatremia → fluid-restricted per clinician** (the one direction where less is the target). Blunted thirst means schedule fluids rather than wait for thirst.

For longevity, the strongest *human* signal is dietary pattern — Mediterranean and Blue Zones eating (largely plant-forward, legume-rich, moderate; §06) — not a supplement. Caloric restriction extends lifespan robustly in animals; the human evidence is short-term and modest: CALERIE 2 (220 randomized, 218 analyzed, 2 years) achieved only ~**11.9% restriction** — well below its 25% target, since sustained restriction is hard to adhere to — and improved cardiometabolic and some aging biomarkers, but it tested healthy non-obese adults briefly and does not establish a lifespan benefit [Dorling et al./CALERIE phase 2, Nutrition Reviews 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7727025/).

### What it depends on

- **Exact life stage and sex.** Iron jumps from 8 mg (9–13) to 15 mg (girls 14–18) and 27 mg (pregnancy); protein rises from 0.8 to 1.0–1.2 g/kg after ~65; allergen-introduction risk stratifies by eczema/egg-allergy status (high-risk → IgE/skin test before peanut).
- **Feeding mode in infancy.** Breastfed infants need 400 IU vitamin D from day one and ~1 mg/kg/day iron from 4 months; formula at ≥1 L/day (≈400 IU + ~12 mg iron/L) covers both.
- **Pre-pregnancy BMI.** Sets the gestational weight-gain range (IOM 2009): **28–40 / 25–35 / 15–25 / 11–20 lb** for underweight/normal/overweight/obese.
- **Diet pattern.** Vegan/vegetarian raises non-heme iron needs ×1.8, makes B12 supplementation mandatory, and shifts DHA to algal oil and iodine to deliberate sources (§12, §17).
- **Medications and gut health.** PPIs, metformin, and atrophic gastritis cut B12 absorption — deficiency ~38% with atrophic gastritis vs 15% controls — so use crystalline B12 (§15).

### Practical takeaways

- **Infants:** exclusive milk to ~6 months, then iron-rich complementary foods; introduce peanut/egg early (risk-stratify high-risk babies with a clinician); **no honey before 12 months; avoid added sugar in early childhood** (2025–2030 DGA: until ~age 10); give breastfed babies **400 IU vitamin D** daily (skip if formula-fed ≥1 L/day) and **~1 mg/kg/day iron from 4 months** (skip if formula-fed; ~2 mg/kg if preterm).
- **Toddlers/children:** offer variety with low-pressure repeated exposure; the 2025–2030 DGA now advises **avoiding added sugar until ~age 10**, with the older AHA-2017 ceiling of **≤25 g/day (none before 2)** as a fallback benchmark for school-age kids; keep SSBs to **≤1 eight-oz serving/week**; the cap is absolute, so go lower for the youngest; avoid restriction, weight comments, and "good/bad food" framing.
- **Teens:** prioritize iron (menstruating girls **15 mg/day**, boys 11 mg, **×1.8 if vegetarian**) and calcium (**1,300 mg**) + vitamin D (**600 IU**, more if status is low) for peak bone mass; watch athletes for low energy availability and watch everyone for disordered eating.
- **Pregnancy:** **400–800 mcg folic acid from ≥1 month before conception** (4 mg if prior NTD), then 600 mcg folate, 27 mg iron (×1.8 vegetarian), 220 mcg iodine, 450 mg choline, **≥200 mg added DHA**; add **+0/+340/+452 kcal by trimester** (low end if overweight/obese); follow IOM weight-gain ranges by pre-pregnancy BMI; avoid listeria foods, high-mercury fish, raw animal foods, and all alcohol. **Lactation:** ~+330–400 kcal/day, iodine 290 mcg, choline 550 mg.
- **Older adults:** raise protein to **1.0–1.2 g/kg/day** (~25–30 g per meal), 1.2–1.5 with illness, but **0.55–0.8 g/kg if CKD pre-dialysis**, plus resistance training; supplement or fortify **B12** and check vitamin D (**800 IU at 71+**); mind hydration (~30 mL/kg/day), appetite, and dental health; favor a Mediterranean/Blue Zones *pattern* over longevity supplements.

### Unsettled or debated

- **The optimal protein target for older and sarcopenic adults** is debated (estimates ~1.0 to ~1.5+ g/kg/day); the *direction* (above the 0.8 g/kg RDA) is solid consensus, the exact number is not.
- **Caloric restriction for human longevity** is genuinely unsettled — compelling in animals, but human data (CALERIE) are short-term and show biomarker shifts, not proven lifespan extension; sustained restriction also risks muscle and bone loss, especially in older adults.
- **General-population early allergen introduction** beyond peanut/egg (e.g., the full EAT panel) has weaker trial support; the strongest evidence remains early peanut in high-risk infants.
