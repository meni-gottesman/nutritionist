## 07. Beverages & Hydration

**Bottom line:** Make water (or unsweetened coffee/tea) your default and treat swapping out a daily sugary drink as one of the highest-yield changes in the whole guide; coffee up to ~400 mg caffeine is fine, and the goal beyond sugar is still water, not diet drinks.


Beverages are the most under-rated lever in the whole diet. The reason is mechanical: liquid calories slip past the body's satiety machinery almost unnoticed, so a sugary drink adds energy without the "I'm full" signal that the same calories from food would trigger. And because the *fluid* itself is just water, two glasses of identical volume can be net-protective or net-harmful depending entirely on what's dissolved in them. That makes "what you drink" one of the highest-leverage choices in this guide — and one of the easiest to fix.

The evidence supports a fairly stable hierarchy. At the top: water, plus unsweetened coffee and tea. In the middle: cow's milk, fortified soy milk, and small amounts of 100% juice. At the bottom: sugar-sweetened beverages, energy drinks, and alcohol. Swapping a daily sugary drink for water is, frankly, one of the most evidence-backed single changes you can make.

Two areas are in genuine scientific motion, and I'll flag them honestly as we go. Alcohol is covered in §05 (with the adjustable limits); the short version is that the old "a glass of red is good for you" J-curve has largely collapsed under scrutiny — the WHO now states plainly that no level of alcohol consumption is safe for health [WHO 2023](https://www.who.int/europe/news/item/04-01-2023-no-level-of-alcohol-consumption-is-safe-for-our-health). **The health-optimal target is 0; if you drink, the *ceiling* (not a goal) is ≤1 standard drink/day for women and ≤2 for men (≤14 g / ≤28 g ethanol), adjusted down toward zero for cancer/liver risk or any history of alcohol use disorder — full numbers in §05.** The live debate within *this* section is low/no-calorie sweeteners, where short-term randomized trials and long-term observational cohorts point in opposite directions.

A note on certainty: I'll mark where evidence is strong, where it's observational only, and where it's genuinely contested.

### Key evidence

**How much water — less precise than the "8 glasses" rule implies (moderate).** The European Food Safety Authority sets adequate total-water intakes of about **2.5 L/day for men and 2.0 L/day for women**, counting all sources [EFSA 2010](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/nda100326). Roughly 80% comes from drinks and 20% from food, so the *beverage* target is about 2.0 L (men) and 1.6 L (women) — not far from "eight glasses" (eight 8-oz/240-mL glasses ≈ 1.9 L), which turns out to be a decent heuristic rather than a derived requirement. The US NASEM/IOM figures are higher (3.7 L men, 2.7 L women) but are population *averages* of total water, not minimums [NASEM 2004](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/report-sets-dietary-intake-levels-for-water-salt-and-potassium-to-maintain-health-and-reduce-chronic-disease-risk). For most healthy adults, thirst is an adequate guide and pale-yellow urine a practical marker. Both extremes carry real risk: hyponatremia from extreme overdrinking (e.g., endurance athletes who over-drink water without electrolytes), and chronically concentrated urine (with possible kidney-stone and CKD-progression links) from sustained underdrinking. Precise individual requirements aren't sharply defined — but they *do* scale predictably with body size, sweat losses, and life stage, so the target should be set per-person rather than as a flat number.

**Adjust it —** *baseline:* total water ≈ **2.5 L/day (men) / 2.0 L/day (women)**, of which ~80% (≈2.0 / 1.6 L) is from drinks [EFSA 2010](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/nda100326). *How it scales:* a body-weight rule of **~30–35 mL/kg/day total water** is the standard clinical rule of thumb — so a 60-kg person ≈ 1.8–2.1 L, a 90-kg person ≈ 2.7–3.2 L. *What it depends on (each with a number):*
  - **Exercise or heat → add ~0.4–0.8 L of fluid per hour of activity** (ACSM endurance range; lighter/cooler at the low end, heavier athletes in hot conditions at the high end) [ACSM 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277604/). Sessions >1 h or sweat losses >~1 L/h also need sodium — **sweat sodium ≈ 0.5–1.5 g/L** (highly individual, up to ~2.5 g/L in heavy "salty sweaters"), so plain water alone can dilute serum sodium; aim ~300–600 mg sodium/h on long efforts.
  - **Pregnancy → +~0.3 L/day** total water (EFSA scales it with the +300 kcal/day of gestation) [EFSA 2010](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/nda100326).
  - **Lactation → +~0.7 L/day** (replaces ~700 mL/day of milk output) [EFSA 2010](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/nda100326).
  - **Fever → +~10–12% of maintenance fluid per 1 °C above normal**; vomiting/diarrhea raise needs further (oral rehydration salts if losses are large).
  - **Clinical fluid *restriction* reverses the sign:** advanced heart failure or hyponatremia/SIADH is often capped at **~1.5–2 L/day**, and refractory cases at **<0.8–1.0 L/day** [PMC 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8142352/); dialysis-dependent and oliguric CKD patients have individualized limits set by their nephrology team. If you have kidney, heart, or liver failure, follow the prescribed limit — the baseline above does not apply. *Worked example:* a 70-kg recreational runner doing a 90-min summer session ≈ 2.4 L baseline (35 mL/kg) **+** ~0.9–1.2 L replacing sweat ≈ **~3.3–3.6 L** for that day, with electrolytes.

**Coffee — net beneficial or neutral, not a vice (moderate, observational).** This surprises people: 3–4 cups a day is associated with the *lowest* all-cause mortality. A large dose-response meta-analysis (21 cohorts, ~997,000 participants) put the nadir at 4 cups/day, with all-cause mortality roughly **16% lower than non-drinkers** (RR ~0.84, 95% CI 0.82–0.87); the CVD-mortality benefit peaked at 3 cups/day (~21% lower) [Am J Epidemiol 2014](https://academic.oup.com/aje/article/180/8/763/2739131). Umbrella reviews link moderate coffee to lower risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular and liver disease, and Parkinson's [BMJ 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5696634/). Decaf shows similar benefits, which implicates polyphenols and chlorogenic acids, not just caffeine. The caveats are real but narrow: unfiltered/boiled coffee (French press, espresso, Turkish) contains cafestol and kahweol that raise LDL cholesterol; high intake disturbs sleep and acutely raises blood pressure; and caffeine should stay under ~200 mg/day in pregnancy. EFSA judges habitual intakes up to 400 mg/day, and single doses up to 200 mg (~3 mg/kg), safe for most adults, with a 200 mg/day ceiling in pregnancy [EFSA 2015](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102); the US FDA likewise cites 400 mg/day as the level "not generally associated with negative effects" in healthy adults [FDA 2024](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much), while the 200-mg pregnancy limit is ACOG's [ACOG 2010](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2010/08/moderate-caffeine-consumption-during-pregnancy). This is observational data, so causation isn't proven — but the consistency is reassuring.

**Adjust it —** *baseline:* caffeine ceiling ≈ **400 mg/day** for healthy non-pregnant adults [EFSA 2015](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102). *How it converts to cups (this is the catch — "4 cups" assumes a small cup):* an 8-oz/240-mL brewed coffee ≈ **~95 mg** (range ~70–140), so 400 mg ≈ **~4 small cups but only ~2 large (16-oz) mugs**; an espresso shot ≈ **~65 mg**; 8-oz black tea ≈ **~47 mg**, green tea ≈ **~28 mg**; a 16-oz energy drink ≈ **~150–300 mg** [Mayo Clinic 2024](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/in-depth/caffeine/art-20049372). Count *total* caffeine (pre-workout, soda, chocolate, supplements), not just coffee. *What it depends on (each with a number):*
  - **Pregnancy → ≤200 mg/day** (~2 small cups); caffeine half-life roughly *doubles to triples* in late pregnancy (from ~3–5 h to ~8–18 h), so it lingers far longer for both mother and fetus [ACOG 2010](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2010/08/moderate-caffeine-consumption-during-pregnancy).
  - **Children/adolescents → ~3 mg/kg/day** (EFSA), i.e. ~120 mg for a 40-kg child — and energy drinks are best avoided entirely [EFSA 2015](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102).
  - **Slow CYP1A2 metabolizers (carriers of the *1F/C allele, ~50–60% of people) → effectively a lower ceiling.** In a prospective cohort of young adults, slow metabolizers who were heavy coffee drinkers had a **3.0× risk of developing hypertension** needing treatment (HR 3.00, 95% CI 1.53–5.90) and ~1.7× at moderate intake, while fast (*1A/*1A) metabolizers showed no increase — and possibly a lower risk [Palatini, J Hypertens 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19451835/). No genotype test? Use your body as the assay: if coffee gives you palpitations, jitter, or wrecks sleep, **cap at ~1–2 cups and none after ~2 pm** (caffeine's ~5-h half-life means a 3 pm 100-mg coffee still leaves ~33 mg at an 11 pm bedtime — about 8 h, ≈1.6 half-lives).
  - **Oral contraceptives → metabolism slows (half-life ~doubles)**, so the same dose hits harder — treat ~200–300 mg as your practical ceiling.
  - **Smoking → metabolism speeds up (clearance ~doubles, half-life ~halves)**, which is why smokers tolerate more; this is not a reason to drink more, and it flips when you quit.
  - **Single-dose cap:** keep any one serving **≤200 mg (~3 mg/kg)** to avoid acute anxiety/BP spikes, regardless of the daily total [EFSA 2015](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102).

**Tea — modest benefit, clearest for green (moderate, observational).** A meta-analysis of 38 cohorts (~1.96 million people) found the highest tea intake associated with **~10% lower all-cause and ~14% lower CVD mortality**, with each additional cup tracking ~1.5% lower all-cause mortality [Epidemiol Health 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11573487/); [Adv Nutr 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32073596/). Green tea (rich in catechins like EGCG) shows the clearest signal. The benefits are real but smaller and less consistent than coffee's. Two caveats: added sugar negates the benefit, and very hot beverages above ~65 °C are linked to esophageal cancer (IARC classes drinking very hot beverages above 65 °C as "probably carcinogenic," Group 2A) — so let it cool [IARC 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK543943/).

**Sugar-sweetened beverages — the strongest harm signal of any drink (strong).** This is the clearest "minimize or eliminate" category in the whole guide, and it's the rare nutrition claim that holds up across study designs. Each additional daily serving is associated with about **27% higher type 2 diabetes risk** (RR 1.27, 95% CI 1.15–1.41) and **~9% higher CVD risk** (RR 1.09), and a weaker, less consistent signal for all-cause mortality (the per-serving estimate, RR 1.10, 95% CI 0.97–1.26, is not statistically significant; only the highest-vs-lowest comparison reaches significance, RR 1.14, 95% CI 1.04–1.24) [Nutrients 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8402166/). A typical 12-oz can holds 30–42 g (7–10 tsp) of added sugar and ~150 calories that don't trigger fullness, driving passive overconsumption, weight gain, fatty liver, and dental caries. SSBs are the single largest source of added sugar in the US diet [Harvard Nutrition Source 2023](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/healthy-drinks/sugary-drinks/), and the CDC likewise identifies them as a leading source of added sugars tied to weight gain, type 2 diabetes, heart disease, fatty liver, and dental caries [CDC 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/nutrition/php/data-research/sugar-sweetened-beverages.html). For context, the AHA caps added sugar at ≤36 g/day for men and ≤25 g/day for women — one can can blow the whole budget [AHA 2024](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/eat-smart/sugar/how-much-sugar-is-too-much).

**Adjust it —** *baseline:* the optimal intake is **0 servings/day** — this is the one beverage with no protective threshold, so the target is elimination, not moderation. *How the dose scales (every serving counts, linearly):* each additional 12-oz/355-mL serving/day ≈ **+27% type-2-diabetes risk** and **+9% CVD risk** and adds ~30–42 g sugar + ~150 kcal [Nutrients 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8402166/) — so cutting from 2/day to 0 removes ~300 "invisible" kcal, which at a ~500-kcal deficit ≈ 0.45 kg/week (§20) is more than half a week's loss. *What a realistic non-zero ceiling depends on:* if you won't hit zero, the binding constraint is the **added-sugar budget** — **≤25 g/day (women) / ≤36 g/day (men)** total from *all* sources [AHA 2024](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/eat-smart/sugar/how-much-sugar-is-too-much), and one full-sugar can (~39 g) already exceeds both. Tighter still for: **diabetes or prediabetes → effectively 0** (these are the people the per-serving risk hits hardest); **children → the current US Dietary Guidelines (2025–2030, released Jan 2026) advise *avoiding* added sugars until about age 10, up from the previous "before age 2" [DGA 2025–2030](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/); the older AHA-2017 benchmark of <25 g/day total (and *no* SSBs before age 2) remains a useful expert ceiling for school-age children [AHA 2024](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/eat-smart/sugar/how-much-sugar-is-too-much)**; **a glycogen-depleted endurance athlete mid-race** is the rare context where rapid-acting carbohydrate (30–60 g/h) is a *feature*, not a harm — context-specific fuel, not daily hydration.

**100% juice — better than soda, not equal to fruit (moderate).** Juice has vitamins and polyphenols, but it delivers concentrated free sugars *without* the fiber, so it behaves more like a sugary drink than like fruit. Whole fruit is protective (≥2 weekly servings of certain fruits linked to ~23% lower diabetes risk), while ≥1 daily serving of juice tracks with up to **~21% higher diabetes risk**, and ≥250 g/day is associated with higher overall and heart-disease mortality [Muraki BMJ 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4073481/); [PMC 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9144949/). The ADA suggests capping juice at ~4 oz (120 mL)/day. Bottom line: eat the fruit, don't drink it.

**Adjust it —** *baseline:* **≤120 mL (4 oz)/day** of 100% juice for adults, taken with a meal (the fat/protein/fiber blunts the glucose spike). *How risk scales with dose:* ≥1 serving/day tracks with up to **~21% higher diabetes risk**, and **≥250 g/day** with higher overall and cardiovascular mortality — so the harm is dose-dependent, and the per-serving signal for juice resembles SSBs more than whole fruit [Muraki BMJ 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4073481/); [PMC 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9144949/). *What the cap depends on — age (AAP limits):* **none under age 1**; **≤120 mL (4 oz)/day ages 1–3**; **≤120–180 mL (4–6 oz)/day ages 4–6**; **≤240 mL (8 oz)/day ages 7+** [AAP 2017](https://publications.aap.org/aapnews/news/14804/Weighing-in-on-fruit-juice-AAP-now-says-no-juice). Tighten toward 0 for **diabetes/prediabetes or active weight loss**; the one place a fast free-sugar hit helps is **treating hypoglycemia** (~15 g, e.g. ~120 mL juice, then recheck).

**Milk and plant milks — fortification is everything (moderate).** Cow's milk (~8 g protein/cup, plus calcium, vitamin D, B12, iodine) and fortified soy milk (~7–8 g protein) are the nutritionally complete options. Fortified soy is, in fact, the *only* plant milk the US Dietary Guidelines count in the dairy group; in an analysis of 1,042 US plant-based milks, only about **11% met proposed fluid-milk-substitute nutrient standards** — and the few that did were mostly soy [Drewnowski 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9695877/) (industry-funded: Nestlé). Almond and oat milks typically carry only **~1 g protein/100 g** (well below dairy's ~3.3 g) and, unless fortified, lag on calcium, D, B12, and iodine — so choose unsweetened, fortified versions and treat sweetened/flavored milks as SSBs.

**Adjust it —** *baseline:* if a beverage is doing nutritional work in your diet, prefer cow's milk or fortified soy (**~7–8 g protein/cup**); almond/oat (**~1 g protein/cup**) is closer to flavored water with fat and should be unsweetened and fortified. *How the protein gap scales:* swapping 2 cups/day of dairy/soy for unfortified almond milk drops ~14–16 g protein — meaningful against a daily target of **~0.8 g/kg for sedentary adults up to ~1.2–1.6 g/kg for active or older adults** (canonical math in §02/§20). *What the choice depends on:* **calcium/D/B12/iodine intake** — if milk is your main source, an *unfortified* plant milk leaves a gap (dairy/fortified soy give ~300 mg calcium and ~2.5 µg D per cup; unfortified almond ~off the chart low), so check the label for added calcium (~120 mg/100 mL), D, and B12; **added sugar** — a "vanilla" or "original" plant milk often carries ~7–12 g sugar/cup and should count against the SSB budget above; **lactose intolerance or dairy avoidance →** fortified soy is the evidence-backed swap that preserves the dairy-group profile.

**Low/no-calorie sweeteners — genuinely contested (see below).**

### What it depends on

- **Climate, activity, and physiology.** Water needs rise with heat, exercise, fever, pregnancy/lactation, and high salt/protein intake — and fall in cool, sedentary conditions. Kidney disease changes the calculus entirely.
- **What's *in* the cup, not the volume.** Coffee's and tea's benefits assume little or no added sugar and cream; sweetened versions forfeit them.
- **Brewing and temperature.** Unfiltered coffee raises LDL; drinks hotter than ~65 °C raise esophageal cancer risk.
- **Dose, for juice.** ~120 mL with a meal is low-risk; daily large glasses are not. Whole fruit is always preferable.
- **Fortification, for plant milks.** An unfortified almond milk is essentially flavored water with a little fat; a fortified soy milk is a legitimate dairy substitute.
- **Life stage.** Pregnancy lowers the caffeine ceiling to ~200 mg/day; children have tight age-based juice limits.

### Practical takeaways

- **Make water (or unsweetened coffee/tea) your default.** Aim roughly for 2.0 L/day of fluids if you're a man, 1.6 L if you're a woman (≈30–35 mL/kg total water), and **add ~0.4–0.8 L per hour of exercise/heat**, **+~0.3 L pregnant, +~0.7 L lactating** — but let thirst and pale urine guide you, and follow a *prescribed* limit (~1.5–2 L or less) if you have heart, kidney, or liver failure.
- **Keep drinking your coffee.** Up to ~400 mg caffeine/day (≈4 small/2 large cups, all sources counted) is fine for most adults; 3–4 cups is associated with the lowest mortality. Drop to **≤200 mg if pregnant**, and **≤1–2 cups if you're a slow metabolizer** (palpitations/poor sleep are the tell). Skip the sugar, and prefer filtered if your LDL runs high.
- **Treat soda, sports/energy drinks, and sweetened lattes as occasional treats, not hydration.** This is the single highest-yield swap in the whole guide — the CDC's core advice is simply to choose water in their place [CDC 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-weight-growth/rethink-your-drink/index.html).
- **Eat fruit; cap juice at ~120 mL/day** and take it with a meal if at all.
- **For milk, choose unsweetened cow's milk or fortified soy** for a complete profile; read labels on almond/oat for added sugar and fortification.
- **Use diet drinks as a bridge off sugar, not a destination** — water remains the goal (see below).

### Unsettled or debated

**Low- and no-calorie sweeteners (LNCS) are the live controversy.** The two best lines of evidence disagree. Short-term **randomized trials** (4 weeks to 3 years) find neutral-to-modestly-*beneficial* effects on body weight when sweeteners replace sugar. Long-term **observational cohorts** find associations with higher BMI, diabetes, and CVD — but those are heavily confounded by reverse causation, because people often switch to sweeteners precisely *because* they already carry metabolic risk. Bias-adjusted analyses line up more with the trials.

In 2023 the **WHO issued a conditional recommendation against using non-sugar sweeteners for weight control or NCD prevention** — on *low-certainty* evidence, and the recommendation is openly contested by trialists (WHO explicitly excludes people with pre-existing diabetes from this recommendation) [WHO 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-05-2023-who-advises-not-to-use-non-sugar-sweeteners-for-weight-control-in-newly-released-guideline); [Appl Physiol Nutr Metab 2025](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/10.1139/apnm-2025-0440); [Eur J Clin Nutr 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10630128/). On safety, IARC classified aspartame as Group 2B ("possibly carcinogenic," limited evidence), but JECFA left the acceptable daily intake at 40 mg/kg — a 70-kg adult would need roughly **9–14 cans of diet soda a day** to exceed it [WHO/IARC/JECFA 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-07-2023-aspartame-hazard-and-risk-assessment-results-released). The honest read: diet drinks are far less harmful than sugar and make a reasonable bridge off it, but the goal is still water.
