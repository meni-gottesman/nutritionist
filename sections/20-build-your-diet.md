## 20. Build *Your* Healthiest Diet — The Framework, the Matrix, and Sample Days

**Bottom line:** Start from the core (not a brand-name diet), set your calorie ballpark and meet it with filling whole foods, turn only the one or two dials that apply to you (use the matrix), pick a pattern you'll keep, and make it cheap and sustainable — the master table turns each dial into your exact number.


Everything before this was the evidence. This section turns it into something you can act on tomorrow. It gives you (a) a simple visual model, (b) a five-step method to construct your own diet, (c) a master **"it depends" matrix** that pulls together every personalization dial from the guide, and (d) concrete sample days for different people.

> 🧮 **This section is interactive in the HTML version of the guide:** it opens with a live **diet engine** that turns your biological sex, age, height, weight, **body-fat %**, daily activity, **exercise calories burned**, **allergies/restrictions**, **health conditions**, and goal (lose fat · build muscle · recomposition at the same weight · maintain & get healthier) into a full personalized target set — calories, protein/carb/fat, fiber, sugar, saturated & trans fat, dietary cholesterol, sodium, alcohol, caffeine, produce, whole grains, fish, legumes/nuts, red-meat limits, water, and a **complete vitamin-and-mineral panel (25+ micronutrients, all DRIs)**, plus **condition- and allergy-specific adjustments** — organized into *energy & macros · daily limits · food targets · vitamins · minerals*, each shown with the rule behind it. It also includes an optional, **on-device refiner**: log your weight every week or so (saved only in your browser — nothing leaves your device) and it **back-calculates your *measured* maintenance calories** to replace the formula estimate — the single most accurate refinement — plus a tape-measure body-fat estimator and a quick hunger/energy check-in. In *any* format, the **adjustable-targets master table** below gives every number, how it scales, and exactly what moves it.

### The healthy plate (the visual default)

A reliable, evidence-aligned default for a single meal — the model used by Harvard's Healthy Eating Plate ([Harvard T.H. Chan, The Nutrition Source](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/healthy-eating-plate/)) and echoed by most national guidelines ([USDA/HHS, Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/); [WHO 2020, Healthy diet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet)):

```
        THE HEALTHY PLATE (per meal)
 ┌───────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │   ½  VEGETABLES & FRUIT                     │
 │       (more veg than fruit; variety/color)  │
 ├──────────────────────┬──────────────────────┤
 │  ¼  WHOLE GRAINS      │  ¼  PROTEIN          │
 │   oats, brown rice,   │  fish, legumes, nuts,│
 │   quinoa, whole wheat │  poultry, eggs, tofu │
 └──────────────────────┴──────────────────────┘
   + Healthy fats: olive oil, nuts, seeds, avocado
   + Drink: water, coffee, or tea (not soda)
   Move your body; the plate is a default, not a law.
```

### The five-step method

1. **Start from the core, not from a brand-name diet.** Mostly plants, minimally processed, unsaturated fats, enough protein, little added sugar/sodium — the same foundation national and global authorities converge on ([WHO 2020, Healthy diet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet); [USDA/HHS, Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/)). Don't engineer a special diet — engineer *this* one to fit you.
2. **Set your calorie ballpark, then prioritize filling foods.** Estimate needs from body size and activity using the calorie engine in the next block — Mifflin–St Jeor by default, or Katch–McArdle if you enter your body-fat %, with an optional exercise-calorie override (the weight-management evidence behind it is in §10), then meet them mostly with protein-, fiber-, and water-rich whole foods so satiety takes care of portion control. You rarely need to count calories if the food quality is high.
3. **Turn the one or two dials that actually apply to you** (use the matrix below). Pregnant? Older? Athlete? Diabetic? Plant-based? Each changes the *emphasis* of a few nutrients — not the foundation.
4. **Pick a pattern you'll keep.** Mediterranean and DASH have the best evidence — a Mediterranean diet supplemented with olive oil or nuts cut major cardiovascular events by ~30% versus a control diet in the PREDIMED randomized trial (see §06) — but choose the version that fits your culture, schedule, palate, and budget. Adherence beats theoretical optimality every time.
5. **Make it sustainable and cheap where it counts.** Lean on cheap staples (legumes, oats, eggs, frozen veg), cut beef/lamb for the biggest environmental win, reduce waste, and supplement only the genuine gaps (Section 12).

### Personalize to your numbers (age · sex · height · weight · BMI)

The framework above is qualitative; this block makes it quantitative. Plug in your own age, sex, height, and weight to set a calorie target, screen your weight status, and size your protein. Treat every output as a starting estimate to adjust against real-world results, not a verdict.

**1) Estimate your calories.** The Mifflin–St Jeor equation is the most validated way to estimate resting metabolic rate from body size ([Mifflin et al. 1990, *Am J Clin Nutr*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2305711/)):

```
  Men:   BMR = 10 · weight(kg) + 6.25 · height(cm) − 5 · age(yr) + 5
  Women: BMR = 10 · weight(kg) + 6.25 · height(cm) − 5 · age(yr) − 161
```

If you know your **body-fat %**, the **Katch–McArdle** equation is more accurate because it works from lean mass rather than total weight: **BMR = 370 + 21.6 × lean body mass (kg)**, where lean mass = weight(kg) × (1 − body-fat%/100). The interactive calculator switches to this automatically when you enter body fat (and then doses protein on lean mass).

Then scale BMR to your maintenance calories with an **additive** model — the one the interactive calculator uses. Pick your **daily activity: the movement in your life that is _not_ a workout** (your job and lifestyle), then add any **workout calories on top**. This avoids the classic error of a single "activity multiplier" that already bakes in an *assumed* amount of exercise and then double-counts it when you also log a workout:

**maintenance ≈ BMR × daily-activity factor + workout kcal** · capped at **BMR × 2.4** (the physical-activity level that is sustainable long-term — FAO/WHO/UNU 2001)

| Daily activity — *not counting workouts* | Factor |
|---|---|
| Mostly sitting — desk job | 1.25 |
| Lightly active — some standing/walking | 1.45 |
| On your feet most of the day (nurse, server, retail) | 1.7 |
| Physical / manual-labor job | 1.9 |
| Very heavy labor | 2.2 |

*These are NEAT-dominant occupational factors (IOM/NASEM DRI 2005; FAO/WHO/UNU 2001) — deliberately **not** the classic 1.2–1.9 "activity multipliers," which already fold in workouts and would double-count when you add exercise on top. Log structured exercise separately as calories; treat wearable calorie figures as high and recheck against your 2–4-week weight trend.*

*Worked example* — a 35-year-old woman, 165 cm, 68 kg, lightly active desk-plus-walking lifestyle, no separate workout logged:
BMR = 10·68 + 6.25·165 − 5·35 − 161 = 680 + 1,031 − 175 − 161 ≈ **1,375 kcal/day**.
maintenance = 1,375 × 1.45 ≈ **1,990 kcal/day**. If she also logs a 300-kcal workout, her day becomes ≈ **2,290 kcal**.

**Adjust it — turning TDEE into a fat-loss or muscle-gain target.** Baseline maintenance = BMR × activity factor. From there:

- **To lose fat:** subtract **−500 to −750 kcal/day** (≈ −0.45 to −0.68 kg/week, using ~7,700 kcal/kg). The old "3,500 kcal = 1 lb" rule overestimates real loss over months because expenditure adapts downward as you shrink, so expect closer to **−0.25 to −0.45 kg/week** in practice and recompute as weight drops (§10). *Depends on, with deltas:* **lean/athletic (<15% body fat men, <22% women) → hold to −300 to −500 kcal/day (≤0.5–0.7%/week)**, since steeper cuts measurably accelerate muscle loss; **BMI ≥30 → can tolerate up to −750 kcal/day (~1%/week)**; **floor: do not drop below ~1,200 kcal/day (women) / ~1,500 kcal/day (men)** without clinical supervision.
- **To gain lean mass:** add **+250–500 kcal/day** (≈ +10–20% above maintenance) for **+0.25–0.5% of body weight/week**, paired with resistance training so the surplus builds muscle, not fat (§10). *Depends on:* **novice/younger/returning → upper end (~+0.5%/week, +10–20%)**; **advanced (many years trained) → lower end (~+0.25%/week, +5–10%)**, because muscle accretion is biologically capped; **gaining >~0.5%/week or waist climbing → trim the surplus ~250 kcal/day**.
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** add to maintenance, not subtract — **T2 → +340 kcal/day, T3 → +452 kcal/day** (T1 unchanged), exclusive lactation → **+330 kcal/day (months 0–6), +400 (7–12)** — the ~500 kcal/day milk cost partly offset by ~170 from fat stores (§08).

These are population averages — Mifflin–St Jeor lands within 10% of measured resting metabolism in only ~82% of people (max overestimation ~15%, underestimation ~18%), and individual TDEE varies by **±10–15%** ([Frankenfield et al. 2005, *J Am Diet Assoc*](https://www.jandonline.org/article/S0002-8223(05)00149-5/abstract)). Use the number as a hypothesis and recalibrate from how your weight actually trends over **2–4 weeks** ([NIH/NHLBI, BMI & body-weight planning](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/educational/lose_wt/BMI/bmicalc.htm)).

**2) BMI — useful screen, blunt tool.** BMI = weight(kg) ÷ height(m)². It is a quick population screen, not a body-fat measurement ([WHO 2024, Obesity and overweight](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/obesity-and-overweight); [CDC, Adult BMI categories](https://www.cdc.gov/bmi/adult-calculator/bmi-categories.html)):

| Category | BMI (kg/m²) |
|---|---|
| Underweight | < 18.5 |
| Healthy weight | 18.5 – 24.9 |
| Overweight | 25.0 – 29.9 |
| Obesity, class I | 30.0 – 34.9 |
| Obesity, class II | 35.0 – 39.9 |
| Obesity, class III | ≥ 40.0 |

In the worked example above, BMI = 68 ÷ 1.65² ≈ **24.98** — the very top of the "healthy" band (18.5–24.9), a hair below the 25.0 overweight cutoff, which shows how a single point near a boundary can mislead without context (one kilogram either way flips the label).

- **Ethnicity shifts the cut-points (Adjust it).** Baseline overweight/obesity thresholds are **25.0 / 30.0**. For many **South, Southeast, and East Asian** populations, a WHO expert consultation flagged higher cardiometabolic risk at lower BMIs and set additional action points at **≥23** (increased risk) and **≥27.5** (high risk) kg/m² — roughly a **−2 to −2.5 unit** downshift of the whole scale ([WHO Expert Consultation 2004, *The Lancet*](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(03)15268-3/abstract)). So a BMI of **24** reads "healthy" by the standard table but "increased risk" for someone of Asian ancestry. The thresholds are *unchanged* for most other groups.
- **Pair BMI with waist circumference (Adjust it).** Baseline action point (US/NHLBI): **>102 cm (40 in) men, >88 cm (35 in) women** ([NIH/NHLBI, Aim for a Healthy Weight — Assessing Your Risk](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/educational/lose_wt/risk.htm)). *Depends on ancestry:* the IDF uses ethnicity-specific cut-offs — for South Asian, Chinese, Japanese, and other Asian populations, central obesity starts lower, at **≥90 cm men / ≥80 cm women** ([IDF consensus worldwide definition of the metabolic syndrome, 2006](https://sites.pitt.edu/~super1/Metabolic/IDF1.pdf)). Waist-to-height ratio is an even simpler screen: keep waist **< half your height** (ratio <0.5) regardless of sex or ethnicity.
- **Where BMI misleads:** it can't distinguish muscle from fat (athletes read "overweight"), ignores fat distribution, and reads differently across ethnicities, in older adults (who lose lean mass), and in pregnancy (where it does not apply). Use it as one input, not a diagnosis.

**3) Protein by body weight.** Scale protein to your mass in **kilograms** (lb ÷ 2.205), not to a flat gram target. (For people well above a healthy weight, calculate on a reasonable-/adjusted-body-weight basis or protein is overstated — see §02.) **If you know your body-fat %, dose protein on lean (fat-free) mass** instead of total weight — about **1.6–2.0 g per kg of lean mass** at maintenance and **2.0–2.4 g/kg of lean mass** in an active/fat-loss or muscle-building phase; this is what the calculator does when you enter body fat, and it avoids overstating protein in people carrying more fat. This is the same canonical math as §02:

- **0.8 g/kg/day** is the RDA — a floor to prevent deficiency in 97.5% of healthy adults, *not* an optimum ([NASEM/IOM, Dietary Reference Intakes (protein)](https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/10490)).
- **~1.2–1.6 g/kg/day** is the new *general-adult* target in the 2025–2030 US Dietary Guidelines (released January 2026) — well above the 0.8 floor, though the size of that jump (and its silence on protein *source*) is genuinely debated (see §02) ([DGA 2025–2030](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/)).
- **~1.0–1.2 g/kg/day** for healthy older adults (≥65), to defend against age-related muscle loss, paired with resistance exercise; **1.2–1.5** with acute/chronic illness, up to **2.0** in severe illness/malnutrition ([Bauer et al. 2013, PROT-AGE, *JAMDA*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23867520/); see §08).
- **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day** for athletes/building muscle (hypertrophy gains plateau near **~1.6** g/kg — little extra muscle above that); **1.6–2.4 g/kg/day** in an active fat-loss phase, since higher protein preserves lean mass in a deficit — the leaner and steeper the cut, the higher within that band ([Jäger et al. 2017, ISSN position stand, *JISSN*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/); [Morton et al. 2018, *BJSM*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/); see §02/§10).
- **Pregnancy → 1.1 g/kg/day; lactation → 1.3 g/kg/day** (IOM DRI; see §08).
- **Reduced in non-dialysis CKD** — a clinician-supervised exception where the "more protein" advice *inverts*. Per KDIGO 2024, stages G3–G5 not on dialysis target **~0.8 g/kg/day and avoid >1.3**; the stricter KDOQI framing is **0.55–0.60 g/kg/day without diabetes, 0.6–0.8 with diabetes**; **on dialysis the target rises back to 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day**. Do not self-prescribe — renal-dietitian territory (cross-reference §09).

**Adjust it — your daily and per-meal protein.** *Baseline (general adult):* **1.2–1.6 g/kg/day**, spread across **3–4 meals at ~0.4 g/kg each (≈25–30 g)** because muscle protein synthesis is maximized per-bolus, not per-day ([Schoenfeld & Aragon, ISSN review 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5828430/); see §02). *Depends on, with deltas:* **age ≥65 → push the per-meal dose to ~0.4–0.55 g/kg (≈30–40 g)** to clear the ~3 g leucine threshold of anabolic resistance; **plant-only → add ~25% more total** and combine complementary sources (a single plant protein clears the threshold at a larger dose); **fat-loss phase → +0.4–0.8 g/kg over the general target (→ 1.6–2.4)**; **non-dialysis CKD → cap at the clinician-set 0.55–0.8 g/kg** — this delta is negative and overrides all the others.

*Worked example* — our 68 kg woman: floor ≈ 0.8·68 = **54 g/day**; general target ≈ 1.2–1.6·68 = **~82–109 g/day**, eaten as ~4 meals of ~25–28 g (0.4·68 ≈ 27 g/meal). In a fat-loss phase she'd raise to ~1.6–2.0·68 = **~109–136 g/day**.

**4) Sex differences (brief).** Same core diet, a few dials set by physiology:

| Dial | Women (reproductive age) | Men |
|---|---|---|
| Iron (RDA) | **18 mg/day** (menstrual losses) | **8 mg/day** ([NIH ODS, Iron](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/)) |
| Calcium (RDA) | 1,000 mg (19–50); **1,200 mg at 51+** (post-menopausal bone loss); **1,300 mg ages 9–18 & pregnant/lactating teens** | 1,000 mg (19–70); 1,200 mg at 71+; **1,300 mg ages 9–18** ([NIH ODS, Calcium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/)) |
| Alcohol (if you drink) | No amount is risk-free (§07); **if you drink**, stay within **≤1 drink/day** — a limit, not a target | If you drink, stay within ≤2 drinks/day — a limit, not a target ([USDA/HHS, DGA 2020–2025, alcohol](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/alcohol/info)) |
| Calorie needs | Lower on average at the same height/age (Mifflin term: −161 vs +5) | Higher on average |

Pregnancy and menopause shift these again — pregnancy raises iron, folate, iodine, choline, and DHA needs (see the matrix below and §08), and menopause raises calcium and vitamin-D priority while lowering calorie needs as lean mass declines ([NIH ODS, Iron](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/); [USDA/HHS, Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/)).

| **Omega-3 (ALA)** | AI **1.6 g M / 1.1 g F** | walnuts, chia, flax, canola oil | Pregnancy 1.4 / lactation 1.3 g; conversion to EPA/DHA is inefficient — plant-based eaters should also take algal EPA+DHA |

### The master "it depends" matrix

This is the single most-requested thing about "the healthiest diet": *what changes for whom.* Every row keeps the same core — it only turns specific dials.

| If you are… | Turn these dials | Watch / supplement | Section |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A healthy general adult** | The core as-is; produce ≥400 g/day (floor) toward ~800 g optimum, fiber **~14 g per 1,000 kcal** (≈25 g women / 38 g men), mostly unsaturated fat | Vitamin D if low sun | 01–07 |
| **Pregnant or planning** | +Folate **before** conception (400+ mcg folic acid), +iron (27 mg), +iodine (220 mcg), +choline, **≥**200 mg DHA/day (commonly 200–300 mg); small calorie increase | Folic acid, iron, algal DHA, iodine; avoid alcohol, high-mercury fish, raw/unpasteurized foods | 08, 12 |
| **An infant / young child** | ~6 mo exclusive breastfeeding, then introduce common allergens **early** (incl. peanut); nutrient-dense foods; lower added-sugar tolerance | Vitamin D; iron-rich first foods | 08 |
| **An older adult (65+)** | ↑Protein to **1.0–1.2 g/kg/day** (1.2–1.5 with chronic illness), ~0.4 g/kg (≈30–40 g) per meal; pair with resistance exercise — *unless* non-dialysis CKD, where it drops to 0.55–0.8 (§09) | B12 (absorption falls), vitamin D, hydration | 02, 08, 12 |
| **An athlete / building muscle** | ↑Protein to **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day** (gains plateau ~1.6; 1.6–2.4 when dieting to keep muscle); carbs **periodized to training** (up to 6–10 g/kg in heavy load) | Creatine (optional, well-evidenced); fueling/timing | 02, 08, 11 |
| **Managing type 2 diabetes / insulin resistance** | Carb **quality first** (≥14 g fiber per 1,000 kcal, intact grains, low glycemic load); individualize carb **quantity** (lower-carb <130 g/day buys ~0.5% extra HbA1c at 6 mo, fading by 12); **weight loss ≥10–15 kg can drive remission** (best <6 yr duration, not on insulin) | Coordinate meds (hypo risk on insulin/sulfonylureas); clinician-guided | 06, 09 |
| **Managing high blood pressure** | **DASH** pattern; sodium <2,300 mg, toward **<1,500 mg** when hypertensive/salt-sensitive/Black/older (largest BP drop, ~1 mmHg per ~250–300 mg cut at high baseline); potassium **3,500–5,000 mg/day from food**; consider 75/25 NaCl/KCl salt substitute (unless contraindicated) | Salt substitute is contraindicated in advanced CKD (eGFR <45) **and** for anyone on potassium-raising meds (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics) — check with a clinician | 05, 06, 09 |
| **Managing high cholesterol / heart disease** | Replace **saturated/trans fat with unsaturated**; ↑soluble fiber, nuts, fish; Mediterranean pattern | Plant sterols optional; statin coordination | 02, 06, 09 |
| **Living with chronic kidney disease** | Protein **inverts downward**: G3–G5 non-dialysis ~**0.8 g/kg/day** (avoid >1.3), stricter 0.55–0.8 by KDOQI; **rises back to 1.0–1.2 on dialysis**. Sodium <2.0–2.3 g/day; potassium/phosphorus **individualized to labs** (no reflexive K restriction without persistent hyperkalemia); avoid salt substitutes if advanced | **Must** be dietitian/nephrology-guided | 09 |
| **Living with IBS / GERD** | Trial **low-FODMAP** (then reintroduce); identify trigger foods | Dietitian-guided to avoid over-restriction | 09 |
| **Living with gout / high urate** | Limit purine-rich meat/organ/shellfish, beer & spirits, and high-fructose drinks; lose weight if overweight; coffee, low-fat dairy, and cherries may help (urate target **<6 mg/dL** is mainly drug-driven — diet is adjunctive) | Don't use vitamin-C supplements as urate therapy | 09 |
| **Living with fatty liver (NAFLD/MASLD)** | Stepwise weight loss — **≥5%** cuts liver fat, **≥7%** resolves steatohepatitis, **≥10%** can regress fibrosis; hypocaloric **Mediterranean** pattern; cut added sugar/fructose & refined carbs; minimal alcohol | — | 09 |
| **Living with osteoporosis** | Calcium **1,200 mg** (women 51+, men 71+) and **vitamin D 800–1,000 IU** (food first); adequate protein; weight-bearing/resistance exercise | Vitamin D | 09 |
| **Living with heart failure** | Avoid **excessive** sodium (~**2,000–3,000 mg**, *not* necessarily <1,500 — SODIUM-HF showed no event benefit from strict limits); fluid limits & daily weights per clinician ⚕ | Clinician-set sodium/fluid ⚕ | 09 |
| **Plant-based (vegan/vegetarian)** | Complementary plant proteins; modestly more total protein; iron/zinc/calcium attention | **B12 (essential)**, vitamin D, algal DHA/EPA, iodine | 02, 03, 12 |
| **Lactose intolerant** | Usually **dose-dependent** — small amounts, fermented dairy, hard cheese, or lactose-free; not zero calcium | Calcium from other sources | 03, 08 |
| **Celiac / IgE food allergy** | **Absolute, lifelong exclusion** of the trigger | Replace lost fiber, B-vitamins, iron deliberately | 08 |
| **On a tight budget** | Cheap staples: dried/canned legumes, oats, eggs, frozen/canned veg & fruit, peanut butter, seasonal produce | Batch-cook; reduce waste | 13 |
| **Optimizing for the planet** | Cut **beef & lamb** first → legumes, poultry, fish ([Harvard T.H. Chan, The Nutrition Source](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/elevate-your-plate/a-little-less-red-meat/)); "eat lower on the food chain" beats "eat local" | — | 13 |
| **Trying to lose weight** | Calorie deficit of **−500 to −750 kcal/day** (≈0.5–1%/week) via **filling, high-quality** foods; protein to **1.6–2.4 g/kg/day** to preserve muscle; cut ultra-processed foods & SSBs; floor ~1,200 (women)/1,500 (men) kcal/day | Avoid extreme restriction; mind disordered-eating risk | 10, 11 |
| **On a GLP-1 / weight-loss medication (semaglutide, tirzepatide)** | Keep protein at the **upper end, ~1.6–2.4 g/kg/day**, + resistance training **≥2×/week** throughout treatment (≈25–40% of GLP-1 weight loss can be lean mass); against suppressed appetite, deliberately prioritize protein, fiber, fluid and micronutrients; **dose titration is clinician-set — don't self-adjust** | Protein, fiber, fluid, micronutrients hard to hit on low appetite; weight tends to return if the drug stops | 10, 12 |

> **The throughline:** every row keeps the same plant-forward, minimally-processed foundation. Personalization is tuning, not rebuilding. If a "personalized plan" tells you to abandon the core, be skeptical.

### Sample days (each ~plant-forward, minimally processed)

**General healthy adult (~2,000 kcal)**
- *Breakfast:* oats with berries, walnuts, and plain yogurt; coffee.
- *Lunch:* big salad with chickpeas, mixed veg, olive-oil vinaigrette; whole-grain bread.
- *Snack:* apple + a handful of almonds.
- *Dinner:* baked salmon, quinoa, roasted broccoli and peppers; olive oil.
- *Drinks:* water and tea through the day.

**Older adult (protein-forward, ~30–40 g protein/meal; ~0.4 g/kg)**
- *Breakfast:* Greek yogurt + eggs + fruit; whole-grain toast.
- *Lunch:* lentil-and-vegetable soup with a side of tuna or beans.
- *Dinner:* chicken or tofu stir-fry with brown rice and mixed vegetables.
- *Plus:* resistance exercise; B12 and vitamin D as needed.

**Athlete / muscle gain (higher protein + periodized carbs)**
- *Breakfast:* eggs, oats, banana, milk or soy milk.
- *Lunch:* chicken/tofu, rice, beans, vegetables.
- *Pre/post training:* carbs + ~25–30 g protein (e.g., yogurt + fruit, or a shake).
- *Dinner:* fish or lean meat, potatoes/whole grains, large vegetable portion.

**Type 2 diabetes (carb quality + portion)**
- *Breakfast:* veggie omelet + a small portion of intact whole grains or berries.
- *Lunch:* large salad with beans, olive oil, and chicken or fish.
- *Dinner:* tofu/fish, non-starchy vegetables, a measured portion of lentils or quinoa.
- *Approach:* prioritize fiber and protein, individualize carb amount with your clinician.

**Plant-based / vegan**
- *Breakfast:* fortified soy yogurt or oats with chia, fruit, and nuts.
- *Lunch:* lentil dal or chickpea bowl with whole grains and greens.
- *Dinner:* tofu/tempeh, mixed vegetables, brown rice; tahini or olive oil.
- *Daily:* B12 supplement; algal DHA; vitamin D and iodine as needed.

**Budget-friendly (~low cost, still healthy)**
- *Breakfast:* oats with peanut butter and frozen berries.

- *Staples:* dried beans/lentils, oats, eggs, frozen veg, canned fish, seasonal produce.

### A sample week

A template, not a prescription — built on the healthy plate, meant to be adapted to your culture, schedule, and palate. Pick themes, then swap freely within each (rotate the protein, the grain, the vegetable). Anchor *breakfast* on a few defaults you actually like; vary lunch and dinner more.

| Day | Breakfast | Lunch | Dinner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Oats + fruit + nuts | Grain bowl (beans, veg, olive oil) | Fish, whole grain, roasted veg |
| Tue | Yogurt + berries + seeds | Big salad + leftover protein | Stir-fry (tofu/chicken) + brown rice |
| Wed | Eggs + whole-grain toast + tomato | Lentil soup + bread | Bean chili + greens |
| Thu | Oats + peanut butter + banana | Wrap (hummus, veg, beans) | Sheet-pan chicken + vegetables |
| Fri | Smoothie (milk/soy, fruit, oats) | Leftover chili | Whole-grain pasta + veg + sardines |

*Snacks (any day):* fruit, a handful of nuts, plain yogurt, raw veg + hummus, or whole-grain crackers with cheese. Every dinner keeps the half-plate-vegetable rule; protein and a whole grain fill the rest. If a day goes off-script, the week still holds — aim for "usually."

### Your grocery list & pantry

A stocked kitchen is the cheapest adherence tool there is: when whole-food staples are on hand, a healthy meal is always the path of least resistance. Most of these keep for weeks or months, and frozen produce is nutritionally on par with fresh — often picked and flash-frozen at peak ripeness ([Harvard T.H. Chan, The Nutrition Source — Meal Prep](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/meal-prep/)). Lean on the cheap, high-leverage staples (cross-reference §13).

- **Produce (fresh + frozen):** frozen mixed vegetables and berries, plus slow-spoiling fresh — onions, carrots, cabbage, potatoes, citrus, apples; leafy greens for the week.
- **Whole grains:** oats, brown rice, whole-wheat pasta/bread, quinoa, barley.
- **Legumes/canned:** dried and canned beans, lentils, chickpeas; canned tomatoes; canned fish (sardines, tuna, salmon).
- **Proteins:** eggs, plain yogurt, tofu/tempeh, and your chosen fish/poultry.
- **Healthy fats:** extra-virgin olive oil, nuts, seeds, peanut/nut butter, tahini.
- **Flavor (so it isn't bland):** garlic, onions, dried/fresh herbs and spices, vinegar, lemon, mustard, low-sodium stock.

Buy a few base staples first (rice, oats, beans), then build meals around what's seasonal and on sale to cut cost and waste.

### Batch cooking & meal prep

Meal prep is one of the most reliable levers for adherence: when a healthy option is already made, you skip the decision that usually ends in takeout ([Harvard T.H. Chan, The Nutrition Source — Meal Prep](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/meal-prep/)). You don't need to plate seven identical containers — that path leads to boredom and waste.

- **Cook once, eat twice.** Deliberately make extra dinner; tomorrow's lunch is done.
- **Prep components, not full meals.** A pot of grains, a tray of roasted vegetables, a batch of beans, a protein, and a sauce mix and match into a week of different plates.
- **Wash and chop on arrival.** Five minutes of prep when groceries land makes the rest of the week frictionless.
- **Freeze in portions.** Soups, stews, chili, and cooked beans freeze well for 2–3 months; label and date them so the freezer becomes a personal ready-meal aisle ([Harvard T.H. Chan, The Nutrition Source — Meal Prep](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/meal-prep/)).

Start small — one batch-cook session a week beats an ambitious system you abandon by Wednesday.

### Eating out, travel & social meals

A healthy diet has to survive restaurants, airports, and dinner with friends — restriction that can't is just a slower failure. Use heuristics, not anxiety:

- **Build the plate the usual way.** Aim for half vegetables, a protein, and a whole grain or starch; order an extra side of veg or a salad to anchor it.
- **Watch the liquid calories.** Sugary drinks and alcohol are where restaurant meals quietly inflate — default to water, coffee, or tea, and keep alcohol within limits (§05).
- **Mind the sauces and refined sides.** Ask for dressing or rich sauces on the side; swap fries for a vegetable or salad when it's easy.
- **Use the 80/20 mindset.** What you do most of the time is what counts. One celebratory meal doesn't undo a good week, and treating it as a "cheat" you must "earn back" feeds disordered patterns, not health (§11).

Travel days: pack fruit and nuts, choose the protein-plus-plants option, and don't let one off-routine day become the excuse to abandon the routine.

### How to read a food label

A label takes ten seconds to scan and tells you most of what matters. On the US Nutrition Facts panel, check, in order ([FDA, How to Understand and Use the Nutrition Facts Label](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/how-understand-and-use-nutrition-facts-label)):

1. **Serving size first.** Every number on the panel is *per serving*, and a package often holds several — a "150-calorie" snack can be three servings. Scale the rest to what you'll actually eat.
2. **The %DV rule of thumb.** As a quick guide, **5% DV or less is low** and **20% DV or more is high** for any nutrient ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/how-understand-and-use-nutrition-facts-label)).
3. **Get less of:** added sugars (Daily Value **50 g/day**), sodium (DV **2,300 mg/day**), and saturated fat (DV **20 g/day**) ([FDA, Added Sugars](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/added-sugars-nutrition-facts-label)). Added sugars are now listed separately — distinct from naturally occurring sugars in fruit or dairy. **Adjust it —** these DVs are anchored to a **2,000-kcal** reference and a "<10% of calories" cap, so scale them to *your* energy budget: added sugars ≈ **25 g per 1,000 kcal** (so ~37 g on 1,500 kcal, ~62 g on 2,500 kcal); saturated fat the same **~11 g per 1,000 kcal**, dropping to **~7 g per 1,000 kcal (<6% of calories)** if your LDL is elevated (§09); sodium stays a flat **<2,300 mg/day**, tightening to **<1,500 mg** if hypertensive/salt-sensitive/Black/diabetic/older (§05).
4. **Get more of:** dietary fiber, potassium, calcium, vitamin D, and iron.
5. **Scan the ingredient list.** It's ordered by weight, so the first few items dominate. A long list of additives, refined oils, and unfamiliar chemical names flags an ultra-processed product (§05); watch for added sugar hiding under many aliases (syrup, dextrose, maltose, fruit-juice concentrate). Front-of-pack claims ("natural," "made with whole grain") are marketing — the Nutrition Facts panel and ingredient list are the truth.

The strongest signal isn't any single number — it's whether the food is a whole or minimally processed one in the first place.

### A low-effort 30-day starter (swap, don't overhaul)

You don't need a perfect diet — you need a better one you'll keep. Diet quality is one of the largest preventable drivers of death worldwide: poor diet was linked to roughly **11 million deaths in 2017**, with too little whole grains and fruit and too much sodium leading the list ([GBD 2017 (Afshin et al. 2019), *The Lancet*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6899507/)). High-leverage swaps:

1. **Refined grains → whole grains** (white bread/rice → whole-grain, brown rice, oats). Each 90 g/day of whole grains is tied to ~22% lower cardiovascular disease risk and ~17% lower all-cause mortality ([Aune et al. 2016, *BMJ*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4908315/)).
2. **Sugar-sweetened drinks → water/coffee/tea.** Possibly the single biggest easy win — regular sugary-drink intake raises risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease ([Harvard T.H. Chan, The Nutrition Source](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/healthy-drinks/sugary-drinks/)).
3. **Add a vegetable or fruit to every meal** until you reach ~5/day — at least 400 g of fruit and vegetables daily is the WHO target ([WHO 2020, Healthy diet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet)).
4. **Butter and processed meat → olive oil, nuts, fish, legumes.**
5. **A daily handful of nuts and a serving of legumes.** Higher nut intake is associated with ~19–25% lower cardiovascular mortality ([Kim et al. 2019, *Int J Environ Res Public Health*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31817639/)), and each 50 g/day of legumes with ~6% lower all-cause mortality ([Zargarzadeh et al. 2023, *Advances in Nutrition*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10103007/)).
6. **Cook one extra meal at home each week** from whole ingredients; batch-cook.
7. **Read one label a day** — steer away from items with lots of added sugar, sodium, and a long ultra-processed ingredient list.

Consistency for years beats perfection for weeks. The core is forgiving; aim for "usually," not "always."
