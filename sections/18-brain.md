## 18. Diet and the Brain: Mental Health and Cognition

**Bottom line:** The same Mediterranean-style pattern that wins everywhere helps mood as a real *adjunct* to professional care (not a treatment), while the brain-protection claim is promising-but-unproven — so eat the ordinary good diet, no special 'brain diet' needed.


The brain is a hungry, fatty, glucose-thirsty organ, and the idea that what you eat changes how you feel and think has obvious appeal. The evidence is real, but it is also smaller and more uneven than the wellness internet suggests. For **mental health** — specifically depression — there is now genuine randomized-trial evidence that improving diet quality can help, on top of usual care. For **cognition and dementia**, the observational signals are strong and consistent, yet the one well-powered randomized trial of a "brain diet" came up empty. Holding both of those facts at once is the honest position.

A safety caveat belongs at the very top, not the bottom. Diet is a **supportive, adjunct factor** — never a treatment for clinical depression, anxiety, or dementia. The depression trials below were run *alongside* the medications and therapy people were already receiving; not instead of them. If you or someone you know is struggling, the right move is professional care — a doctor, therapist, or crisis line — and eating better is something you can do *in addition*, not a reason to delay or replace treatment. The mechanisms that plausibly link diet to mood — inflammation, the gut–brain axis (§15), vascular health, and blood-glucose stability — are the same ones that make a generally healthy diet worth eating regardless of its effect on your brain.

The reassuring throughline: the pattern that shows up in the mental-health and cognition literature is the *same* Mediterranean-style, minimally-processed, plant-forward pattern that wins everywhere else in this guide (§06). You do not need a special "brain diet." You need the ordinary good one.

### Key evidence

**Diet can improve depression — the SMILES trial (moderate; first of its kind).** SMILES (Jacka et al., 2017) was the first randomized controlled trial to test diet as an adjunctive treatment for **moderate-to-severe major depression**. Sixty-seven adults, most already on medication or in therapy, were randomized to seven sessions of dietetic support toward a modified Mediterranean diet, or to a matched "befriending" social-support control. At 12 weeks the diet group improved by about **7.1 more points on the MADRS** depression scale (Cohen's *d* = −1.16, a large effect), and reached **remission at 32.3% versus 8.0%** in controls — a number-needed-to-treat of roughly 4 [Jacka et al., BMC Medicine 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5282719/) (government-funded by the NHMRC, with in-kind industry support including Meat and Livestock Australia, which funded the biochemistry analyses). The result is striking but must be read with caution: it is a single small trial (only 56 completers), it could not blind participants to whether they were getting cooking advice or chit-chat, and improvements tracked with how much diet quality actually changed.

  **Adjust it — the realistic, personalized expectation is *not* "minus 7 MADRS points for everyone."** That 7.1-point gap and NNT of ~4 came from people with **moderate-to-severe depression eating a genuinely poor baseline diet**, almost all already on medication or therapy, who then made a large diet change over 12 weeks. How it scales: the benefit is roughly **proportional to how much your diet quality actually improves** — SMILES saw mood gains track with diet-score change, so someone already eating a near-Mediterranean pattern has little headroom and should expect little additional mood effect. The sober cross-trial estimate is much smaller: pooling longer (≥3-month) trials gives only a **small** effect (e.g., SMD ≈ −0.23 for calorie restriction, low certainty; see below). What it depends on, with numbers: **poor baseline diet + clinical depression →** plan a **~12-week** structured change and judge by your own symptom scale, expecting a modest add-on lift; **already Mediterranean-ish →** treat diet as maintenance, not a mood lever; **mild/subclinical low mood →** evidence is thinner and the expected effect smaller still; **any case →** this is an *adjunct* to professional care, not a substitute (see safety caveat above).

**Supporting trials and meta-analyses (moderate).** HELFIMED (Parletta et al., 2019) randomized 152 adults with self-reported depression to Mediterranean-diet hampers, cooking workshops, and fish oil versus social groups, and found greater improvement in mental health and quality of life in the diet arm [Parletta et al., Nutritional Neuroscience 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29215971/). Pooling the trials, a meta-analysis of 16 RCTs concluded that dietary improvement significantly reduced depressive symptoms — though with no measurable effect on anxiety [Firth et al., Psychosomatic Medicine 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30720698/). The most rigorous recent synthesis is more sober: a 2025 review of 25 longer (≥3-month) trials found that calorie restriction and low-fat diets produced only **small** reductions in depressive symptoms (e.g., SMD −0.23 for calorie restriction) at **low certainty**, with too little evidence to judge anxiety [Abukmail et al., Annals of Internal Medicine 2025](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/ANNALS-24-03016). The fair summary: a real but modest adjunct effect, strongest in people whose diets start poor.

**Observational links: Western/ultra-processed diets track with more depression (moderate, observational).** Across prospective cohorts, higher diet quality predicts lower depression risk. Lassale et al.'s meta-analysis found the strongest signal for the Mediterranean diet — about a **33% lower risk** of depression for the highest vs. lowest adherence (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.55–0.82) — and a parallel benefit for less-inflammatory diets (RR 0.76, 0.63–0.92) [Lassale et al., Molecular Psychiatry 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-018-0237-8). At the other end, in the Nurses' Health Study II (31,712 women), the highest ultra-processed-food intake was associated with a **49% higher risk** of depression (HR 1.49, 1.26–1.76, strict definition), with artificially sweetened beverages flagged most strongly (HR 1.37) [Samuthpongtorn et al., JAMA Network Open 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10512104/). These are correlations — reverse causation (low mood drives comfort-food eating) and shared lifestyle factors are very plausible — so they raise, but cannot prove, causation.

  **Adjust it — these are *between-groups* contrasts, so your personal "dose" is how far you move along the pattern.** The 33% lower depression risk (RR 0.67) is **highest vs. lowest** Mediterranean adherence, and the 49% higher risk (HR 1.49) is the **top vs. bottom** band of ultra-processed intake (roughly **>8.8 vs. <4 UPF servings/day** in that cohort) — not what one extra salad or one fewer soda does. How it scales: the realistic lever for most people is **directional, in steps** — each move toward more vegetables/legumes/whole grains/olive oil and away from ultra-processed foods and sugary *or* artificially-sweetened drinks (the latter flagged at HR 1.37) shifts you along that gradient; the benefit per step is small and unproven causally, so don't expect a 33% personal drop. What it depends on, with numbers: **currently near the bottom (mostly ultra-processed) →** the largest expected headroom, so target a few concrete swaps (e.g., cut sweetened/diet drinks toward 0/day, add legumes 3–4×/week); **already near the top →** little room to move and little expected change; **outcome →** these figures are for **depression**, not anxiety (where the evidence is thinner) or dementia (different literature, below).

**Plausible mechanisms (limited but biologically coherent).** Four threads recur: chronic low-grade **inflammation** (pro-inflammatory diets associate with both depression and elevated CRP/IL-6); the **gut–brain axis**, where fiber-fed microbes produce short-chain fatty acids and influence neuro-immune signaling (§15); **vascular health**, since the same diet that protects arteries protects the brain's small vessels; and **blood-glucose stability**, since the swings driven by refined carbohydrate can affect energy, irritability, and concentration. None of these is a proven causal pathway in humans, but together they make the diet–mood link mechanistically credible.

**Cognition and dementia — strong observational signal, null trial (contested).** Cohort studies are remarkably consistent: across prospective studies (>34,000 participants), high Mediterranean-diet adherence is associated with roughly **20% lower risk** of cognitive disorders (pooled RR ~0.79, 0.70–0.90) and slower decline [Wu & Sun, Scientific Reports 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep41317), and the MIND diet's original cohorts suggested dramatic protection. But the decisive randomized test failed: a rigorous 3-year RCT of the MIND diet found **no cognitive benefit** versus a control diet (§06) [Barnes et al., NEJM 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10513737/). This is the field's cautionary tale — the observational story may reflect confounding, or three years may be too short to move a decades-long process. Honest verdict: **promising, not proven**.

  **Adjust it — there is no number to "dose" here for brain protection, so set expectations accordingly.** The 20% lower risk (RR 0.79) is an **observational, highest-vs-lowest** contrast that a 3-year randomized trial could **not** reproduce (global-cognition difference just **+0.035 units, p = 0.23**, with both arms losing ~5 kg). What that means for you, with numbers: do **not** adopt a Mediterranean/MIND pattern *for the purpose of* preventing dementia expecting a measurable cognitive payoff — the trial evidence for that specific goal is currently null. The defensible reason to eat this way is its **proven cardiometabolic and vascular benefit** (§06, §09), which protects the brain's small vessels as a by-product; the time horizon for any cognitive effect is **decades, not the weeks-to-months** over which mood can shift, so judge it on heart/metabolic markers you can actually track, not on a felt cognitive change.

**Omega-3 and B-vitamins/homocysteine (mixed).** For depression, EPA-predominant omega-3 shows a modest benefit, often as an **add-on** to antidepressants rather than a standalone fix. The meta-analytic signal points to formulations that are **≥60% EPA at ~720–1,000 mg EPA/day**, with no added benefit above ~1 g/day; pure-EPA augmentation of antidepressants gave a small-to-moderate effect (SMD ≈ −0.44), while DHA-predominant formulations showed essentially none [Liao et al., Translational Psychiatry 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6683166/). The evidence is heterogeneous, though — the pooled signal is driven by EPA-predominant formulations and by trials where it was added to antidepressants, and several well-powered RCTs were null — so don't overstate it. For cognition, B-vitamin/homocysteine-lowering is genuinely mixed: the VITACOG trial slowed brain atrophy and cognitive decline **only in the subgroup with high baseline homocysteine**, while a Cochrane review of older adults with mild cognitive impairment found **little or no cognitive benefit** from B vitamins [Cochrane, McCleery et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30383288/). Supplements are not a substitute for the underlying dietary pattern.

  **Adjust it — omega-3 for low mood is an *adjunct*, dialed to EPA content, not bottle size.** Baseline: ~**1 g/day of EPA** specifically (not total omega-3, not DHA), from a supplement that is **≥60% EPA**, on top of (never instead of) clinician-directed care. How it scales: benefit appears across roughly **720–1,000 mg EPA/day** and does **not** increase beyond ~1 g/day — going to 2–4 g buys side-effects (reflux, loose stools, a small bleeding-risk bump), not mood. Read the label: a "1,000 mg fish-oil softgel" typically delivers only ~**300 mg EPA + 200 mg DHA**, so hitting 1 g EPA often means **2–3 such softgels** or a concentrated EPA product. What it depends on, with numbers: **predominant depression with low diet quality →** worth a 6–8-week trial at ~1 g EPA/day as an add-on; **no depression, general brain "insurance" →** skip the pill and get omega-3 from **2 servings/week of oily fish** (≈250–500 mg EPA+DHA/day equivalent, the §06 target) instead; **on anticoagulants/antiplatelets or pre-surgery →** doses ≥3 g/day raise bleeding risk, so stay ≤1 g and clear it with your prescriber; **pregnancy →** prioritize DHA (≈200–300 mg/day) for fetal needs over EPA-for-mood, and choose low-mercury sources. If 8 weeks at ~1 g EPA/day changes nothing, stop — it is not working for you. (For B-vitamins, the "adjustable" lever is a *test*, not a dose: only worth considering if a clinician documents **elevated homocysteine** — see §05/§09.)

**Caffeine, alcohol, and blood sugar.** Caffeine reliably boosts short-term alertness; moderate coffee intake is modestly associated with *lower* depression risk in observational data, but that link is correlational — reverse causation (low mood reduces coffee intake) and confounding are plausible, and randomized effects on mood are mixed — and high doses can worsen anxiety and wreck sleep. EFSA judges intakes up to **~400 mg/day** of caffeine from all sources, and **single doses up to ~200 mg (~3 mg/kg)**, safe for healthy non-pregnant adults [EFSA caffeine opinion 2015](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102). **Alcohol** is a central-nervous-system depressant that disrupts sleep architecture (notably REM) and is bidirectionally tied to depression and anxiety — it tends to worsen the very moods people drink to relieve [MedlinePlus: Alcohol](https://medlineplus.gov/alcohol.html); §07. And large **blood-sugar swings** from refined carbohydrate can drive transient irritability, fatigue, and poor concentration, which is one practical reason the steadier, fiber-rich Mediterranean pattern feels better day to day.

  **Adjust it — caffeine: a per-body, per-clock ceiling, not a flat "4 cups."** Baseline: **≤400 mg/day** total and **≤200 mg in any single dose** for a healthy ~70-kg adult. How it scales: the safe single dose is roughly **3 mg/kg**, so ~200 mg at 70 kg, ~150 mg at 50 kg, ~270 mg at 90 kg; one brewed 240 mL (8 oz) coffee is ~**95 mg**, an espresso shot ~**65 mg**, black tea ~**40–50 mg**, a 250 mL energy drink ~**80 mg**, so "4 cups" is a rough stand-in for the 400 mg cap, not the cap itself. **Timing for alertness:** caffeine's half-life is ~**5 hours** (range 3–7), so a 200 mg afternoon dose still has ~100 mg active 5 hours later; even **~100 mg near bedtime can lengthen the time to fall asleep and shorten sleep** in sensitive people, so set a personal cutoff ~**8 hours before bed** (e.g., last dose by ~2–3 pm for an 11 pm bedtime). What it depends on, with numbers: **pregnant or lactating → ≤200 mg/day**; **anxiety, panic, or palpitations →** cut toward ~**100–200 mg/day** and watch the single dose; **poor sleep →** push the cutoff to ≥10 hours pre-bed or drop the afternoon dose entirely; **slow metabolizer / caffeine-sensitive (CYP1A2) →** treat ~3 mg/kg as a ceiling you rarely approach; **adolescents and children →** ~3 mg/kg/day is the safe limit (far below 400 mg).

  **Adjust it — alcohol: there is no "mood-helpful" dose; the only question is how much harm.** Baseline for brain/mood purposes: **less is better, and zero is a defensible target**, since alcohol is a depressant that fragments sleep. How it scales: even **1–2 standard drinks** (a U.S. standard drink = **14 g pure alcohol** ≈ 355 mL/12 oz beer at 5%, 148 mL/5 oz wine at 12%, or 44 mL/1.5 oz spirits at 40%) within ~3 hours of bed measurably suppresses REM and worsens second-half-of-night sleep, which feeds next-day low mood. What it depends on, with numbers: **if you drink, stay at or below the long-standing moderate-use ceiling — ≤2 drinks/day for men, ≤1/day for women** (the 2020–2025 U.S. limits; the 2025–2030 update drops the explicit numbers for a blunter "drink less for better health," which only reinforces a lower target — see §07), and don't "save up" doses; **active depression/anxiety or any sleep complaint → aim for 0**, since it reliably worsens the moods people drink to relieve; **on antidepressants, sedatives, or sleep aids → 0** (additive CNS depression); **pregnancy → 0**. Drinking is never an evidence-based mood treatment — if you are using it to manage feelings, that is a reason to talk to a clinician (see safety caveat above).

### What it depends on

- **Your starting diet.** The mood benefit is largest when you begin with a poor, ultra-processed diet — there is more room to improve.
- **Whether it is adjunct or substitute.** Diet helps *alongside* therapy and medication. It is not a replacement, and treating it as one is the real risk.
- **Outcome.** Depression has supportive trial evidence; cognition/dementia does not (yet). Anxiety evidence is thinner still.
- **Time horizon.** Mood can shift in weeks; dementia prevention plays out over decades, where trials are hardest to run.
- **Individual factors.** Baseline homocysteine (for B-vitamins), caffeine sensitivity and sleep, and any alcohol use all shift the picture.

### Practical takeaways

- **Eat the Mediterranean-style pattern (§06)** — vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts, olive oil, fish — for the best-supported mood and brain payoff. No special "brain diet" is needed.
- **Cut ultra-processed foods and sugary/diet drinks**, the foods most consistently linked to higher depression risk.
- **Eat fish or take EPA-predominant omega-3** as a reasonable adjunct if you have low mood — discuss with your clinician, not in place of one. *Adjust:* baseline **2 servings oily fish/week** (≈250–500 mg EPA+DHA/day) for general intake; for a *mood* trial, **~1 g EPA/day from a ≥60% EPA supplement** for 6–8 weeks as an add-on (≤1 g, no extra benefit above; ≤1 g and clinician-cleared if on blood thinners).
- **Keep blood sugar steady** with fiber, protein, and minimally-processed carbs to smooth out energy and mood. *Adjust:* aim for the §06/§02 fiber and protein targets (≈**14 g fiber/1,000 kcal**; protein per §02/§20) and pair carbs with protein/fat rather than eating them alone.
- **Mind caffeine and alcohol:** caffeine for daytime alertness only; treat alcohol as a depressant and sleep-disrupter, not a mood aid (§07). *Adjust caffeine:* **≤400 mg/day total, ≤200 mg (~3 mg/kg) per dose**, last dose **~8 h before bed**; **≤200 mg/day in pregnancy**, lower (~100–200 mg) if anxious or caffeine-sensitive. *Adjust alcohol:* less is better, **0 with active depression/anxiety, sleep problems, CNS-active meds, or pregnancy**; if you drink, stay within **≤2 drinks/day (men) / ≤1 (women)** and not near bedtime.
- **Get professional help for clinical symptoms.** Diet is the supportive layer, never the treatment, for depression, anxiety, or dementia (§05, §09).

### Unsettled or debated

- **Is the diet–depression effect causal, and how big?** SMILES and HELFIMED say a real adjunct effect exists; the 2025 Annals review says it is small and low-certainty. Both can be true.
- **Does any diet protect the aging brain?** Strong, consistent cohort data versus a null MIND RCT (§06). The cognitive-protection claim is currently promising but unproven.
- **B-vitamins for cognition:** a benefit may exist only in people with elevated homocysteine, but population-wide supplementation is not supported.
