## 03. Micronutrients: Vitamins, Minerals, and Common Deficiencies

**Bottom line:** A handful of nutrients are reliably under-eaten (vitamin D, calcium, potassium, fiber; plus iron, B12, iodine, folate) — get them from food where you can, and reserve supplements for documented deficiency or defined high-risk groups, because high-dose single nutrients can backfire.


Macronutrients supply your energy; micronutrients make it usable. Vitamins and minerals are needed in milligram or microgram amounts — a day's worth of vitamin B12 weighs about as much as a few grains of table salt — yet they sit at the center of nearly every physiological process, from oxygen transport to DNA synthesis to nerve signaling. Chronic shortfalls drive a large share of the global disease burden, and even in wealthy countries a handful of nutrients are reliably under-eaten.

Two facts organize everything below. First, the US Dietary Guidelines single out four **"dietary components of public health concern"** — under-consumed to a degree linked to chronic disease — vitamin D, calcium, potassium, and dietary fiber, with iron added for pregnant women and infants [DGA 2020-2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/resources/2020-2025-dietary-guidelines-online-materials/food-sources-select-nutrients). Awareness is strikingly low: in one large shopper survey, fewer than 30% of people considered any of them, and potassium registered with just 14.5% [Gustafson & Rose, Appetite 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36460120/). Globally, the dominant deficiencies are iron, vitamin A, iodine, vitamin D, B12, folate, and zinc.

Second — the through-line of the whole guide — **nutrients from whole-food patterns consistently outperform isolated high-dose supplements.** Several large trials of single nutrients failed to reproduce the benefits seen in food, and a few caused net harm — beta-carotene supplements raised lung cancer incidence and mortality in smokers and asbestos-exposed workers [Omenn et al., NEJM 1996 (CARET)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8602180/). Requirements are also deeply context-dependent: life stage, sex, pregnancy, sun exposure, skin pigmentation, diet pattern, malabsorption, and certain medications all shift both your risk and the case for a targeted supplement. I flag evidence strength as we go.

### Key evidence

**Vitamin D corrects deficiency reliably; it does not broadly prevent disease in healthy adults (strong).** The RDA is 600 IU (15 mcg)/day for ages 1-70 and 800 IU (20 mcg) above 70; the tolerable upper limit is 4,000 IU (100 mcg). Deficiency — serum 25(OH)D below 30 nmol/L (12 ng/mL) — causes rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults, and correcting it is unambiguously beneficial [NIH ODS 2024](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/). But the landmark VITAL trial (25,871 adults, 2,000 IU/day; NIH-funded, with vitamin D pills and placebos donated by Pharmavite) found **no** significant reduction in total cancer incidence, heart attacks, strokes, or cardiovascular death in already-replete people [Manson et al., NEJM 2019 (VITAL)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6425757/); [NIH ODS 2024](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/), nor any reduction in total, nonvertebral, or hip fractures [LeBoff et al., NEJM 2022](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMoa2202106). This is the cleanest example in the guide of bone-health logic failing to translate into RCT-proven disease prevention [Harvard T.H. Chan, The Nutrition Source](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/vitamin-d/). Because few foods are naturally rich (fatty fish, UV-exposed mushrooms, fortified milk) and skin makes the rest from sunlight, latitude, season, and pigmentation genuinely matter.

**Adjust it —** baseline **600 IU (15 mcg)/day** for ages 1-70, hard ceiling (UL) **4,000 IU (100 mcg)** for ages 9+ (2,500 IU/63 mcg for ages 1-3; 3,000 IU/75 mcg for ages 4-8). Scale to the dependency, not a fixed dose:
  - **Age >70 → 800 IU (20 mcg)** (the RDA itself rises here).
  - **Goal is correcting documented deficiency** (serum 25(OH)D <30 nmol/L / <12 ng/mL), not prevention: typical repletion is **1,000-2,000 IU/day** to push above the 50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL) sufficiency line; test rather than guess.
  - **Obesity (BMI ≥30) → roughly 1.5-2x** the dose of a normal-weight person for the same blood level (fat sequesters vitamin D).
  - **Dark skin or latitude >37° in winter, or near-zero sun → assume little-to-no cutaneous synthesis;** rely on the 600-800 IU food/supplement floor year-round.
  - **Malabsorption (celiac, Crohn's, bariatric surgery) → dose to a target serum level** under clinician guidance, often well above 800 IU. Do not exceed 4,000 IU/day chronically without monitoring.

**Iron is the world's most common nutrient deficiency (strong).** The RDA is 8 mg/day for men and postmenopausal women, 18 mg for menstruating women, and 27 mg in pregnancy; the upper limit is 45 mg [NIH ODS 2024](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/). Iron deficiency is the leading cause of anemia worldwide [WHO 2025](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/anaemia), and the global burden is enormous: an estimated 1.92 billion people (24.3% of the population) were anemic in 2021 [GBD 2021 Anaemia Collaborators, Lancet Haematology 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2352-3026(23)00160-6). The burden falls disproportionately on women and children — about 33.7% of women aged 15-49 and 39.8% of children 6-59 months are anemic, versus ~17.5% of men overall [GBD 2021 Anaemia Collaborators, Lancet Haematology 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2352-3026(23)00160-6); [WHO GHO](https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/themes/topics/anaemia_in_women_and_children). Heme iron (meat, seafood) is far better absorbed than non-heme (plant) iron, so vegetarians are advised to target roughly 1.8x the RDA; vitamin C boosts non-heme absorption while phytates and tea polyphenols blunt it [Harvard T.H. Chan, The Nutrition Source](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/iron/). Caution cuts both ways: deficiency impairs cognition and work capacity, but acute pediatric overdose (≥60 mg/kg) can be fatal, so keep supplements away from children.

**Adjust it —** baseline **8 mg/day** (men, postmenopausal women), ceiling (UL) **45 mg/day** for adults. The driver is blood loss and absorption:
  - **Menstruating women 19-50 → 18 mg** (≈2.25x the male baseline); **pregnancy → 27 mg**; **lactation → 9-10 mg** (back down, because menses pause).
  - **Vegetarian/vegan → multiply your target by 1.8** (heme iron is absent): a menstruating vegan effectively targets ≈**32 mg/day** of dietary non-heme iron (18 × 1.8). Pair with **vitamin C** (≈75-100 mg, e.g. half a bell pepper) at the meal to roughly double non-heme absorption; separate iron-rich meals from **tea/coffee** (polyphenols) and high-**phytate** foods by ≥1-2 hours.
  - **Heavy menstrual bleeding, frequent blood donation, or endurance training → individualize upward** via ferritin testing, not a flat number; treat confirmed deficiency, don't blanket-supplement.
  - **No routine supplement for adult men or postmenopausal women** absent documented deficiency — excess iron is pro-oxidant and there is no excretion route; hemochromatosis carriers should avoid supplements entirely.

**Calcium is essential, but the supplement-for-disease case is mixed (strong on need, contested on supplements).** The RDA runs 1,000-1,300 mg/day depending on age, highest for women 51+, everyone 70+, and adolescents; the upper limit is 2,000-2,500 mg [NIH ODS 2024](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/). Absorption is about 30% from dairy and fortified foods but only ~5% from high-oxalate greens like spinach, so leafy greens are a weaker source than they look. Fracture-prevention evidence from supplements is genuinely mixed, and one meta-analysis linked calcium *supplements* to a ~15% higher cardiovascular risk (composite of myocardial infarction or stroke, RR 1.15) [Bolland et al., BMJ 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21505219/); [NIH ODS 2024](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/) — an earlier 15-trial meta-analysis similarly found roughly a 27-31% higher myocardial-infarction risk from calcium supplements [Bolland et al., BMJ 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20671013/) — a finding many expert panels dispute, generally agreeing intake up to the upper limit is safe, and that food sources are preferable.

**Adjust it —** baseline **1,000 mg/day** for adults 19-50 and men 51-70; the number rises by life stage, and the UL *falls* with age:
  - **Women 51+ and everyone 71+ → 1,200 mg**; **adolescents 9-18 → 1,300 mg** (peak bone accrual).
  - **UL:** 2,500 mg for ages 19-50, dropping to **2,000 mg at 51+** — closer to the RDA than people assume, so stacking a 1,200 mg supplement onto a dairy-rich diet can overshoot.
  - **Source matters more than the headline number:** absorption is ~30% from dairy/fortified foods but only ~5% from high-oxalate greens (spinach), so 1 cup of cooked spinach delivers far less usable calcium than its label suggests; low-oxalate greens (kale, bok choy) and fortified soy/tofu are the efficient plant sources.
  - **Prefer food; cap supplements.** If you must supplement, keep single doses **≤500 mg** (absorption efficiency drops above that) and take with meals; the contested cardiovascular signal is tied to *supplements*, not dietary calcium.
  - **Dairy-free → plan ≈1,000-1,200 mg from fortified alternatives** (most fortified plant milks ≈300 mg/cup) plus tofu set with calcium and low-oxalate greens.

**Potassium and the sodium ratio move blood pressure (strong).** The Adequate Intake is 3,400 mg/day for men and 2,600 mg for women, with no upper limit for healthy people — and most Americans fall short (~3,016 mg men, ~2,320 mg women) [NIH ODS 2024](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Potassium-HealthProfessional/). The potassium-rich DASH pattern lowers blood pressure by about 5.5/3.0 mmHg, and higher intake is associated with roughly 21% lower stroke risk (RR 0.79 per 1.64 g/day higher intake, across 11 cohorts and 247,510 adults) [D'Elia et al., JACC 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21371638/). One critical caveat: these targets do **not** apply to people with impaired potassium excretion (chronic kidney disease, certain medications), for whom high potassium can be dangerous. Get it from potatoes, legumes, dried fruit, and greens — not pills.

**Adjust it —** baseline AI **3,400 mg/day (men) / 2,600 mg/day (women)**; no UL for healthy kidneys. The same foods that raise potassium displace sodium, so think of it as a *ratio* lever:
  - **Life stage:** pregnancy → **2,600-2,900 mg**; lactation → **2,500-2,800 mg**; teens 14-18 → **3,000 mg (M) / 2,300 mg (F)**.
  - **Blood-pressure goal:** moving toward the AI plus trimming sodium toward **<2,300 mg (or <1,500 mg if hypertensive/salt-sensitive)** buys roughly the DASH effect (~**5.5/3.0 mmHg** in normotensives, up to ~**11/6 mmHg** in hypertensives). Roughly **+1,640 mg/day potassium ≈ 21% lower stroke risk** in cohort data — i.e., about one extra baked potato (~925 mg) plus a cup of beans (~600 mg).
  - **Hard reversal — CKD (especially G3b-G5), or ACE inhibitors / ARBs / potassium-sparing diuretics → do NOT chase these numbers;** target is individualized and often *restricted* (commonly ~2,000-3,000 mg under a renal dietitian), and supplemental/"salt-substitute" potassium can cause fatal hyperkalemia. Confirm with labs first.
  - **Food, not pills:** OTC potassium tablets are capped at 99 mg for a reason — get the rest from potatoes (~925 mg/medium baked), beans/lentils (~600-700 mg/cup), spinach, and dried fruit.

**Folate before conception prevents neural tube defects (strong).** The RDA is 400 mcg DFE for adults and 600 mcg in pregnancy. Every woman capable of pregnancy should get 400 mcg/day of folic acid from supplements or fortified foods, **beginning before conception** — mandatory US grain fortification since 1998 cut neural tube defects at the population level [NIH ODS 2024](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Folate-HealthProfessional/). One important caution: high folic acid can correct the anemia of B12 deficiency while letting its neurological damage progress undetected — which is why the UL for *synthetic* folic acid (supplements plus fortified foods) is **1,000 mcg/day** for adults, while food folate carries no ceiling.

**Adjust it —** baseline **400 mcg DFE/day** for adults; synthetic folic-acid UL **1,000 mcg/day**. The big multiplier is reproductive stage, and it is *time-sensitive*:
  - **Anyone who could become pregnant → 400 mcg/day of folic acid** from supplements or fortified foods (on top of food folate), **starting ≥1 month before conception** through the first trimester, because the neural tube closes by ~week 4 — before most people know they are pregnant.
  - **Pregnancy → 600 mcg DFE**; **lactation → 500 mcg DFE.**
  - **Higher-risk pregnancies → clinician-directed high dose:** a **prior neural-tube-defect pregnancy** warrants ~**4,000 mcg (4 mg)/day** under medical supervision (above the general UL, deliberately). Certain anti-seizure meds and methotrexate also raise needs — individualize with a prescriber.
  - **Don't mega-dose blind:** if you take ≥1,000 mcg folic acid and have any B12 risk (vegan, age >50, metformin/PPI), check B12 first so masked deficiency isn't missed.

**B12 is the defining vegan-and-aging nutrient (strong).** The RDA is 2.4 mcg/day, with no upper limit (toxicity is low). Because B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods, vegans and vegetarians **must** use fortified foods or supplements; deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia and potentially irreversible nerve damage [NIH ODS 2024](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/). Roughly 3.6% of US adults are deficient and ~12.5% insufficient. Older adults (reduced stomach acid), people with pernicious anemia, and long-term users of metformin or proton-pump inhibitors are also at risk — and because liver stores are large, symptoms can take years to appear.

**Adjust it —** baseline **2.4 mcg/day** for adults; no UL (toxicity is minimal). The RDA barely moves, but the *form and source* you need changes sharply with biology:
  - **Pregnancy → 2.6 mcg; lactation → 2.8 mcg.**
  - **Age >50 → keep the same 2.4 mcg target but get most of it from fortified foods or a supplement,** not food-bound B12: 10-30% of older adults have atrophic gastritis and can't liberate B12 from animal protein, whereas *crystalline* B12 (fortified cereals, supplements) doesn't need stomach acid.
  - **Vegan/vegetarian → non-negotiable supplement or fortified foods.** Because absorption per dose is limited (~50% at low doses, far less per single large dose), a practical regimen is **50–100 mcg/day** of cyanocobalamin **or 1,000–2,000 mcg twice weekly** — the higher numbers exploit passive (acid-independent) absorption, not because daily needs are that high.
  - **Pernicious anemia, post-gastrectomy/bariatric, or documented deficiency → high-dose oral (1,000-2,000 mcg/day) or intramuscular injections** per clinician; **metformin or PPI/H2-blocker users → screen serum B12 periodically.**

**Iodine protects the developing brain (strong).** The RDA is 150 mcg/day, rising to 220 mcg in pregnancy and 290 mcg in lactation; the upper limit is 1,100 mcg. Deficiency causes goiter and, in pregnancy and infancy, irreversible cognitive loss — chronic moderate-to-severe deficiency reduces IQ by about 12-13.5 points [NIH ODS 2024](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-HealthProfessional/). Iodized salt is the global cornerstone; about 88% of households worldwide use it, leaving roughly a billion people without [NIH ODS 2024](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-HealthProfessional/).

**Adjust it —** baseline **150 mcg/day** for adults, UL **1,100 mcg/day**. Pregnancy is where the dependency bites hardest, because the deficit is paid by the fetal brain:
  - **Pregnancy → 220 mcg; lactation → 290 mcg.** Most prenatal vitamins should supply ~150 mcg iodine (typically as potassium iodide); check the label, as not all do.
  - **No iodized salt → plan a deliberate source.** Sea salt, kosher salt, Himalayan salt, and the salt in *processed/restaurant* food are mostly **not** iodized; switching your home shaker to iodized salt (~**76 mcg per ¼ tsp**) typically covers the adult RDA. Dairy, eggs, and seafood are the other reliable foods.
  - **Vegan/dairy-and-seafood-avoider → highest risk;** use iodized salt or a supplement providing ~150 mcg, but **avoid kelp/seaweed megadoses** (a single serving can deliver thousands of mcg and tip into the 1,100 mcg UL, paradoxically causing thyroid dysfunction).
  - **Pre-existing thyroid disease → individualize with a clinician;** both too little and too much iodine can destabilize Hashimoto's or Graves'.

**Magnesium: widely under-eaten, modest measured benefit (moderate).** The RDA is ~400-420 mg/day for men and ~310-320 mg for women, and about 48% of Americans fall below the estimated average requirement from food; the 350 mg upper limit applies only to *supplemental* magnesium, not food [NIH ODS 2024](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/). Observational data tie higher intake to ~15% lower type 2 diabetes risk per 100 mg/day, but supplement trials are inconsistent and blood-pressure effects are small. Pumpkin seeds, almonds, spinach, legumes, and whole grains are reliable sources.

**Adjust it —** baseline **men 400-420 mg / women 310-320 mg per day** from food, with a **350 mg UL on *supplemental* magnesium only** (food magnesium has no ceiling — the kidneys clear the excess):
  - **Age:** the RDA steps up at 31 (men 400→420, women 310→320). **Pregnancy → +40 mg** over the age-matched baseline (~350-360 mg).
  - **Supplement cautiously:** keep added magnesium **≤350 mg/day** to avoid diarrhea; choose better-absorbed forms (citrate, glycinate) over oxide; the ~15%-lower-diabetes signal is observational, so prioritize the food sources above before pills.
  - **Reduced clearance — CKD or severe renal impairment → avoid magnesium supplements/antacids** without medical supervision (risk of hypermagnesemia); the food-vs-supplement line matters most here.
  - **PPI use >1 year, chronic diarrhea, or alcohol-use disorder → higher depletion risk;** check a serum (ideally RBC) magnesium rather than blanket-dosing.

### What it depends on

- **Life stage and sex.** Menstruation roughly doubles iron needs; pregnancy raises iron, folate, and iodine; aging raises vitamin D, calcium, and B12 needs while reducing B12 absorption.
- **Diet pattern.** Vegans and vegetarians must plan for B12 (non-negotiable supplement or fortification), and watch iron, iodine, and zinc; dairy-avoiders need another calcium source.
- **Sun, latitude, and skin pigmentation.** All shift vitamin D synthesis — darker skin and northern winters mean more reliance on food or supplements.
- **Medications and malabsorption.** Metformin and PPIs lower B12; gastric-bypass and celiac/Crohn's impair multiple nutrients; chronic kidney disease *inverts* the potassium message into a hazard.
- **Whole food vs. supplement.** For most healthy adults, food-based intake is both safer and better-supported; supplements earn their place mainly for documented deficiency or defined high-risk groups.

### Practical takeaways

- **Cover the non-negotiable supplements by group:** 400 mcg/day folic acid before and during early pregnancy; a B12 supplement or fortified foods for anyone eating little or no animal product.
- **Lean on iodized salt** as your default salt — a quiet, high-leverage public-health win.
- **Eat for potassium, not pill it:** potatoes, beans, lentils, dried fruit, and leafy greens, while trimming sodium. (Exception: kidney disease or potassium-affecting meds — ask your clinician first.)
- **Treat vitamin D as deficiency-correction, not a cure-all.** Test and supplement if you are at risk (limited sun, dark skin, older age, obesity, malabsorption); do not expect 2,000 IU/day to prevent cancer or heart disease.
- **Boost plant iron with vitamin C** (citrus, peppers, tomatoes) at the same meal; keep iron supplements locked away from children.
- **Favor food sources of calcium and magnesium** — dairy or fortified alternatives, nuts, seeds, legumes, greens — over high-dose supplements.

### Unsettled or debated

- **Calcium supplements and heart risk.** Whether calcium *supplements* (not dietary calcium) modestly raise cardiovascular risk is genuinely contested; meta-analyses have found increases ranging from ~15% in cardiovascular risk to ~27-31% in myocardial infarction [Bolland et al., BMJ 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20671013/), but major expert panels dispute the interpretation and consider intake up to the upper limit safe [NIH ODS 2024](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/).
- **Optimal vitamin D level and dosing.** VITAL settled that routine high-dose supplements don't prevent disease in replete adults [Manson et al., NEJM 2019 (VITAL)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6425757/), but the ideal target level, who benefits from screening, and possible signals in normal-weight people or cancer mortality remain open questions.
