## 08. What It Depends On: Personalizing the Healthiest Diet

**Bottom line:** Roughly 80-90% of a healthy diet is the same for everyone — personalization tunes the *emphasis, dose, and form* of that core for life stage, goals, genetics, and conditions, so turn one or two dials, don't rebuild from scratch (and be skeptical of $200+/yr tests that beat free advice by single-digit percent).


By this point a fair question is: if the core is so settled, what's left to personalize? The honest answer is *less than the wellness industry wants you to believe, but more than nothing*. The convergent evidence — the US Dietary Guidelines, WHO/FAO, the EAT-Lancet Commission, and the Global Burden of Disease analysis — agrees that roughly 80-90% of what makes a diet healthy is the same for nearly everyone: mostly minimally-processed plants, adequate-not-excessive protein, unsaturated over saturated and trans fats, little added sugar or sodium, and calories matched to needs. The GBD 2017 analysis attributed about **11 million deaths a year** to suboptimal diet, dominated in aggregate by too much sodium and too little whole grains and fruit — the *same handful of levers* globally, though which one leads varies by region (high sodium leads in China and Japan, low whole grains in the US and India, low fruit in parts of sub-Saharan Africa) [GBD 2017 Diet, Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/article/S0140-6736(19)30041-8/fulltext); [GBD 2017 Diet, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6899507/).

So personalization is mostly not about discovering your secret idiosyncratic diet. It's about tuning the **emphasis, dose, and form** of that shared core to fit your life stage, physiology, genetics, gut, medical conditions, and culture and budget. A pregnant 30-year-old, a sarcopenic 80-year-old, a marathoner, and someone with celiac disease all eat recognizably the same foundation — they just turn different dials. Where a "modifier" is real and well-evidenced, I'll say so; where the personalization marketing has outrun the data, I'll say that too.

The strongest, most actionable "it depends" factors are **life stage** (infancy, pregnancy/lactation, older adults), **performance goals**, a few **genetic and microbiome modifiers**, and **diagnosed intolerances**. The frontier of deep individual metabolic prediction (PREDICT/ZOE) is genuinely interesting science, but its added value *over* the convergent core is still modest and partly contested.

### Key evidence

**The core dominates; personalization tunes proportions (strong).** Across guidelines the same foundation accounts for most of the measurable benefit. The DGA frames roughly **85% of calories** for nutrient-dense foods, leaving only ~15% for added sugars plus saturated fat, with targets of under 10% of calories each and sodium near 2,300 mg/day [DGA 2020-2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/sites/default/files/2021-11/DGA_2020-2025_TheGuidelines.pdf). The EAT-Lancet Commission's planetary-health diet emphasizes the same whole grains, fruits, vegetables, legumes, nuts, and unsaturated fats found across national guidelines [EAT-Lancet, Willett et al., Lancet 2019](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(18)31788-4/abstract); [EAT-Lancet 2019](https://eatforum.org/knowledge/diets-for-a-better-future/2019-eat-lancet-commission/). The takeaway: most "personalized plans" succeed because they keep this core, not because they replace it.

**Life stage is the single largest legitimate modifier — sometimes qualitatively, not just by dose (strong).** Infancy: WHO and the AAP recommend roughly 6 months of exclusive breastfeeding, then complementary foods — and timing of allergen introduction was *reversed* by trial evidence. The LEAP RCT found that early introduction of peanut (at 4-11 months) cut peanut allergy by about **80%** (17.2% to 3.2% by age 5) in high-risk infants, overturning decades of "delay" advice [Du Toit/LEAP, NEJM 2015](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMoa1414850); [WHO, Breastfeeding](https://www.who.int/health-topics/breastfeeding). Pregnancy raises specific requirements sharply, well above the non-pregnant adult RDAs of **400 mcg DFE folate** and **8 mg iron** (men) / **18 mg iron** (women 19-50) [NIH ODS, Folate](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Folate-HealthProfessional/); [NIH ODS, Iron](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/): **600 mcg DFE folate** (with at least 400 mcg supplemental folic acid started pre-conception to prevent neural-tube defects), **27 mg iron**, **220 mcg iodine**, **450 mg choline**, and about **200 mg DHA**, alongside only a small energy increase (~+340 kcal in the second trimester, ~+452 in the third) [NIH ODS, Pregnancy](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Pregnancy-HealthProfessional/); [ACOG, Nutrition During Pregnancy](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/nutrition-during-pregnancy). These are qualitative shifts: the folic-acid window matters before most people know they're pregnant — the CDC advises 400 mcg of folic acid daily starting before conception to prevent neural-tube defects [CDC, Folic Acid](https://www.cdc.gov/folic-acid/about/index.html).

**Adjust it — energy and key micronutrients by reproductive stage.** *Baseline (non-pregnant adult woman 19-50):* 400 mcg DFE folate, 18 mg iron, 150 mcg iodine, 425 mg choline, no extra energy. *Scales by trimester/stage:*
- *Energy:* T1 → +0 kcal; **T2 → +340 kcal/day; T3 → +452 kcal/day**; exclusive lactation → **+330 kcal/day (months 0-6), +400 kcal/day (months 7-12)** — the ~500 kcal/day milk-production cost is partly offset by ~170 kcal/day mobilized from fat stores, so the dietary add stays below the third-trimester figure (subtract toward the lower end if losing pregnancy weight is a goal).
- *Folate:* pregnancy → **600 mcg DFE** (≥400 mcg as supplemental folic acid, started pre-conception); lactation → **500 mcg DFE**. A prior NTD-affected pregnancy raises the clinician-directed dose to **4,000 mcg/day**.
- *Iron:* pregnancy → **27 mg/day** (≈+50%); lactation drops to **9-10 mg/day** (amenorrhea spares iron).
- *Iodine:* pregnancy → **220 mcg**; lactation → **290 mcg**. *Choline:* pregnancy → **450 mg**; lactation → **550 mg**. *DHA:* aim **~200-300 mg/day** preformed throughout pregnancy and lactation.
*Worked example:* a woman whose pre-pregnancy maintenance is ~2,000 kcal eats ~2,000 in T1, ~2,340 in T2, and ~2,450 in T3 — not "eating for two."

**Older adults need more protein than the general RDA (moderate, expert-consensus).** The adult RDA is 0.8 g/kg/day, but the PROT-AGE and ESPEN expert groups recommend **1.0-1.2 g/kg/day** for healthy older adults, **1.2-1.5** with acute or chronic illness, and up to **2.0 g/kg/day** in severe illness or malnutrition, to counter "anabolic resistance" and sarcopenia [Bauer/PROT-AGE, JAMDA 2013](https://www.jamda.com/article/S1525-8610(13)00326-5/fulltext); [Deutz/ESPEN, Clinical Nutrition 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24814383/). The practical lever is per-meal, leucine-rich protein (~25-30 g) plus resistance exercise. A 2025 indicator-amino-acid study estimated even higher needs in sarcopenic elders (recommended intake ~1.54 g/kg/day; industry-funded: Danone nutrition research grant) [Frontiers in Nutrition 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11906324/). This is the clearest age-driven change to the core; the exact number is debated, the direction is not.

**Adjust it — daily protein for older adults, set by health status, then split per meal.** *Baseline:* general-adult RDA **0.8 g/kg/day**. *Scales:* healthy 65+ → **1.0-1.2 g/kg/day** (a +0.2 to +0.4 g/kg bump); acute or chronic illness → **1.2-1.5 g/kg/day**; severe illness, injury, or malnutrition → up to **2.0 g/kg/day**. *Per-meal lever:* anabolic resistance raises the per-meal threshold from ~0.24 g/kg in the young to **~0.4 g/kg (≈25-40 g, ~2.5-3 g leucine)** in elders, so spread protein across **3-4 meals** rather than back-loading dinner. *Worked example:* a 70 kg older adult targets 70-84 g/day (1.0-1.2 g/kg) as roughly 4 × ~20-25 g. *Critical override — kidney disease:* this "more is better" rule reverses in CKD. Stage G3-5 not on dialysis and metabolically stable → **0.55-0.60 g/kg/day** (0.6-0.8 with diabetes); only maintenance dialysis returns the target to **1.0-1.2 g/kg/day** [KDOQI Nutrition in CKD 2020](https://www.ajkd.org/article/S0272-6386(20)30726-5/fulltext). When the two pull in opposite directions, the kidney target wins — see §09.

**Performance goals push protein and carbohydrate well above the general target (strong).** Morton et al.'s meta-analysis (49 studies, 1,863 participants) found a muscle-hypertrophy "breakpoint" near **1.6 g/kg/day** of protein, with the confidence interval reaching ~2.2 [Morton, BJSM 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/). Sports-nutrition bodies endorse 1.4-2.0 g/kg/day for athletes broadly, and up to ~3.1 g/kg/day during energy restriction to preserve lean mass [Jäger/ISSN Position Stand, JISSN 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/). Endurance training also demands carbohydrate *periodized to workload* — up to 6-10 g/kg/day in heavy training — which is the opposite emphasis from someone managing insulin resistance [Thomas/AND-DC-ACSM, Med Sci Sports Exerc 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26891166/); [Kato/IAAO, PLoS ONE 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4913918/).

**Adjust it — protein and carbohydrate scale to goal and training load.** *Protein baseline:* 0.8 g/kg/day RDA. *Scales:* maximize hypertrophy → **~1.6 g/kg/day** (meta-analysis breakpoint 1.62; nothing reliably gained above the 95% CI ceiling of **2.2 g/kg/day**); general athlete maintenance → **1.4-2.0 g/kg/day**; dieting to preserve lean mass in an energy deficit → **2.0 up to ~3.1 g/kg/day** (the leaner and steeper the cut, the higher). Per training meal, **~0.4 g/kg (≈0.3-0.5)** maximizes muscle protein synthesis, so a 80 kg lifter takes ~32 g × 4 meals ≈ 128 g (1.6 g/kg). *Carbohydrate baseline:* general intake ~3-5 g/kg/day. *Scales to workload:* light/skill days → **3-5 g/kg**; ~1 h moderate → **5-7 g/kg**; 1-3 h endurance → **6-10 g/kg**; >4-5 h/day → **8-12 g/kg**. Periodize *down* on rest days; this carbohydrate emphasis is the explicit, evidence-based exception to "lower-carb is healthier," and the opposite dial from insulin resistance.

**Insulin sensitivity shifts carbohydrate emphasis — within several patterns (moderate).** The ADA's nutrition consensus concluded that reducing overall carbohydrate "has demonstrated the most evidence" for improving blood glucose in type 2 diabetes, and that this can be applied across eating patterns chosen to fit preference [Evert/ADA Consensus, Diabetes Care 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7011201/). Low-carbohydrate diets reliably lower HbA1c, triglycerides, and medication needs short-term, but the effect attenuates by about 12 months and long-term cardiovascular outcomes are weaker. Carbohydrate *quality* — fiber, intact grains, low glycemic load — helps everyone; the optimal *quantity* genuinely depends on the individual.

**Adjust it — carbohydrate quantity by metabolic status; quality for everyone.** *Quality baseline (universal):* aim for **~14 g fiber per 1,000 kcal** (≈25 g/day for women, 38 g/day for men), with most carbohydrate from intact grains, legumes, fruit, and vegetables — this lever applies regardless of how much carbohydrate you eat. *Quantity scales with insulin sensitivity:* a metabolically healthy, active person can sit at the higher end of typical intake (~45-55% of calories, more if training hard, per the carbohydrate rule above); with insulin resistance or type 2 diabetes, **reducing total carbohydrate has the most evidence** for lowering glucose — but no single split is mandated. *Depends on:* by the common Feinman classification, "moderate-carb" is **26-45% of calories**, "low-carb" is **<26%** (often ~50-130 g/day), and "very-low/ketogenic" is **<10% (~20-50 g/day)**; choose the highest carbohydrate level that keeps HbA1c and post-meal glucose at target so the diet stays sustainable. Expect the early HbA1c advantage of going low to **fade by ~12 months**, and individualize with a clinician if on insulin or a sulfonylurea (hypoglycemia risk) — see §06 and §09.

**Individual responses to identical meals vary a lot — the basis for personalized nutrition (moderate, partly contested).** PREDICT 1 (~1,000 adults including hundreds of twins) found large between-person variability in responses to identical meals — population coefficients of variation of about **68% for glucose and 103% for triglyceride** (i.e., well over two-fold spreads), and even within one person across the day. Common genetic variants explained only ~**9%** of glucose-response variation and under 1% of triglyceride variation, while the gut microbiome explained ~**6-7%** — and identical twins often responded quite differently, showing genes alone predict poorly [Berry/PREDICT 1, Nature Medicine 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8265154/). The ZOE METHOD RCT (n=347, 18 weeks; industry-funded: ZOE Ltd, with multiple authors employees/consultants/shareholders) found a personalized program improved triglycerides, weight, and microbiome diversity versus a USDA-guideline control — though the *incremental* benefit over good generic advice, and the commercial framing, are debated [Bermingham/ZOE METHOD, Nature Medicine 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11271409/).

**Adjust it — weight your trust to how much each input actually explains.** *Baseline:* the shared core explains the large majority of dietary health benefit; treat individual-response data as fine-tuning, not foundation. *Scales with variance explained (PREDICT 1):* genetic variants accounted for only **~9.5% of glucose-response variation and ~0.8% for triglycerides**; the gut microbiome added **~7.1% for post-meal lipemia and ~6.0% for glycemia** — each modest, and even identical twins responded differently, so no single test is decisive. *Depends on:* spend on personalization roughly in proportion to that signal — a **$200-300+/yr** subscription test buys single-digit-percent predictive gains over free generic advice, so prioritize it only after the high-yield core (sodium, fiber, whole grains, saturated-fat swap, calorie match) is already in place. The one place individual glucose data is clinically worth it is established diabetes, where it guides medication and meal timing — not as a wellness upsell for metabolically healthy people.

**A few genes and ancestries change specific foods — lactase persistence is the cleanest case (strong).** Only about **35% of adults worldwide** keep producing lactase into adulthood; roughly **65%** are lactase non-persistent, with non-persistence exceeding 90% in parts of East Asia and high across many African and Indigenous populations [Genetics of Lactose Intolerance, Nutrients 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7551416/). This changes whether fluid milk is a practical "core" food — but fermented dairy, hard cheese, lactose-free milk, or non-dairy calcium sources meet the same need, so it shifts *food choice*, not the requirement for calcium and protein (the calcium RDA is **1,000 mg/day** for adults 19-50 and **1,200 mg/day** for women 51+ and everyone 71+) [NIH ODS, Calcium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/). Separately, **FADS** gene variants slow the already-low conversion of plant omega-3 (ALA) to EPA/DHA — baseline conversion is only ~5-10% to EPA and roughly 2-5% to DHA — so vegetarians, vegans, and pregnant/lactating people who are "slow converters" may benefit from direct algal DHA rather than relying on flax or chia [FADS/vegetarian, Nutrients 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6682884/).

**Adjust it — calcium target by life stage, dairy *form* by genotype.** *Baseline:* 1,000 mg calcium/day for adults 19-50. *Scales:* the requirement is genotype-independent — lactase persistence changes the *vehicle*, not the *amount*. *Depends on:* age 51+ women / everyone 71+ → **1,200 mg/day**; pregnancy/lactation ≤18 yrs → **1,300 mg/day** (19+ stays 1,000 mg). For the lactose-intolerant, hit the same target via ~1 cup lactose-free milk or fortified soy (~300 mg each), 1.5 oz hard cheese (~300 mg, near-zero lactose), 6 oz yogurt (~250-300 mg, lower lactose), or 2 cups cooked kale/bok choy (~250-350 mg). *FADS slow converters* (vegan/vegetarian, pregnant/lactating): don't rely on ALA — target **200-300 mg/day preformed DHA (+EPA)** from algal oil, since converting that much from flax would require an implausible 4-6+ g ALA at a 2-5% yield.

**Diagnosed intolerances override the core for specific foods — non-negotiably (strong).** Celiac disease (~1% prevalence) requires a **strict, lifelong gluten-free diet** — the only effective treatment, an absolute exclusion rather than a preference, with attention to replacing fiber, B vitamins, and iron from naturally gluten-free whole foods [ACG, Celiac/Gluten](https://gi.org/topics/celiac-non-celiac-gluten-sensitivity-gluten-free-diets/). IgE food allergy requires complete avoidance of the trigger. By contrast, the common adult-onset (primary) form of lactose intolerance is usually *dose-dependent*: many people with it tolerate about 12 g of lactose (roughly one cup of milk), especially with meals [NIDDK, Lactose Intolerance Diet](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/lactose-intolerance/eating-diet-nutrition). This dose-dependence does not apply to the rarer congenital lactase deficiency (little or no lactase from birth) or to active secondary deficiency from gut injury (e.g., infection, celiac, or Crohn's disease), which can require near-total avoidance [NIDDK, Lactose Intolerance Symptoms & Causes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/lactose-intolerance/symptoms-causes).

**Adjust it — exclusion is binary for some diagnoses, a dial for others.** *Absolute (zero tolerance):* celiac disease → **strict lifelong gluten-free**, with no safe dose (trace gluten triggers immune injury); IgE food allergy → **complete avoidance** of the trigger. These do not scale — replace the lost nutrients instead (gluten-free whole grains and fortified foods for fiber, B vitamins, and iron). *Dose-dependent (primary adult lactose intolerance):* *baseline* tolerance ≈ **12 g lactose at once** (~1 cup milk), usually without major symptoms, especially taken with a meal. *Scales/depends on:* split across the day → many tolerate **~18-24 g/day** in divided doses; *fermented or aged forms* cut the load (hard cheese near-zero, yogurt lower than milk); *lactase enzyme tablets* extend tolerance further. *Override:* congenital or active secondary deficiency → drop toward **near-zero** until/unless the gut heals.

### What it depends on

- **Life stage.** Infancy (breastfeeding, early allergen introduction), pregnancy/lactation (folate, iron, iodine, choline, DHA), and older age (more protein, ~1.0-1.2+ g/kg/day) each reshape the core meaningfully.
- **Goal and activity level.** Muscle gain and endurance performance raise protein (to ~1.6 g/kg/day, up to ~2.0-3.1 when dieting to spare lean mass) and carbohydrate (to 6-10 g/kg/day in heavy training) far above the general 0.8 g/kg target.
- **Metabolic health.** Insulin resistance or type 2 diabetes makes carbohydrate *quantity* — not just quality — worth individualizing; this is one dial where personalization is clinically real.
- **Genetics and ancestry.** Lactase persistence changes how much fluid dairy fits; FADS variants change whether plant ALA is enough omega-3 for you.
- **Gut microbiome and individual response.** Glucose and lipid responses to identical meals vary up to ~2-fold; useful to know, but not yet a proven reason to abandon the core.
- **Diagnosed conditions.** Celiac and IgE allergy are absolute exclusions; lactose intolerance is dose-dependent, not all-or-nothing.
- **Culture and budget.** The core flexes across every cuisine — the right personalization keeps it affordable and sustainable, not exotic. **Adjust it —** the cheapest protein and fiber sources hit the same targets: dried legumes deliver protein at well under **$0.50 per ~15-18 g protein** per cooked cup versus several dollars for the same protein in meat, and canned/frozen produce matches fresh on most nutrients at a fraction of the cost; a healthy core does *not* require a $200+/yr personalization subscription or specialty "superfoods."

### Practical takeaways

- **Start from the shared core, then adjust one or two dials** that actually apply to you — don't rebuild from scratch. To put real numbers on those dials, use Section 20's five-step method and "it depends" matrix to personalize your calorie ballpark, BMI, and protein target, and Section 09's per-condition evidence and matrix for disease-specific dietary targets.
- **If you're pregnant or planning to be,** take 400+ mcg folic acid *before* conception (4,000 mcg if a prior pregnancy had a neural-tube defect), and meet the raised targets: 27 mg iron, 220 mcg iodine, 450 mg choline, and ~200-300 mg DHA, adding only ~+340 kcal/day in T2 and ~+452 in T3. For your infant, introduce common allergens early — but stratify by risk: for low-risk babies, introduce peanut and other common allergens early at home; for high-risk babies (severe eczema and/or egg allergy — the LEAP study population), have peanut-specific IgE or a skin-prick test done first, with a possible supervised first feeding, before any peanut introduction [NIAID 2017 Addendum Guidelines](https://www.niaid.nih.gov/sites/default/files/addendum-peanut-allergy-prevention-guidelines.pdf).
- **If you're over ~65,** raise protein toward 1.0-1.2 g/kg/day (1.2-1.5 with chronic illness), aim for ~0.4 g/kg (≈25-40 g) per meal across 3-4 meals, and pair it with resistance exercise — *unless* you have non-dialysis CKD, where the target drops to 0.55-0.8 g/kg/day (see §09).
- **If you train hard,** scale protein toward ~1.6 g/kg/day (up to ~2.2 for hypertrophy, ~2.0-3.1 when dieting to keep muscle) and carbohydrate to training volume (~3-5 g/kg light days, 6-10 g/kg in heavy endurance blocks); this is a real, evidence-based exception to "lower-carb is healthier."
- **If you have insulin resistance,** prioritize carbohydrate quality first — ~14 g fiber per 1,000 kcal (≈25 g/day women, 38 g/day men) from intact grains, legumes, and vegetables — then individualize *quantity* downward with your clinician (low-carb <26% of calories, very-low <10%); both lower-carb and Mediterranean/DASH patterns are legitimate, and watch for hypoglycemia if on insulin or a sulfonylurea.
- **If dairy bothers you,** you likely just need a smaller dose, fermented forms, or lactose-free products — not zero calcium (still aim for the **1,000-1,200 mg/day** RDA from any source).
- **If you eat little or no fish,** consider an algal DHA/EPA supplement, especially when pregnant, breastfeeding, or vegan.
- **Get celiac and food allergy formally diagnosed before excluding foods** — and replace the lost nutrients deliberately. Self-diagnosed "gluten sensitivity" is a poor reason for permanent, nutritionally narrowing restriction.
- **Be skeptical of expensive "personalized nutrition" tests** ($200-300+/yr) that contradict the core. Genes explain only ~9.5% of glucose-response variation (~0.8% for triglycerides) and the microbiome ~6-7% — single-digit-percent gains over free generic advice. The biggest wins are still the boring shared ones; personalization is the fine-tuning, not the foundation.

### Unsettled or debated

- **How much does deep metabolic personalization add over good generic advice?** PREDICT/ZOE show real person-to-person variation and a positive RCT, but the *incremental* benefit beyond a Mediterranean/DASH-style core — and the commercial framing — remain genuinely debated.
- **The exact optimal protein target for older and sarcopenic adults** is unsettled: the direction (higher than 0.8 g/kg/day) is solid expert consensus, but estimates range from ~1.0 to ~1.5+ g/kg/day.
- **The optimal carbohydrate quantity in type 2 diabetes** is contested; low-carb's early HbA1c edge fades by a year, and authorities decline to name one best macro split (see Sections 06 and 09).
