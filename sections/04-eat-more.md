## 04. What to Eat More Of: The Protective Foods

**Bottom line:** Build the plate around vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts/seeds, oily fish, and olive oil — most of the benefit is captured going from 'almost none' to 'a reasonable amount,' and it works largely by crowding out refined grains, processed meat, and butter.


The "eat more" list is short, consistent, and genuinely enjoyable. Across the largest prospective cohorts (EPIC, the Nurses' Health Study, PURE), dose-response meta-analyses of hundreds of studies, and a handful of landmark randomized trials (PREDIMED, DASH), the same foods keep surfacing as protective: vegetables and fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts and seeds, oily fish, and extra-virgin olive oil. These are the backbone of every credible dietary pattern, from Mediterranean to DASH to the planetary EAT-Lancet reference diet, and they anchor the evidence-based [Harvard Healthy Eating Plate](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/healthy-eating-plate/).

Two themes matter more than any single food. First, **the benefit is largely about replacement** — eating more fish, beans, and olive oil works in part by crowding out refined grains, processed meat, and butter. Second, **whole foods beat isolated nutrients**. The clearest cautionary tale is omega-3: eating fish is reliably protective, yet low-dose fish-oil supplements have mostly failed to reproduce that benefit. The food, not the pill, is what the evidence supports.

A practical note on the numbers below: most quantitative estimates come from observational cohorts, which establish strong, consistent associations but cannot fully prove causation. The dose-response curves are almost always **curvilinear** — benefits accrue steeply at low-to-moderate intakes and then flatten. You capture most of the upside by getting from "almost none" to "a reasonable amount." You do not need to be perfect, and more is not endlessly better.

One timing note: the new **2025–2030 US Dietary Guidelines** were released January 7, 2026, shifting tone toward "eat real food" and somewhat higher protein, while retaining the emphasis on vegetables, fruits, and whole grains discussed here ([Harvard, *The Nutrition Source* 2026](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/2026/01/09/dietary-guidelines-for-americans-2025-2030/)).

### Key evidence

- **Fruits and vegetables (strong, observational).** The long-standing WHO target is ~400 g/day (about 5 servings of 80 g) for everyone older than 10 ([WHO, *Healthy diet*](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet)), but pooled data show mortality benefit continuing up to ~800 g/day. The Aune 2017 meta-analysis (95 studies, 142 publications) found all-cause mortality roughly 31% lower at ~800 g/day versus none (RR ~0.69), with cancer risk plateauing around 600 g/day ([Aune 2017, *Int J Epidemiol*](https://academic.oup.com/ije/article/46/3/1029/3039477)). An earlier 833,234-person analysis found mortality dropped ~5% per added daily serving (HR ~0.95), plateauing around 5 servings/day ([Wang 2014, *BMJ*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4115152/)).
  - **Adjust it —** **floor 400 g/day (5 servings), optimum ~800 g/day (~10 servings)**, where a WHO serving = **80 g** (≈ ½ cup chopped, or 1 medium fruit). **Scales with energy needs and the curve:** roughly half the mortality benefit is already captured by the *first* ~250 g, and the all-cause curve flattens by ~800 g (cancer by ~600 g) — going beyond ~800 g/day adds little measured benefit. On a larger plate, hold produce near **≈5 servings per 1,000 kcal** so a 3,000-kcal athlete lands near the ~800 g optimum without crowding out needed energy. **Depends on:**
    - **Limited budget/access or a non-Western pattern →** the global PURE cohort saw maximal benefit already at **3–4 servings/day (375–500 g)**; treat 400 g as a high-value target, not a failure point.
    - **Advanced CKD (G4–G5) or hyperkalemia →** potassium-restrict; choose lower-potassium produce (berries, apples, grapes, cabbage, green beans) and **cap added potassium per a renal dietitian's mg/day target** rather than maximizing volume (see §09).
    - **Warfarin →** keep vitamin-K-rich greens *consistent* week to week (don't swing from 0 to 3 cups); the target is stability, not avoidance.
    - **IBS / strictures / recent GI surgery →** raise volume gradually (e.g., **+1 serving every 2–3 days**) and lean on cooked, low-FODMAP produce.
- **The highest-value produce (moderate).** Leafy greens, cruciferous vegetables, berries, and citrus carry the strongest associations. Highest versus lowest green-leafy-vegetable intake is associated with ~16% lower CVD incidence (RR ~0.84) ([Pollock 2016, *JRSM Cardiovasc Dis*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4973479/)). Plausible mechanisms include dietary nitrate (greens), which improves blood pressure and endothelial function, and glucosinolates (cruciferous).
- **Whole grains (strong, observational).** Target at least 3 servings/day (~90 g of whole-grain food). Per 90 g/day, the Aune 2016 meta-analysis found RR 0.78 for cardiovascular disease, 0.81 for CHD, and 0.83 for all-cause mortality, with benefit continuing to ~210–225 g/day ([Aune 2016, *BMJ*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4908315/)).
  - **Adjust it —** **baseline ~3 servings/day (~90 g)**, where one serving ≈ **1 slice 100%-whole-grain bread, ½ cup cooked oats/brown rice/quinoa, or ~30 g dry**; the Aune curve keeps dropping to **~210–225 g/day (~7 servings)** before flattening. The DGA framing is **"make at least half your grains whole"** — so the absolute number scales with total grain intake, which scales with calories: at 2,000 kcal aim ~6 oz-equivalents of grain (≥3 whole), at 3,000 kcal ~10 oz-equivalents (≥5 whole). **Depends on:**
    - **Type 2 diabetes / glycemic control →** prioritize intact/minimally milled grains (steel-cut oats, barley, bulgur, whole kernels) over finely ground whole-wheat flour; particle size, not just "whole," drives the glucose response.
    - **Celiac disease or gluten sensitivity →** swap to naturally gluten-free whole grains (brown rice, quinoa, buckwheat, certified GF oats, millet) — same target, different grains.
    - **Weight loss on a low-calorie plan (<1,500 kcal) →** keep whole grains but the absolute count drops with the calorie budget; protect protein and produce first, then fit grains to the remaining energy.
- **Dietary fiber (strong).** The protective target is **~25–38 g/day** (US Dietary Guidelines: ~25 g for women, ~38 g for men); the Lancet/Reynolds meta-analysis finds the steepest risk reduction at 25–29 g/day, with more still helping — yet most adults get only ~15 g. Highest versus lowest intake cut all-cause and CVD mortality, CHD, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and colorectal cancer by 15–30%, and the RCTs showed fiber lowers weight, cholesterol, and systolic blood pressure ([Reynolds 2019, *Lancet*](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(18)31809-9/fulltext)).
  - **Adjust it —** the cleanest scaling rule is the DGA/IOM figure: **~14 g of fiber per 1,000 kcal**. So a 1,600-kcal diet → ~22 g; 2,000 kcal → ~28 g; 2,500 kcal → ~35 g. The Reynolds sweet spot (25–29 g/day) sits right on this line for most adults. **Depends on:**
    - **Sex/age default (if not calorie-counting) →** ~25 g/day women, ~38 g/day men under 50; both drop ~3–5 g after 50 as energy needs fall.
    - **IBS, strictures, or recent GI surgery →** increase by **~5 g/week**, pair with fluid, and favor soluble/low-FODMAP sources; do not jump to target in one step.
    - **Hitting the produce + whole-grain + legume targets above already lands you near 25–30 g**, so treat fiber as an outcome of those, not a separate supplement to chase.
- **Legumes (moderate).** About 1 serving/day. Each added 50 g/day is associated with ~6% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.94) ([Zargarzadeh 2023, *Adv Nutr*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10103007/)); in that analysis the benefit was clearest for all-cause and stroke mortality, with non-significant associations for CVD-, CHD-, and cancer-specific mortality — so the hard-outcome evidence for legumes is more mixed than for nuts or fish. A separate dose-response meta-analysis found the inverse association with coronary heart disease leveling off around 400 g/week ([Mendes 2023, *Nutr Metab Cardiovasc Dis*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.numecd.2022.10.006)). RCTs (~130 g/day) consistently show lower LDL cholesterol and improved glycemic control. The 2025 Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee's Scientific Report recommended reclassifying beans, peas, and lentils into the **Protein Foods Group** and increasing the amount (e.g., ~3 cups/week vs. 2 cups in the 2,200-kcal pattern) ([Vegetarian Resource Group summary, 2025](https://www.vrg.org/blog/2025/01/31/2025-dietary-guidelines-advisory-committees-scientific-report-recommends-eating-more-beans-peas-and-lentils-and-eating-less-red-and-processed-meat/)) — though the final 2025–2030 guidelines departed from parts of that report, so treat ~2.5–3 cups/week as a well-supported target rather than settled law.
  - **Adjust it —** **baseline ~2.5–3 cups cooked/week (DGAC floor 2.5 cups per 2,000 kcal)**, where ½ cup cooked beans = one serving ≈ **100 kcal, ~7–8 g protein, ~6–8 g fiber**; the CHD curve flattens around **~400 g/week (~3 cups)**. **Scales two ways:** with calories (the cup target rises with the pattern's energy level), and with how much you're using legumes as a **protein source** — ½ cup ≈ ~7–8 g toward your daily protein math (canonical in §02/§20). **Depends on:**
    - **Vegetarian/vegan or replacing red & processed meat →** push toward the upper end (~3+ cups/week) so legumes do real protein work; substitution for meat is where the mortality signal is strongest.
    - **Type 2 diabetes / LDL lowering →** the RCT dose is higher (~130 g/day ≈ ~1 cup/day, ~5+ cups/week) and reliably cuts LDL and post-meal glucose.
    - **IBS/FODMAP sensitivity →** start at **½ cup** servings, rinse canned beans (cuts fermentable oligosaccharides and sodium), or use smaller, better-tolerated lentils; build up gradually.
    - **Advanced CKD →** beans are potassium- and phosphorus-rich; portion to the renal team's mg/day limits rather than maximizing.
- **Nuts and seeds (strong).** About 28 g/day (1 oz, a handful). Per 28 g/day, RR 0.71 for CHD and 0.78 for all-cause mortality ([Aune 2016, *BMC Med*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5137221/)). This is reinforced by RCT data: in PREDIMED, a Mediterranean diet supplemented with ~30 g/day mixed nuts cut major cardiovascular events ~28% (HR 0.72) ([Estruch 2018, *NEJM*](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389)). Note that PREDIMED compared whole dietary patterns against a low-fat-advice control — not the same diet with and without nuts — so the HR reflects the overall pattern rather than nuts alone (the 2013 results were retracted and republished in 2018 after a randomization error was corrected; test foods industry-donated; see §06). Despite their energy density, nuts are not linked to weight gain in cohorts.
  - **Adjust it —** **baseline ~28 g/day (1 oz, a handful) ≈ 160–200 kcal**; the dose-response benefit is near-maximal by ~28 g/day and the curve flattens beyond ~15–28 g, so more is not better. **Fit to your calorie budget:** at 1 oz, nuts are ~8–10% of a 2,000-kcal day but ~13% of a 1,500-kcal day — on a tight deficit, drop to **½ oz (~14 g, ~80–100 kcal)** rather than cutting them out, since the per-28 g cardioprotective signal is what you're after. **Depends on:**
    - **Weight loss (<1,500 kcal/day) →** ½ oz/day keeps the benefit while protecting the deficit; portion by weight, not by free pour (handfuls drift to 2–3 oz).
    - **Higher energy needs (active, ~3,000 kcal) →** 1.5–2 oz/day is fine and an easy, nutrient-dense way to add calories.
    - **Sodium-sensitive/hypertensive →** choose **unsalted** (salted nuts add ~100–185 mg sodium per oz, varying by brand); see §05/§09.
    - **Tree-nut or peanut allergy →** substitute seeds (pumpkin, sunflower) at the same ~28 g/day for comparable fats and minerals.
    - **Blood-thinner note:** nuts are fine; no INR concern (that caveat is for vitamin-K greens, above).
- **Fish, especially oily fish (strong).** The AHA recommends **2 servings/week** (~7 oz/198 g, i.e. two 3.5-oz cooked servings); the FDA/EPA frame the same target as **8–12 oz/week** using a 4-oz cooked serving. One serving of oily fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel) supplies roughly the AHA's ~250 mg/day EPA+DHA target on its own ([AHA/Rimm 2018, *Circulation*](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000574)). In pooled analyses, ~250 mg/day EPA+DHA was associated with roughly 36% lower CHD-death risk, with benefit plateauing after 1–2 servings/week ([Mozaffarian & Rimm 2006, *JAMA*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17047219/)).
  - **Adjust it —** **baseline ~7–8 oz/week (two ~3.5–4 oz cooked servings) of fish, at least one oily**, delivering ≈250 mg/day EPA+DHA. **Scales with the outcome you want:** the CHD-mortality curve is steep from 0→1–2 servings/week and flat above; you do *not* gain by eating fish daily. **Depends on:**
    - **Pregnancy/breastfeeding → 8–12 oz/week** of lower-mercury fish (the upper end matters: ~50% of pregnant women eat <2 oz/week, far below target), restrict mid-mercury species to ≤4 oz/week, and avoid the seven high-mercury "Choices to Avoid."
    - **No oily fish / vegetarian →** aim for an algal-oil EPA+DHA supplement of **~250 mg/day** (pregnancy often **≥200–300 mg DHA/day** specifically); plant ALA (flax, walnuts, chia) converts to EPA at only ~5–10% and to DHA at typically <1%, so it is not a reliable substitute.
    - **Established CHD on a statin with triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL →** this is the one group where high-dose prescription EPA (icosapent ethyl 4 g/day) has trial support (REDUCE-IT); food fish stays the baseline for everyone else (see *Unsettled*).
    - **Gout/hyperuricemia →** small oily fish (sardines, anchovies, mackerel) and fish roe are purine-rich; if attacks are frequent, favor salmon/trout and keep total fish toward the lower end (~2 servings/week).
- **Extra-virgin olive oil (strong).** In ~92,000 US health professionals followed 28 years, >7 g/day (about half a tablespoon) versus rare use was associated with 19% lower total mortality (HR 0.81); substituting ~10 g/day of olive oil for butter, margarine, mayonnaise, or dairy fat lowered total and cause-specific mortality 8–34% ([Guasch-Ferré 2022, *JACC*](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2021.10.041); [full text, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8851878/)). PREDIMED, a randomized trial, found a Mediterranean diet enriched with extra-virgin olive oil cut major cardiovascular events ~31% (HR 0.69) versus a low-fat-advice control ([Estruch 2018, *NEJM*](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389)) — again a whole-pattern effect, not an isolated olive-oil effect.
  - **Adjust it —** the measured-benefit threshold is **>7 g/day (~½ tbsp)**; **1 tbsp ≈ 13.5–14 g ≈ 120 kcal**. The PREDIMED arm used a generous **~50 g/day (~4 tbsp)**, so anywhere from **½ to ~4 tbsp/day** sits inside the evidence. **The value is mostly substitution, not addition:** swapping ~10 g/day of olive oil for butter, margarine, mayonnaise, or dairy fat lowered mortality 8–34%; pouring it on *top* of existing fat just adds ~120 kcal/tbsp. **Depends on:**
    - **Calorie budget →** at ~120 kcal/tbsp, count it; on a weight-loss plan keep it near ½–1 tbsp and use it to replace other fats rather than stack on them.
    - **Cooking →** EVOO is fine for most home cooking (smoke point ~190–210°C); for very high-heat searing, a more refined oil avoids smoking, but the polyphenols favoring EVOO are a cold-use bonus.
    - **Default-fat rule →** make it your replacement for butter/margarine/mayo first; that swap, not the absolute grams, is where the mortality signal lives.

### What it depends on

- **How much produce is "enough" is genuinely debated.** Western cohorts suggest benefit up to ~800 g/day; the global PURE study found maximal benefit already at 3–4 servings/day (375–500 g) with no further gain — relevant where higher intakes are costly ([PURE 2017, *Lancet*](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(17)32253-5/fulltext)).
- **Preparation matters.** Fried fish negates the cardiovascular benefit; canned or salted legumes add sodium; salted or honey-roasted nuts add sodium and sugar.
- **Pregnancy and young children.** Choose low-mercury fish (salmon, sardines, trout) and avoid all seven fish on the FDA/EPA "Choices to Avoid" list — shark, swordfish, king mackerel, bigeye tuna, marlin, orange roughy, and Gulf of Mexico tilefish ([FDA, *Advice About Eating Fish*](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish); [EPA, *EPA-FDA Advice About Eating Fish*](https://www.epa.gov/choose-fish-and-shellfish-wisely/epa-fda-advice-about-eating-fish-and-shellfish)). **Adjust it —** pregnant or breastfeeding → **8–12 oz/week** (2–3 servings) of lower-mercury "Best Choices"; children's serving size scales with age: **1 oz at ages 1–3, 2 oz at 4–7, 3 oz at 8–10, 4 oz (adult portion) at 11+**, 2 servings/week. Albacore ("white") and yellowfin tuna are "Good Choices" (≈3× the mercury of light tuna) → in pregnancy, **1 serving (4 oz)/week and no other fish that week**; canned *light* tuna is a "Best Choice" and fits the normal 2–3 servings/week.
- **Dairy (or fortified soy) has a dose, too.** The US Dietary Guidelines place dairy at **~3 cup-equivalents/day** in the 2,000-kcal pattern (1 cup-equiv = 1 cup milk or yogurt, or 1.5 oz hard cheese), with fortified soy milk/yogurt as the standard swap. The fermented forms (yogurt, kefir) carry the best observational signal; **full-fat is roughly neutral** for cardiometabolic outcomes, so choose by preference and how it fits your calorie and saturated-fat budget. **Scales with energy needs**, and it is optional rather than required — if you skip dairy, cover calcium and protein elsewhere.
- **Much of the benefit is substitution.** Legumes and fish help most when they replace red and processed meat; olive oil helps most replacing butter and margarine.
- **Some people should individualize these targets.** People on warfarin do not need to avoid vitamin-K-rich leafy greens but should keep intake *consistent* so their INR stays stable ([NIH MedlinePlus, *Warfarin*](https://medlineplus.gov/druginfo/meds/a682277.html)). Advanced chronic kidney disease often requires limiting potassium and phosphorus, which restricts many fruits, vegetables, legumes, and nuts ([National Kidney Foundation, *Potassium*](https://www.kidney.org/atoz/content/potassium)). And anyone with IBS, intestinal strictures, or recent GI surgery should raise fiber *gradually* rather than all at once.

### Practical takeaways

- Aim for **5+ servings of fruit and vegetables daily** (floor 400 g, optimum ~800 g; ~5 servings per 1,000 kcal), leaning on leafy greens, cruciferous vegetables, and berries. The first few servings deliver most of the win.
- Make **at least half your grains whole** (~3 servings/day at 2,000 kcal; scale with total grain intake): oats, brown rice, whole-wheat, barley, quinoa.
- Push fiber toward **~14 g per 1,000 kcal** (≈25–29 g/day for most adults) — this happens almost automatically if you hit the produce, whole-grain, and legume targets.
- Eat **legumes ~2.5–3 cups cooked/week** (up to ~1 cup/day if you use them as your main protein) and a **daily handful (~1 oz, ~160–200 kcal) of unsalted nuts** — half that on a tight calorie budget.
- Eat **fish twice a week (~7–8 oz total, two ~3.5–4 oz cooked servings)**, favoring oily fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel, trout), and not fried; pregnancy **8–12 oz/week** of lower-mercury choices.
- Use **extra-virgin olive oil as your default fat** (~120 kcal/tbsp), especially in place of butter, margarine, or mayonnaise.

The throughline on adjustability: these are *floors that scale with calories and goals*, not fixed daily quotas. A ~1,500-kcal weight-loss day and a ~3,000-kcal training day should both hold the same food *pattern* — roughly the same servings-per-1,000-kcal of produce, fiber, and whole grains — while protein and total volume flex (calorie math via Mifflin–St Jeor and protein math are canonical in §20; disease-specific limits in §09).

### Unsettled or debated

- **Fish versus fish-oil supplements (contested).** Dietary fish is reliably protective, but supplements are not a clean substitute. The large VITAL trial found 1 g/day of marine omega-3 did not significantly reduce major cardiovascular events (HR 0.92; 95% CI 0.80–1.06) ([Manson 2019, *NEJM*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6392053/)). High-dose prescription EPA (icosapent ethyl 4 g/day) did cut events ~25% in REDUCE-IT — but only in statin-treated, high-triglyceride patients, and it raised atrial fibrillation (5.3% vs 3.9%), with the trial's mineral-oil placebo still debated ([Bhatt 2019, *NEJM*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/)). The NIH's position is that supplements do not replace eating fish; experts genuinely disagree on their broader utility ([NIH NCCIH 2024](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/omega3-supplements-what-you-need-to-know); [NHLBI 2024](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/news/2024/omega-3s-heart-health-exploring-potential-benefits-and-risks)).
- **The optimal produce ceiling**, as above: Western cohorts point to ~800 g/day, while PURE suggests benefit plateaus closer to 400 g/day.
