## 14. Where the Science Is Genuinely Unsettled

**Bottom line:** Think in swaps, not villains — nearly every contested call (saturated fat, red meat, eggs, salt floors, seed oils) resolves into 'replace it with *what?*'; the foods everyone argues about matter far less than the consensus nobody serious disputes, so don't whipsaw on headlines.


A guide that pretended nutrition science was settled would be lying to you. The convergent core (Sections 04–06) is robust, but around its edges sit several debates that credentialed experts argue about in good faith — and that the headlines distort weekly. Understanding *why* these are unsettled is more useful than any single verdict, because it inoculates you against the next sensational study. Most of the noise comes from one underlying problem: most nutrition evidence is **observational**, and untangling the effect of one food from the entire lifestyle it travels with is genuinely hard.

### Key evidence

**Saturated fat — the diet-heart debate.** Almost nobody disputes that saturated fat raises LDL cholesterol. The fight is over what it does to *hard outcomes* and, above all, **what you replace it with**. Some meta-analyses of observational data found no clear association between saturated fat and cardiovascular disease [Siri-Tarino et al., AJCN 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2824152/). But the **replacement framework** reconciles much of it: a Cochrane review of randomized trials found that *reducing* saturated fat produced a potentially important ~17% reduction in combined cardiovascular events (relative risk 0.83; moderate-certainty evidence; no clear effect on all-cause or cardiovascular mortality), with greater reductions in saturated fat producing greater reductions in events [Hooper et al., Cochrane 2020](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD011737.pub3). (Cochrane's own subgrouping did not separate replacement with polyunsaturated fat from replacement with carbohydrate; the case that the benefit is **largest when saturated fat is replaced with polyunsaturated fat** — rather than with refined carbohydrate or sugar — rests instead on the AHA's pooled read of the randomized trials [Sacks et al., AHA Presidential Advisory, Circulation 2017](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000510).) The honest synthesis: swap butter and processed meat for olive oil, nuts, and fish — not for white bread and soda. This is exactly the substitution the PREDIMED randomized trial tested, where a Mediterranean diet enriched with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts cut major cardiovascular events versus a lower-fat control (hazard ratios ~0.69 and ~0.72; industry note: olive-oil and nut producers donated the trial foods, though the sponsors had no role in study design or analysis, and public funding predominated) [Estruch et al., NEJM 2018](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389).

**Adjust it — saturated fat ceiling by your LDL and risk.** Baseline: keep saturated fat under **10% of calories** (the 2020–2025 U.S. Dietary Guidelines ceiling; ≈22 g/day on 2,000 kcal, since 10% × 2,000 = 200 kcal ÷ 9 kcal/g) [DGA 2020–2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/sites/default/files/2020-12/DGA_2020-2025_ExecutiveSummary_English.pdf). How it scales: the ceiling is a *percentage of your own intake* — recompute as 0.10 × total kcal ÷ 9; and each **5% of energy** you shift from saturated fat to polyunsaturated fat lowers coronary heart disease risk by ≈**10%** [Sacks et al., AHA 2017](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000510). What it depends on:
  - **Elevated LDL (≥100 mg/dL without ASCVD), or established cardiovascular disease/diabetes → tighten to <6% of calories** (≈13 g/day on 2,000 kcal) — the AHA's threshold for people who need to lower LDL [AHA, Saturated Fats](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/eat-smart/fats/saturated-fats).
  - **Replacement matters more than the cap:** the same gram of saturated fat removed buys an LDL drop only if it's replaced by PUFA/MUFA or whole grains, not refined starch/sugar. A worked swap: trading ~22 g/day butter-fat for olive oil and walnuts (a ~5%-energy shift on 2,000 kcal) corresponds to ≈10% lower CHD risk.
  - **Normal LDL, no diabetes, no CVD → the 10% ceiling is enough**; chasing <6% has little marginal benefit and is mostly about *what replaces the fat*, not the number itself.

**Red meat — the NutriRECS firestorm.** In 2019, a group published guidelines in *Annals of Internal Medicine* concluding adults could continue current red- and processed-meat intake because the certainty of harm was "low" [Johnston et al., NutriRECS, Annals 2019](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M19-1621). (The guideline itself reported no industry funding, but the lead author did not disclose a prior study funded by the food-industry group ILSI, which fueled the backlash.) Leading nutrition departments publicly demanded retraction. Both sides were partly right: the *evidence quality* for unprocessed red meat truly is low (the absolute risk changes are small and observational), yet the *precautionary and environmental* case for moderation is strong. Processed meat (Section 05) is a firmer "limit."

**Adjust it — red-meat ceiling by goal and risk.** Baseline: keep *unprocessed* red meat to about **≤350–500 g cooked/week (~3 modest portions)** and processed meat as close to **zero** as is practical [WCRF/AICR](https://www.wcrf.org/research-policy/evidence-for-our-recommendations/limit-red-processed-meat/). How it scales: colorectal-cancer risk rises roughly **~18% per 50 g/day of processed meat** (and ~17% per 100 g/day of unprocessed red meat), so cuts at the high-intake end matter most [IARC, 2015](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf). What it depends on:
  - **Family history of colorectal cancer, or existing high CV risk → trend toward the low end (~≤300 g/week) and effectively no processed meat.**
  - **Iron-deficiency or high physical demand (pregnancy, heavy athletes, recovering anemia) → the lower bound is flexible upward**; lean unprocessed red meat is a dense source of heme iron, and a few extra 100 g portions/week is a defensible trade against deficiency.
  - **The number is a soft ceiling, not a hard cliff** — the unprocessed-meat evidence is low-certainty, so 1–2 portions over isn't an emergency; processed meat is where the firmer "limit" sits.

**Dietary cholesterol and eggs.** For decades, dietary cholesterol was demonized; the 2015–2020 U.S. Dietary Guidelines dropped the strict 300 mg/day cap, concluding cholesterol *in food* has a weaker effect on blood cholesterol than saturated and trans fats do for most people [Snetselaar et al., Nutrition Today 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8713704/). The American Heart Association reached a similar read, advising a heart-healthy dietary pattern rather than a strict numeric cholesterol limit [Carson et al., AHA Science Advisory, Circulation 2020](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000743). Egg studies still conflict — some large cohorts link higher egg/cholesterol intake to modestly higher cardiovascular risk (in the pooled U.S. cohorts, each additional 300 mg/day of dietary cholesterol — and, separately, each additional half-egg/day — tracked with ~17% higher CVD risk) [Zhong et al., JAMA 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2728487), while updated meta-analyses pooling more cohorts find no association up to about one egg/day — and individuals vary in how much dietary cholesterol raises their blood levels ("hyper-responders"). The defensible read: an egg a day is fine for most healthy people; those with diabetes or high cardiovascular risk may want to be more cautious — though the evidence here is mixed and largely observational, so that caution is precautionary rather than firmly established.

**Adjust it — eggs by your risk profile.** Baseline for a healthy adult: up to **1 egg/day (~7/week)** carries no detectable CVD association in pooled cohorts [Drouin-Chartier et al., BMJ 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32132002/). How it scales: in the older U.S. pooled cohorts, **each +half-egg/day was associated with ~17% higher CVD risk**, as was each +300 mg/day of dietary cholesterol (these were two separate exposures, not equivalents — one large egg holds ≈185 mg cholesterol, all in the yolk) [Zhong et al., JAMA 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2728487). What it depends on:
  - **Type 2 diabetes (established) → cap nearer ~3–4 eggs/week.** In diabetic subgroups, each additional ~4 eggs/week was associated with ~40% higher CVD risk, and each egg/day with ~13% higher incident-diabetes risk in those at risk [egg dose-response meta-analysis, EJN 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00394-020-02345-7).
  - **High CV risk / prior event / known hyper-responder → treat eggs as part of your saturated-fat and cholesterol budget**, lean toward ≤3–4 yolks/week, and use whites freely (a 2-white omelet adds ~0 mg cholesterol).
  - **Healthy, normal lipids, no diabetes → 1/day is a reasonable upper habit, not a target to hit**; the protein and choline are useful, and the signal at this level is null.

**Salt — the J-curve dispute.** That high sodium raises blood pressure and that population-level reduction lowers it is well-supported (Sections 05–06). The clearest hard-outcome RCT is the SSaSS trial, in which switching to a *potassium-enriched* salt substitute cut stroke, major cardiovascular events, and death — though this tested sodium-for-potassium substitution (so the added potassium is a co-mechanism, not pure sodium reduction) in an unusually high-risk rural-Chinese population (most had prior stroke or hypertension, with very high baseline sodium) [Neal et al., NEJM 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34459569/). One caveat: potassium-based salt substitutes can be dangerous for people with chronic kidney disease or on potassium-sparing or other potassium-affecting medications, who were excluded from the trial. The controversy is at the *low* end: some large observational studies (notably PURE) found a **J- or U-shaped curve**, with *very* low sodium also associated with higher risk [O'Donnell et al., NEJM 2014](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1311889). Critics counter that those findings are plagued by reverse causation and flawed single-spot-urine estimates. Major bodies still advise most people eat too much sodium and should cut down — the WHO recommends adults keep sodium below 2 g/day (about 5 g/day of salt), well under typical intake [WHO 2023](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/sodium-reduction); the debate is whether there's a floor below which cutting further stops helping.

**Adjust it — sodium target by your blood pressure and risk.** Baseline for most adults: aim **below 2,300 mg/day** (the AHA's everyday target and ~1 tsp salt), versus a typical U.S. intake of ~3,400 mg [AHA, How Much Sodium](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/the-facts-about-high-blood-pressure). How it scales: in DASH-Sodium, cutting sodium lowered systolic blood pressure by roughly **3–8 mm Hg per ~1,000 mg/day reduction**, with the *larger* drops in people who start with higher blood pressure [DASH-Sodium, NEJM 2001](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM200105313442212). What it depends on:
  - **Hypertensive, salt-sensitive, Black, diabetic, or age >50–51 (also CKD or prior cardiovascular event) → push toward <1,500 mg/day** (the AHA "ideal"); salt-sensitivity is present in ~51% of hypertensives vs ~26% of normotensives, so these groups get the biggest BP payoff [AHA, Salt Sensitivity](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/hyp.0000000000000047).
  - **The J-curve caveat applies mainly *below ~1,500 mg/day*** — that is where some observational data (e.g., PURE) hint at a floor, and where confidence is genuinely low; for the ~90%+ of people eating >2,300 mg, the debate is academic and the instruction is unambiguous: cut down. Don't use the floor debate as a reason to stay at 3,400 mg.
  - **Heavy sweating (athletes, hot-climate labor) → add back losses**, not subtract: sweat carries ~0.5–1.5 g sodium per liter, so 2–3 L of sweat can justify replacing ~1–4 g/day on top of baseline.
  - **Salt-substitute caution:** potassium-based substitutes lower risk in most, but are unsafe in advanced CKD (e.g., **eGFR <30 / G4–G5**) or on potassium-sparing drugs, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, or other potassium-raising medication — those groups were excluded from SSaSS.

**Seed oils and omega-6.** A vocal claim holds that industrial seed oils (high in omega-6 linoleic acid) drive inflammation and chronic disease. The weight of randomized and prospective evidence does **not** support this: higher linoleic acid intake is generally associated with *lower* cardiovascular risk, and replacing saturated fat with these oils lowers LDL [AHA Advisory, Circulation 2017](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000510). A pooled analysis of 30 cohorts found higher circulating linoleic acid biomarkers tracked with *lower* risk of cardiovascular disease and cardiovascular death [Marklund et al., Circulation 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30971107/). The legitimate kernel is that seed oils are abundant in *ultra-processed* foods — so the foods carrying them are often the problem, not the oil's fatty acids per se.

**Adjust it — judge the *vehicle*, not the omega-6.** Baseline: there is no evidence-based upper limit on linoleic acid for health; the AMDR for total omega-6 PUFA is ~**5–10% of calories** and most people sit comfortably inside it. How it scales: each 5%-energy swap of saturated fat *to* these oils lowers CHD risk ~10% (same lever as above), so the direction of adjustment is usually *more* liquid plant oil in cooking, not less. What it depends on:
  - **High ultra-processed-food intake → the fix is cutting the UPFs (target <10–15% of calories from UPFs where feasible; §05), not banning canola in your own pan.**
  - **Omega-3 balance, not omega-6 ceiling, is the lever worth pulling:** aim for ~250–500 mg/day EPA+DHA (oily fish ~2×/week) rather than trying to drive linoleic acid down.

**Low-carb vs. low-fat.** For weight loss, the best head-to-head trial (DIETFITS) found **no meaningful average difference** between a healthy low-carb and a healthy low-fat diet once calories and adherence were accounted for, concluding that dietary adherence — more than the specific macronutrient mix — predicted weight-loss success [Gardner et al., JAMA 2018](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2673150). An earlier head-to-head trial, A TO Z, did find the lowest-carb (Atkins) arm lost somewhat more weight at 12 months, but adherence was poor across all arms and the differences narrowed over time [Gardner et al., A TO Z, JAMA 2007](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/205916). Low-carb can offer short-term advantages for glycemic control in type 2 diabetes (Section 09), but long-term cardiovascular outcomes depend on *what* the carbs are replaced with — plant-based low-carb looks better than animal-based. There is no universal winner.

**Adjust it — carb level by metabolic status, not ideology.** Baseline: there is no single optimal carbohydrate fraction; the U.S. guidelines' acceptable range is **45–65% of calories from carbohydrate**, and within it adherence beats macro-ratio. How it scales: each diet in DIETFITS averaged only a ~5–6 kg 12-month loss with *no significant between-diet difference*, so the lever is the ~500 kcal/day deficit (≈0.45 kg/week; see §20), not the carb percentage. What it depends on:
  - **Type 2 diabetes or strong glycemic goal → a lower-carb pattern (commonly defined as <130 g/day, ~26% of a 2,000-kcal diet) can improve HbA1c short-term** (§09) — a ~10–15%-of-energy reduction in carbohydrate buys roughly the ~0.5% HbA1c edge cited in §09, which largely fades by ~12 months; pair it with plant proteins and unsaturated fats, since animal-heavy low-carb tracks worse for long-term CV outcomes.
  - **Endurance athlete or very active → keep carbs higher (often 5–8 g/kg/day)** to fuel training; aggressive low-carb impairs high-intensity performance.
  - **Whatever level you pick, fix the *quality* first:** whole grains, legumes, fruit, and ~14 g fiber/1,000 kcal (§04) matter more than the carb number — "healthy low-carb" and "healthy low-fat" both beat their refined-food versions.

**The limits of nutritional epidemiology (the meta-controversy).** Most diet–disease links rest on observational cohorts using **food-frequency questionnaires** (imprecise self-report), confounded by the fact that health-conscious people do many healthy things at once ("healthy-user bias"), and occasionally distorted by **industry funding**. Critics like John Ioannidis argue these limitations make many single findings unreliable and call for major reform of the field [Ioannidis, JAMA 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30422271/). This is why this guide leans hardest on the places where **randomized trials and convergent patterns agree** (Mediterranean/DASH, fiber, trans fat, sodium, sugar-sweetened beverages) and treats single sensational studies with skepticism.

### What it depends on

- **What the food replaces.** Nearly every fat and carbohydrate debate resolves into a substitution question — saturated fat *for what?*, carbs *for what?* The replacement, not the nutrient in isolation, drives the answer.
- **Your individual physiology.** Cholesterol hyper-responders, salt-sensitive individuals, and people with diabetes get different answers on eggs, salt, and carbohydrate than the average person.
- **Evidence type.** "Low-certainty" (red meat, very-low salt) means *be appropriately humble*, not "do whatever you want." Strong RCT-backed items (trans fat, replacing saturated fat with PUFA) deserve more confidence.
- **Who funded and how it was measured.** Weigh single observational studies — especially industry-funded or FFQ-based ones — far less than convergent trial evidence.

### Practical takeaways

- **Don't whipsaw on headlines.** A single study rarely overturns a convergent body of evidence; wait for replication and meta-analyses.
- **Think in swaps, not villains.** "Replace saturated fat with olive oil, nuts, and fish" is more accurate and more useful than "fat is bad" or "fat is fine."
- **The contested foods are mostly *moderation*, not *elimination*:** eggs, unprocessed red meat, and dairy can fit a healthy diet in modest amounts for most people — full-fat dairy is roughly neutral, not clearly harmful, in modest amounts (~2–3 servings/day), with fermented forms (yogurt/kefir) looking best in cohorts.
- **Trust the boring consensus.** The items everyone argues about (eggs, butter, salt floors, seed oils) matter far less than the items nobody serious argues about: eat more plants and fiber, less sugar and ultra-processed food, no trans fat, don't smoke, stay active.
- **Match the contested calls to your risk — with numbers.** Healthy adults: up to ~1 egg/day, red meat ≤350–500 g/week, sodium <2,300 mg, saturated fat <10% of calories. Higher-risk readers tighten each: diabetes → ~3–4 eggs/week and lower-carb; hypertension/CKD/salt-sensitive → sodium <1,500 mg; elevated LDL or established CVD → saturated fat <6% of calories. The per-condition deltas are in the "**Adjust it —**" boxes above.

### Unsettled or debated

This entire section *is* the unsettled territory. The most important meta-point: where this guide expresses confidence, it's because randomized trials and multiple independent lines of evidence agree; where it hedges, the underlying science genuinely hedges. New evidence that would move these debates would be large, long-term **randomized** trials with hard outcomes and objective intake measures (e.g., biomarkers instead of questionnaires) — which are expensive and rare, and why these arguments will persist.
