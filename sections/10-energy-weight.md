## 10. Changing Your Weight: Calories, Satiety, Losing, and Gaining

**Bottom line:** A sustained moderate deficit is the lever, but *what* you eat sets *how much* you eat — build meals around protein and high-volume whole foods, lift to keep muscle, lose ~0.5-1%/week, and plan for maintenance, which is harder than the loss.


Body weight obeys a law of physics. Over time, when the energy you take in exceeds the energy you burn, you store the surplus; a sustained deficit draws it back down. That is the first law of thermodynamics, and it is not in dispute. The familiar "calories in, calories out" (CICO) slogan is *true* — but as a guide to behavior it is badly incomplete, because both sides of the equation are dynamic, they push on each other, and neither is fully under conscious control.

The single most useful finding in this whole area is that *what* you eat changes *how much* you eat. Foods that are low in energy density, high in fiber, high in protein, and minimally processed promote fullness and quietly reduce intake; foods engineered for "passive overconsumption" do the opposite. So the practical question is rarely "how do I eat 500 fewer calories through willpower?" but "how do I build meals that leave me satisfied at a lower calorie cost — without thinking about it?"

For weight loss specifically, the research delivers an almost anticlimactic verdict: no macronutrient split is magic. Adherence and overall food quality dominate. What separates approaches that last from ones that fail is not the carb-to-fat ratio but whether the eating pattern preserves muscle, keeps you full, and is something you can actually live with. And the hardest phase is not losing weight — it is keeping it off, because the body adapts to defend a higher set point.

### Key evidence

**Energy balance is real, but expenditure has moving parts (strong).** Total daily energy expenditure (TDEE) breaks into four components: basal metabolic rate (BMR, ~60-70% of the total), the thermic effect of food (TEF, ~10%), non-exercise activity thermogenesis (NEAT — fidgeting, posture, spontaneous movement, ~15% but wildly variable), and exercise. NEAT alone can differ by up to ~2,000 kcal/day between individuals [Endotext 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK279077/). Because BMR dominates, changes in metabolic rate — and its *adaptation* during dieting — matter enormously. One nuance worth banking: protein carries the highest thermic effect (~20-30% of its calories spent on digestion, versus ~0-3% for fat), so its "net" calories are lower than the label implies — though a meta-analysis finds this thermic advantage is clear acutely but attenuates over longer-term diets [Guarneiri et al., Adv Nutr 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625215/).

**Ultra-processed foods drive ~500 extra kcal/day — causal evidence (strong RCT).** In a landmark NIH metabolic-ward trial, 20 weight-stable adults lived in a research unit and were randomized to two weeks of an ultra-processed diet then two weeks unprocessed (or the reverse), eating as much as they liked. The diets were matched for presented calories, energy density, sugar, fat, sodium, and fiber. On ultra-processed food, people ate ~508 kcal/day more, ate *faster*, and gained ~0.9 kg; on the unprocessed diet they lost ~0.9 kg, with higher levels of the satiety hormone PYY and lower hunger-signaling ghrelin [Hall et al., Cell Metab 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7946062/). Because it was a randomized crossover with matched macronutrients, it provides rare causal evidence that food *form and processing* — not just nutrient content — drive how much we eat.

**Diet quality and adherence beat any macro split (strong).** In the DIETFITS trial, 609 adults followed a healthy low-fat or healthy low-carb diet for 12 months — both arms taught to emphasize whole, minimally processed foods rather than count calories. Weight loss was statistically equivalent (low-fat −5.3 kg, low-carb −6.0 kg), with *no* benefit from matching the diet to a person's genotype or insulin status [Gardner et al., JAMA 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5839290/). POUNDS Lost (811 adults, 2 years, four macronutrient combinations) reached the same conclusion: ~2.9-3.6 kg lost across all arms, no significant difference [Sacks et al., NEJM 2009](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMoa0804748). The best diet is the nutritious one you can stick to.

**Energy density quietly governs intake (strong).** People tend to eat a fairly constant *weight* of food day to day, so diluting calories with water- and fiber-rich foods lets you eat satisfying volumes at lower calorie cost — the core insight of Barbara Rolls' "Volumetrics." Lower the energy density of a meal and people spontaneously eat fewer calories, with equal reported fullness and no rebound over the following days [Rolls, Physiol Behav 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4182946/); [Nutr Bull 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5687574/). The numbers are stark: most vegetables run 20-80 kcal per 100 g and leafy greens ~20-25, versus 400-550 kcal per 100 g for typical packaged snacks.

**Satiety per calorie is food-specific (moderate).** The classic Satiety Index fed 240-kcal portions of 38 foods and tracked fullness for two hours. With white bread set at 100%, boiled potatoes scored 323% — the most filling — while croissants scored 47%, the least; 76% of foods beat white bread [Holt et al., Eur J Clin Nutr 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7498104/). The study is small (11-13 people per food), so treat the exact rankings as indicative, not gospel — but the pattern is robust: protein, fiber, water, and lower energy density predict fullness.

**Protein protects muscle and satisfies (strong).** Protein is the most satiating macronutrient per calorie, and in a deficit it does something irreplaceable: it preserves lean mass. A meta-analysis of higher-protein interventions found a modest additional weight reduction of ~1.6 kg versus controls [Hansen et al., Nutrients 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8468854/). For muscle building in energy balance, benefits plateau around ~1.6 g/kg/day [Morton et al., BJSM 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/). During weight loss, needs rise — reviews suggest ~1.6–2.4 g/kg/day (up to ~2.3-3.1 g/kg of *fat-free* mass for lean, trained dieters) — and deficits larger than ~500-750 kcal/day accelerate muscle loss [Hector & Phillips, IJSNEM 2018](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/28/2/article-p170.xml). Resistance training is the strongest stimulus for keeping muscle while shedding fat: a systematic review and meta-analysis found that adding resistance exercise to a weight-loss diet produces greater fat loss (high certainty) and protects fat-free mass (moderate certainty) [Binmahfoz et al., BMJ Open Sport Exerc Med 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12406911/).

**Fiber helps, modestly (moderate).** A meta-analysis of 27 RCTs found fiber supplementation produced ~1.25 kg of additional weight loss alongside improvements in waist circumference and insulin markers [Nutrients 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9268533/), and in POUNDS Lost, higher fiber intake predicted both greater loss and better adherence [J Nutr 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6768815/). The target is ~25–38 g/day (US Dietary Guidelines: ~25 g for women, ~38 g for men); the Lancet/Reynolds meta-analysis finds the steepest risk reduction at 25–29 g/day, with more still helping. Most adults manage only ~15 g [Harvard T.H. Chan, The Nutrition Source](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/carbohydrates/fiber/). Fiber's larger value is as a marker of whole-food, low-energy-density eating.

### Losing weight: what the evidence supports

A sustained energy deficit is the necessary lever — but *how* you create it determines whether you lose fat or also burn through muscle. The well-supported playbook:

- **Set a moderate deficit (strong).** The 2013 AHA/ACC/TOS obesity guideline recommends a **500–750 kcal/day deficit** (or fixed targets of ~1,200–1,500 kcal/day for women, 1,500–1,800 for men) inside a program of diet, activity, and behavior support [Jensen et al., Circulation/JACC 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5819889/). That math produces roughly the recommended rate of **~0.5–0.75 kg (1–1.5 lb) per week ≈ 0.5–1% of body weight per week** (a ~7,700 kcal energy cost per kg of body weight is the rough conversion, though adaptive thermogenesis means real-world loss runs somewhat slower over time); a gradual, steady pace also predicts better long-term maintenance than rapid loss [CDC, Steps for Losing Weight](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-weight-growth/losing-weight/index.html). A realistic, clinically meaningful goal is **5–10% of starting weight**; even a sustained 3–5% improves glucose, triglycerides, and blood pressure (see §20 for the Mifflin–St Jeor calorie math).
  - **Adjust it —** Size the deficit by a **weekly rate**: roughly **0.5%/week of body weight (gentle) up to 1% (only if you carry higher body fat)** — this is exactly what the calculator's *Pace* control sets. The classic **−500 to −750 kcal/day** lands in the same place for a typical adult (**−500 ≈ −0.45 kg/week**, **−750 ≈ −0.68 kg/week**; ≈ 7,700 kcal/kg). Estimate maintenance as Mifflin–St Jeor BMR × a **daily-activity factor** (**1.25** desk, **1.45** light, **1.7** on-your-feet, **1.9** physical job, **2.2** heavy labor) **plus any workout calories** (see §20). Two hard rules: cap loss at **~1% of body weight/week**, and never eat below **~1,200 kcal (women) / ~1,500 (men)** — the floor wins, so the real rate just comes out gentler. What it depends on, with deltas:
    - **Higher body fat (e.g. BMI ≥30 or >25% body fat in men / >32% in women) →** you can tolerate the larger end (**−750 kcal/day**, up to ~1%/week) with little risk to lean mass.
    - **Lean / athletic (e.g. <15% body fat in men, <22% in women, or visibly muscular) →** hold to the gentler end (**−300 to −500 kcal/day, ≤0.5–0.7%/week**); deficits >~0.7%/week measurably accelerate muscle loss (see next bullet).
    - **Anchor to a floor, not just a deficit:** don't drop below **~1,200 kcal/day (women) / ~1,500 kcal/day (men)** without clinical supervision — the guideline's lower bound.
    - **Recalibrate every 2–4 weeks from your actual trend, not the formula.** If your 2–4-week average weight is falling faster than ~1%/week (or you feel flat and are losing strength), add back **~250 kcal/day**; if it has stalled for ≥3 weeks despite adherence, subtract another **~250 kcal/day** (metabolic adaptation, not a broken metabolism — see "Unsettled").
    - *Worked example:* a 90 kg man with TDEE ≈ 2,700 kcal eats **~2,000 kcal/day** (−700), targeting ~0.6 kg/week; after a 3-week stall at 84 kg he recomputes TDEE (now ≈ 2,550) and trims to **~1,900 kcal/day** rather than crashing lower.
- **Don't rush it (moderate).** Faster loss costs more lean tissue. In elite athletes (all lifting, all eating adequate protein), losing at **0.7%/week added lean mass (+2.1%)** while losing at **1.4%/week did not (−0.2%)**, despite reaching the same target [Garthe et al., IJSNEM 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/). Rapid loss also drives **gallstones**: risk climbs steeply once loss exceeds **~1.5 kg/week**, with new stones in **~10–12% of people after 8–16 weeks on a low-calorie diet and >30% within 12–18 months of gastric-bypass surgery** (about a third symptomatic) [Stokes et al., meta-analysis (DARE/NCBI) 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK231690/).
  - **Adjust it —** Baseline cap: **≤1% of body weight/week** (the muscle-sparing ceiling); hard ceiling **≤1.5 kg/week** before gallstone risk rises sharply. How it scales: each step up in weekly loss rate trades fat-sparing for lean-sparing — at **0.7%/week** lean mass is preserved or gained, at **~1.4%/week** it is not. What it depends on:
    - **Lean / trained →** stay at **~0.5–0.7%/week** (the rate at which Garthe's athletes *gained* lean mass).
    - **Higher body fat (BMI ≥30) →** **up to ~1%/week** is usually safe for lean mass, but still keep absolute loss **<1.5 kg/week** to limit gallstones.
    - **Very-low-calorie diet or post-bariatric (loss may exceed 1.5 kg/week) →** ask a clinician about **prophylactic ursodeoxycholic acid (clinician-set, typically ~500–600 mg/day for ~6 months — post-bariatric this cuts gallstone incidence from ~38% to ~8%)** and keeping at least **~10 g of dietary fat** in the day (a near-zero-fat VLCD is an independent risk factor).
- **Protect muscle with protein + lifting (strong).** In a deficit, push protein to roughly **1.6–2.4 g/kg/day** (up to ~2.3–3.1 g/kg of *fat-free* mass for lean, trained dieters) and resistance-train — the combination preserves fat-free mass while maximizing fat loss [Jäger et al., JISSN 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/); [Morton et al., BJSM 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/). Lifting is the indispensable co-requisite, not an optional extra (see §02 on protein targets).
  - **Adjust it —** Baseline in a deficit: **~1.6 g/kg/day**, rising toward **~2.4 g/kg/day** as the deficit gets steeper or you get leaner. How it scales: spread it across **3–5 meals** (a daily total split is more reliable than one number) at roughly **~0.24 g/kg/meal for young adults, ~0.4 g/kg/meal (≈25–30 g) for adults >65** to clear the per-meal muscle-synthesis threshold; bias to the **fat-free-mass** basis (**~2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM**) if you're already lean and lifting (canonical per-meal and per-FFM math in §02/§20). What it depends on, with deltas:
    - **Steeper deficit (≥0.75%/week) or lean/trained (<15% BF men, <22% women) → 2.0–2.4 g/kg/day** (upper end).
    - **Higher body fat (BMI ≥30) →** **~1.6 g/kg/day** of *target* (not current) weight is enough; using actual weight overshoots needs.
    - **Age >65 (anabolic resistance) → +~0.2 g/kg/day** and ensure ≥**0.4 g/kg per meal** to clear the higher leucine threshold older muscle needs.
    - **Chronic kidney disease (not on dialysis) → restrict, don't push:** roughly **~0.8 g/kg/day, avoiding >1.3 g/kg/day** if at risk of progression (KDIGO 2024); some metabolically stable patients go lower (**0.55–0.60 g/kg/day**, KDOQI 2020) only under dietitian oversight — these high-protein numbers do *not* apply. Set intake with a clinician [KDOQI 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9303594/) (full disease-specific CKD targets in §09).
- **Macro split barely matters; adherence does (strong).** Low-carb and low-fat diets land within ~1 kg of each other in meta-analyses of 53 trials and 68,128 adults [Tobias et al., Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2015](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landia/article/PIIS2213-8587(15)00367-8/abstract). Pick the pattern you can actually keep.
- **GLP-1 drugs work, but watch lean mass (strong, evolving).** Semaglutide produced **~15% weight loss** at 68 weeks (STEP 1) versus ~2.4% on placebo, and tirzepatide **~21–22%** at 72 weeks (SURMOUNT-1) — far beyond what diet alone reliably delivers [Wilding et al., NEJM 2021](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2032183) (industry-funded: Novo Nordisk); [Jastreboff et al., SURMOUNT-1, NEJM 2022](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMoa2206038) (industry-funded: Eli Lilly). The catch: in the STEP 1 DXA substudy **~40% of the weight lost was lean (fat-free) mass** (10.4 kg fat vs 6.9 kg lean — a ~60/40 split), unless protein and resistance training are deliberately layered in [Wilding et al., DXA substudy, Diabetes Obes Metab 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8089287/) (industry-funded: Novo Nordisk); [Look et al., SURMOUNT-1 body-composition, Diabetes Obes Metab 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11965027/) (industry-funded: Eli Lilly) — broadly similar to the fat-to-lean ratio seen with lifestyle or surgical loss, though this compares across different trials, not head-to-head, and at much larger absolute scale.
  - **Adjust it —** Drug choice and **dose titration are clinician-set** (e.g. the standard slow dose escalation to limit GI side effects) — do not self-adjust. What *you* control is the muscle-defense layered on top, and it scales with how fast the drug is driving loss:
    - **Baseline:** protein **~1.6–2.4 g/kg/day** + resistance training **≥2×/week** throughout treatment (same targets as any deficit; appetite suppression makes hitting protein the hard part — prioritize it at every meal).
    - **Rapid loss (>1%/week on the drug) or already lean →** push protein to the **upper end (~2.0–2.4 g/kg/day)** and don't skip lifting; this is when lean-mass loss accelerates most.
    - **Appetite so suppressed you're under ~0.8 g/kg/day →** flag it to your clinician (consider protein-forward small meals or a shake delivering ~0.4 g/kg) rather than accepting chronic underfueling.
- **Plan for maintenance, the hard part (strong).** More than ~80% of dieters regain. The minority who succeed long-term — tracked by the **National Weight Control Registry** — share a few unglamorous habits: high physical activity (~1 h/day, ~2,800 kcal/week), frequent self-weighing, eating breakfast, and a consistent, lower-calorie pattern across weekdays and weekends [Wing & Hill, Annu Rev Nutr 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11375440/); [Catenacci et al., Obesity 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4560526/).
  - **Adjust it —** At your goal weight, recompute TDEE (Mifflin–St Jeor × activity factor, §20) and eat to that — but **expect maintenance calories to run ~5–15% (≈200–400 kcal/day) below the formula's prediction** because of metabolic adaptation after a ~10% loss (see "Unsettled"). Activity target scales by body size: the NWCR ~**2,800 kcal/week** is roughly **~60 min/day of brisk walking** for a ~70 kg person but only ~45 min for a ~90 kg person (heavier bodies burn more per minute). Self-weigh **≥1×/week** (daily users regain least) and act on a **≥2–3 kg** rebound with a small re-cut rather than waiting for a large one.

**Safety note.** Aggressive crash diets, the lean-mass loss above, weight cycling, and the slide into disordered eating are real harms. Nearly all of this evidence comes from adults with overweight or obesity; applied to someone of normal or low weight — or to anyone with a history of or vulnerability to an eating disorder — the same advice can do damage. If that's you, skip calorie counting and work with a clinician.

### Gaining weight and building muscle: what the evidence supports

Most people asking how to "gain weight" mean *gain muscle, not fat* — which means a deliberate surplus paired with hard training, not just eating more.

- **Run a small surplus (moderate).** Building muscle requires modestly more energy than you burn, but bigger is not better: surpluses **>~500 kcal/day add fat faster than muscle**, because the rate of muscle accretion is biologically capped. The best-supported "lean-gain" range is a **~250–500 kcal/day surplus** (roughly **~10–20% above maintenance**), producing **~0.25–0.5% of body weight per week** (≈0.25–0.5 kg/week for a 100 kg person) [Slater et al., Front Nutr 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320/); [Iraki et al., Sports (Basel) 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/). A randomized trial confirms the surplus does drive protein accretion, but excess spills into fat once muscle-building is saturated [Clin Nutr 2024, randomized trial](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0261561424003467).
  - **Adjust it —** Baseline: **+250–500 kcal/day** above TDEE (§20), targeting **+0.25–0.5%/week**. Because it's a *percentage*, the absolute kcal scales with size and training age — set it by your **weekly weight trend**, not the fixed number, and recompute every **2–4 weeks**:
    - **Novice / younger / returning after a break →** upper end (**~+0.5%/week, ~10–20% surplus**); newcomers genuinely build muscle fast enough to use it.
    - **Advanced / many years trained →** lower end (**~+0.25%/week, ~5–10% surplus**); the muscle-accretion ceiling means more food just adds fat.
    - **Gaining faster than ~0.5%/week (or waist/skinfolds climbing) → cut the surplus ~250 kcal/day**; gaining slower than ~0.25%/week with hard training → **add ~250 kcal/day**.
    - *Worked example:* an 80 kg intermediate with TDEE ≈ 2,800 eats **~3,100 kcal/day** (+300, ~+11%), aiming ~0.3 kg/week; if the scale jumps 0.8 kg in a week he holds, not adds.
- **Protein is the build material (strong).** Fat-free-mass gains from training plateau near **~1.6 g/kg/day** (95% CI up to ~2.2) in the meta-analysis of 49 studies and 1,863 people [Morton et al., BJSM 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/); the ISSN endorses **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day** for general trainees, with little added benefit above ~2.2 in a surplus [Jäger et al., JISSN 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/). Spread it across 3–5 meals of ~20–40 g (see §02).
  - **Adjust it —** Baseline in a *surplus*: **~1.6 g/kg/day** is enough — extra protein beyond this point doesn't add muscle when calories are already adequate (unlike a deficit, which needs more). How it scales: **3–5 meals of ~20–40 g**, i.e. **~0.24 g/kg/meal (young) to ~0.4 g/kg/meal (>65)**, every 3–5 h. What it depends on:
    - **Lean-mass build is the goal and you're already lean → 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day** of the upper band buys a small insurance margin.
    - **Higher body fat → base the number on *target/lean* weight, not total**, or you'll overshoot.
    - **Plant-forward diet → +~0.2–0.3 g/kg/day** and emphasize leucine-rich or blended sources, since plant proteins are lower in leucine and less digestible (see §02).
    - **Above ~2.2 g/kg/day in a surplus → no added muscle**; the calories are better spent on carbs to fuel training.
- **Resistance training is the actual signal (strong).** Calories and protein are permissive; **mechanical tension from progressive resistance training is what tells the body to put the surplus into muscle rather than fat** [Morton et al., BJSM 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/). Without it, a surplus is simply weight gain. Newer and younger lifters gain fastest; trained lifters gain slowly and should lean toward the *lower* end of the surplus.
- **Creatine and timing are minor accelerators (moderate).** Creatine monohydrate is the most evidence-backed legal supplement for added strength and lean mass when combined with training (see §12), and total daily protein matters far more than the exact pre/post-workout window (see §11).

**Safety note.** Two failure modes. First, the **"dirty bulk"** — eating in a large, junk-heavy surplus — buys mostly fat plus worse blood lipids, not extra muscle; keep the surplus small and the food quality high. Second, genuine **clinical underweight (BMI <18.5)** or age-related muscle loss (**sarcopenia**) is a medical situation, not a physique goal: unintentional weight loss, frailty, or an inability to gain despite eating well warrants a clinician's workup, since it can flag an underlying condition rather than a calorie shortfall.

### What it depends on

- **Your goal.** For *losing* weight, diet quality and protein matter most; for *maintaining* it, behavior, activity, and managing metabolic adaptation matter most.
- **How lean you already are.** Leaner dieters (e.g. <15% body fat in men, <22% in women) need more protein (toward **2.0–2.4 g/kg/day**) and gentler deficits (**≤0.5–0.7%/week**) to protect muscle; people with more fat to lose (BMI ≥30) can tolerate larger deficits (**up to ~1%/week, −750 kcal/day**) with less risk to lean mass.
- **Deficit size.** Aggressive cuts (**>~750 kcal/day, or any loss >~1%/week**) lose more muscle and are harder to sustain; slower loss (**~0.5–0.7%/week**) tends to preserve more lean tissue. Recalibrate the deficit from your **2–4-week weight trend**, not the formula.
- **Activity level.** Exercise is a weak lever for *losing* weight but one of the best predictors of *keeping it off*: successful long-term maintainers in the National Weight Control Registry sustain high, objectively measured levels of physical activity [Catenacci et al., Obesity 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4560526/) (see below).
- **Your relationship with food.** For anyone with a history of disordered eating, calorie counting can do more harm than good; food-quality and satiety framing is safer.

### Practical takeaways

- **Build meals around volume and protein.** A palm or two of protein plus a generous base of vegetables fills you up at a low calorie cost — the satiety wins compound automatically.
- **Default to minimally processed food.** This alone is worth ~500 kcal/day of unforced calorie reduction, per the strongest trial we have — though that figure comes from one small, tightly controlled metabolic-ward trial (n=20, 2 weeks): a real but ward-measured effect.
- **Get enough protein:** roughly 1.6 g/kg/day at maintenance, more when dieting, to defend muscle and curb hunger. **Adjust it —** baseline **1.6 g/kg/day**; **+0.0–0.8 g/kg in a deficit (→ up to 2.4)**, **+~0.2 g/kg if >65**, **+~0.2–0.3 g/kg if plant-forward**, but **~0.8 g/kg, clinician-set, if you have non-dialysis CKD** (KDIGO 2024; avoid >1.3 g/kg) — these higher numbers assume normal kidney function and come mostly from healthy, often trained people [KDOQI 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9303594/) (see §02/§09).
- **Lift weights.** Resistance training is the single best way to keep muscle while losing fat, and a top predictor of long-term maintenance.
- **Hit ~25–38 g/day of fiber** (US Dietary Guidelines: ~25 g for women, ~38 g for men); the Lancet/Reynolds meta-analysis finds the steepest risk reduction at 25–29 g/day, with more still helping. **Adjust it —** the underlying IOM rule is **~14 g per 1,000 kcal eaten**, so the number tracks your intake: a 1,600-kcal diet → **~22 g**, a 2,800-kcal diet → **~39 g**. Modifiers: **ramp up by only ~5 g/day per week** (jumping there suddenly causes gas, bloating, cramping); **drink enough fluid** as you add it; if you have **IBS or are flaring with IBD**, a clinician/dietitian may temporarily cap or reshape fiber (e.g. low-FODMAP) rather than push to target. Source it from whole plant foods — a marker for the whole pattern, not just a number [Harvard T.H. Chan, The Nutrition Source](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/carbohydrates/fiber/).
- **Don't out-train a poor diet, but do stay active.** Movement matters far more for keeping weight off than for taking it off.
- **Plan for maintenance as its own phase.** Expect it to feel harder than the loss itself, and treat slow, sustainable change as the win.
- **On a GLP-1 drug, defend your muscle deliberately.** These medications work, but up to ~40% of the weight lost can be lean mass; **dose titration is clinician-set — don't self-adjust.** What you control: keep protein high (**~1.6–2.4 g/kg/day**, biased to the upper end if loss exceeds ~1%/week) and resistance-train **≥2×/week** throughout to keep the loss mostly fat [Wilding et al., Diabetes Obes Metab 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8089287/) (industry-funded: Novo Nordisk).

### Unsettled or debated

- **Does metabolic adaptation permanently sabotage maintenance? (genuinely debated.)** The famous "Biggest Loser" follow-up found resting metabolic rate still ~700 kcal/day below baseline six years on — though much of that raw drop is expected from carrying less weight; the *metabolic-adaptation* component (the part beyond what body composition and age predict) was ~500 kcal/day [Fothergill et al., Obesity 2016](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/oby.21538) — and controlled studies show a 10% weight loss lowers expenditure ~300-400 kcal/day below predicted, partly via falling leptin [Rosenbaum & Leibel, Int J Obes 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3673773/). But a 2020 analysis argues this adaptation largely *fades* once the body returns to weight stability — the measured adaptation roughly halved after weight stabilization and did not predict 1-year regain — making it more a feature of active dieting than a permanent barrier [Martins et al., AJCN 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7657334/). Either way, recidivism to prior weight exceeds ~80%.
- **Do GLP-1 drugs cost too much lean mass? (open question.)** Semaglutide and tirzepatide produce loss far beyond diet alone (~15% and ~21-22%), but DXA substudies show roughly a quarter to 40% of that loss is fat-free mass — about the same fat-to-lean ratio as lifestyle or surgical loss, only at much larger absolute scale [Wilding et al., Diabetes Obes Metab 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8089287/) (industry-funded: Novo Nordisk); [Look et al., Diabetes Obes Metab 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11965027/) (industry-funded: Eli Lilly). Whether this lean-mass loss is clinically harmful, and how well protein-plus-resistance-training protocols offset it, is still being worked out.
- **How much does exercise really add to expenditure? (contested.)** Pontzer's "constrained" model — from research on the highly active Hadza, whose TDEE resembles sedentary Westerners' — suggests the body partially compensates for activity by trimming energy elsewhere [Pontzer, Physiology 2018](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/physiol.00027.2018). Many researchers favor a *partially* additive view: compensation is real but incomplete. The practical upshot holds regardless — you can't reliably out-exercise your fork for fat loss, but exercise is essential for maintenance, muscle, and health independent of weight.
